# 04. Periodization and progression

Last updated: 2026-10-21

**Revision 2026-10-21.** This chapter was revised against the primary-source verification addenda dated 2026-10-21 in `research/periodization-and-progression.md`, `research/splits-and-named-programs.md` and `research/flexible-frequency-programming.md` (each headed "Addendum 2026-10-21: primary-source verification"). It explains the options and the evidence. **The rules this program actually uses for each main lift are in `templates/progression-tracks.md`; this chapter does not restate them.** How a mesocycle is counted when the week length varies is owned by `guide/03-flexible-scheduling.md`.

## 1. Periodization models

### What the terms mean

| Model | Definition | Typical structure (convention) |
|---|---|---|
| Non-periodized | Same sets, reps, and relative load throughout; only the weight changes. | Linear progression programs are "non-periodized" in the research sense. |
| Linear (LP) | Reps fall and intensity rises across a block. | 4-6 weeks from ~65-75% toward 85-95%. |
| Undulating (DUP daily / WUP weekly) | Reps and intensity vary session to session or week to week. | Heavy / medium / light days; or a 10s week, 5s week, 3s week. |
| Block (Issurin-style) | Accumulation (longest), transmutation/intensification, realization (shortest). | Secondary sources: accumulation 2-6 weeks, transmutation up to ~4, realization 1-2; one powerlifting example 5 / 4 / 3; a Bondarchuk-method summary wants transmutation ~3 and realization at most 2. Sources disagree. Issurin's own reviews (2008, 2010, 2016; older or borderline) are paywalled and their abstracts give no lengths, so these ranges stay secondary **(unverified)**. |
| Conjugate (Westside) | Max-effort lifts rotate every 1-3 weeks; dynamic-effort (DE) work is lifted for speed in 3-week waves. | Westside Barbell's 2024 staff guide: lower body 12x2 / 10x2 / 8x2 (or 5x5) at **50 / 55 / 60% bar weight**, upper body 9x3 at **45 / 50 / 55% bar weight**, each plus **~25% band or chain tension** (about 70-85% at lockout). Simmons' own article gives no percentages: it gives reps (1 for pulls, 2 for squats, 3 for bench), says bands or chains "must be used," and treats DE as a replacement for a second max-effort day. Program-author convention, not evidence (PP add.). Critics argue the constant variation costs specificity for raw lifters. |

### Evidence

**Periodized vs non-periodized**

| Study | Finding | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| [Evidence] Williams et al. 2017 (Sports Med 47:2083-2100; 81 effects, 18 studies 1988-2015, 612 participants) | 1RM gains larger with periodization, ES = 0.43 (95% CI 0.27-0.58); **0.38 (0.09-0.66)** after adjusting for effects nested within studies; Egger's test suggested publication bias (P = 0.006). Removing 14 outlying effects from 5 studies gave **ES 0.23 (95% CI 0.13-0.33)**, with no remaining heterogeneity; this is Williams et al.'s own sensitivity analysis. Training status moderated the effect **in favour of untrained** participants (b = -0.59, P = 0.031: "untrained individuals experienced greater increases in 1RM than trained individuals"); effects from 5 studies that did not report training status were coded as untrained. Strength outcome. [PDF](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Comparison-of-Periodized-and-Non-Periodized-Resistance.pdf); [PubMed 28497285](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28497285/) | Moderate |
| [Analysis] Stronger By Science (Nuckols, "Research Spotlight: Effects of periodization on strength gains," 2020) | Reports Williams' 0.23 estimate (it is not an SBS re-analysis). SBS commentary: periodized groups reach higher peak loads, so the effect may be peak intensity rather than sequencing; the only pair of studies matching volume, average, and peak intensity (Antretter) found no advantage. The Antretter point was not checked against those papers. [SBS](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=52473) | Commentary |
| [Evidence] Moesgaard et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:1647-1666; 35 studies, **volume-equated**) | 1RM ES = 0.31 (95% CI 0.04-0.57) favouring periodization. Hypertrophy ES = 0.13 (95% CI -0.10 to 0.36), P = 0.27: **no greater hypertrophy**. The abstract reports no training-status subgroup for this comparison; whether the paywalled full text has one is **(unverified)**, as is a summary's description of the sample mix. [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/) | Moderate-high |

**Linear vs undulating**

| Study | Finding | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| [Evidence] Moesgaard et al. 2022 (Sports Med; volume-equated) | Undulating beat linear for 1RM: ES 0.31 (95% CI 0.02-0.61), P = 0.04. **Trained** ES 0.61 (0.00-1.22), P = 0.05; **untrained** ES 0.06 (-0.20 to 0.31). Hypertrophy LP vs UP: ES 0.05 (-0.20 to 0.29). The trained-only advantage applies to this comparison. [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/) | Both |
| [Evidence] Harries, Lubans & Callister 2015 (older; JSCR 29(4):1113-25; 17 studies, 510 participants) | No difference between linear and undulating for upper- or lower-body strength; short durations and prior training flagged as confounders. [PubMed 25268290](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25268290/) | Strength |
| [Evidence] Grgic et al. 2017 (PeerJ 5:e3695; 13 studies) | LP vs DUP hypertrophy: d = -0.02 (95% CI -0.25 to 0.21), p = 0.848. No difference. [PeerJ 3695](https://peerj.com/articles/3695) | Hypertrophy |
| [Evidence] Caldas et al. 2016 (Int J Sports Sci 6(6):219-229; 25 studies; lower-profile journal, not MEDLINE-indexed) | Undulating beat traditional for 1RM, ES = 0.22 (95% CI 0.07-0.38); hypertrophy ES 0.32 (CI -0.07 to 0.71), not significant, from only 6 effect sizes. [Coventry repository](https://pureportal.coventry.ac.uk/en/publications/traditional-vs-undulating-periodization-in-the-context-of-muscula/) | Both |
| [Evidence] Zhang Z et al. 2026 (Front Public Health; 29 studies, searches to July 2025) | LP and UP comparable for athletic capacity and body composition overall. Mixed, health-oriented populations; does not overturn Moesgaard's trained-lifter strength subgroup. [PubMed 41869632](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41869632/) | Both |

Count: of the three meta-analyses with a strength outcome for this comparison, two favour undulating (Moesgaard, Caldas) and one is null (Harries). Zhang 2026 pools broader "athletic capacity" in mixed populations and finds no difference.

**Block vs DUP in athletes**

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| [Evidence] Gavanda et al. 2019 (IJSPP 14:814-821; 47 adolescent football players, 12 wk) | Both improved mass, strength, jump, sprint; no between-group difference. Mean training experience was only 0.93 years, so this is a near-novice sample, not a trained one. |
| [Evidence] Painter et al. 2012 (older; IJSPP 7:161-9; 31 D-I track athletes, 10 wk) | Trends favoured block for strength and RFD, not significant; block had better gains per unit of volume (a derived efficiency metric). |
| [Evidence] Sabido et al. 2018 (Kinesiology 50(1); 15 handball players, 8 wk, matched) | Both improved squat (DUP +33.3%, block +17.4%) and triple jump; DUP better for throwing velocity (ES 0.96). Very small sample. |

### What it means

- **Some periodization beats none for 1RM by a small margin**, in both meta-analyses: ES 0.31 when volume is equated (Moesgaard); 0.43 unadjusted, 0.38 nested-adjusted, 0.23 without outliers (Williams). The benefit is **not** limited to trained lifters; Williams' moderator found it larger in the untrained. It makes **no detectable difference for hypertrophy** (Moesgaard ES 0.13, n.s.). SBS argues the mechanism is reaching heavier peak loads rather than the sequence itself (commentary, not tested here).
- **Linear vs undulating vs block is mostly preference and schedule, with one qualification.** For strength in trained lifters, undulating has a small, imprecise edge over linear in 2 of 3 meta-analyses (Moesgaard trained ES 0.61, CI lower bound 0.00; Caldas ES 0.22; Harries null). For hypertrophy there is no difference between styles. Block vs DUP has not been pooled in any meta-analysis.
- Daily undulation (heavy / medium / light exposures) suits a lifter who touches each lift often in a short week, and it is the style with the most support for an intermediate's strength; the program's heavy-plus-volume week in `templates/progression-tracks.md` is already a daily-undulating structure. Block suits a lifter peaking for a date. Conjugate's exercise rotation is a poor match for an intermediate who needs practice on the competition lifts (coaching opinion, consistent with specificity).
- **By available days (PP Q1 inference):** on 3 days a week, undulate within the week (each main lift gets its heavy and its volume exposure). On 4-6 days, the same undulation spreads across more sessions; weekly undulation or blocks are equally defensible. None of the comparisons above tests days per week directly.
- Gaps: no meta-analysis pools block vs DUP; Issurin's own block durations and Moesgaard's full subgroup tables are paywalled (PP add., "Still unverified").

## 2. Progression schemes with exact rules

Controlled evidence exists for three progression questions: RPE vs percentage loading, velocity-loss thresholds, and adding reps vs adding load (section 2.8). Everything else below is **[Convention]**: program-author rules that work in practice but have not been tested against each other. The operating rule is: pick one scheme per lift, apply its stall rule literally, and change schemes only after the stall rule has fired.

### 2.1 Linear progression (add load every session)

| Program | Increment | Stall trigger | Reset |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starting Strength | +5 lb squat, bench, press; +10 lb deadlift while it lasts. **(unverified):** startingstrength.com was unreachable; one search snippet of the official page says most young men can add 10 lb to the squat for the first few sessions, which conflicts. | Fail all reps at the same weight 3 sessions in a row | Drop 5-10% ("The Reset: Why and How"), rebuild | 
| StrongLifts 5x5 | **+5 lb (2.5 kg) per workout on squat, bench, overhead press and row**; men add **+10 lb (about 5 kg) per workout on the deadlift until it gets hard**, then +5 lb; women do better with 2.5 lb jumps on bench and press. 5 kg and 10 lb are the same practical increment, so there is no source conflict. [StrongLifts, Progress](https://stronglifts.com/stronglifts-5x5/progress/) | 3 failed workouts at a weight | About -10% on that lift only (the app does it automatically). Current official plateau advice: microload, add weight every 3 workouts instead of every workout, top/back-off sets, fewer squat sessions, or move to an intermediate program. |
| Greyskull LP | +2.5 lb upper, +5 lb lower per session; top set 1x5+ | Fewer than 5 reps on the top set | -10% next session. No author page found **(unverified)**. |
| r/Fitness BBR | +2.5 lb upper, +5 lb lower per session; +5/10 lb if the AMRAP exceeds 10 | Fewer than 15 total reps across the sets | -10% |
| Kg beginner guide | Bench and press 1.25-2.5 kg per session | | |

Stronger By Science's novice fix: when a rep scheme stalls, deload 10-15% and rebuild through progressively lower reps with more sets (4x8 to 6x5 to 6x3); if the 8-rep day stalls before the 5- or 3-rep day, drop ~15%, add a set to the stalled lift, and resume. [SBS](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/making-your-novice-strength-training-routine-more-effective-two-quick-tips/)

Use linear progression only while it works. Switch to weekly or cycle-based progression when per-session jumps fail twice in a row on a lift (PP Q2 inference). Per-session progression ties the rate of progress to the number of sessions: at 3 days a week a lift gets fewer jumps per calendar week than at 5-6 days, which is fine, because the rule is per exposure, not per week.

### 2.2 Double progression (reps, then load)

- **Standard:** fix a load. Add reps each session until every working set hits the top of the range (for example 8-12) with good form. Then increase load and drop back to the bottom of the range. Start with a load you can lift for at least the bottom of the range with a couple of reps in reserve.
- **Dynamic (Helms, Muscle & Strength Pyramid):** each set is independent; increase load on a specific set next week only if that set exceeded the range.
- **Evidence:** two RCTs found adding reps at a fixed load about as effective as adding load at fixed reps for strength and hypertrophy over 8-10 weeks (Plotkin 2022, trained; Chaves 2024, untrained; section 2.8). This supports double progression; it does not test double against triple.
- Best fit: accessories and hypertrophy work at 8-15 reps. This is the program's default for accessories (design spine item 5).

### 2.3 Triple progression (reps, then sets, then load)

- Add reps to the top of the range; then add a set; then add load and drop sets and reps back. The Pyramid variant requires exceeding the rep range by 3-4 quality reps before adding load.
- Best fit: 5-6-day lifters with room to add sets. In this program, the extra set is the first thing a short week removes, so triple progression is only sensible on accessories a lifter trains in every week type. Double vs triple progression is untested.

### 2.4 Wave and percentage schemes

**5/3/1 (Wendler)** [Convention]

- Training max (TM): 90% of true 1RM in the original book; 85% in the later "hardgainers" guidance; stronger lifters do better with a lower TM. Check: if you cannot get at least 5 reps at 95% of TM, the TM is too high.
- Weeks: 65/75/85% x 5/5/5+; 70/80/90% x 3/3/3+; 75/85/95% x 5/3/1+; deload 40/50/60% x 5. "+" is an AMRAP, not to grinding failure. Confirmed in Wendler's 2009 article (older), which states "three or four days a week."
- Progression: after each cycle, +5 lb to bench and press TM, +10 lb to squat and deadlift TM. The increase is to the TM, not the 1RM. "If any of your reps fail to adhere to these standards during your training cycle, YOU DO NOT INCREASE YOUR TRAINING MAX." No re-max needed.
- Stall: when AMRAPs stop beating prior cycles, reset that lift's TM to ~90% of its current value and rebuild. A 10% TM cut was **not found** as Wendler's own rule on jimwendler.com **(unverified)**.
- **"5 forward, 3 back":** three cycles up, then the TM drops by three increments. This is forum moderator **Kevin Deweese's** description, quoted in "The Training Max: What You Need to Know" (jimwendler.com, 2016). It is not Wendler's own wording. [jimwendler.com Training Max](https://www.jimwendler.com/blogs/jimwendler-com/101082310-the-training-max-what-you-need-to-know)
- *Forever* (paid book, no authorized eBook): leader blocks (two 3-week waves of volume work) then anchor blocks (one 3-week wave of intensity work); a TM test replaces the deload: 70/80/90% x 5 then 100% of TM for 3-5 reps; miss and you lower the TM. Reader-transcribed; the leader/anchor details and the exact test scheme were **not confirmed** from Wendler's text (book only). [JimWendler.com hardgainers](https://www.jimwendler.com/blogs/jimwendler-com/5-3-1-for-hardgainers)

**Juggernaut Method (Smith)** [Convention]: four 4-week waves (10s, 8s, 5s, 3s), each accumulation, intensification, realization, deload; reps beaten on the realization AMRAP bump the TM; exact formula **(unverified)**.

**Texas Method (weekly percentage progression)** [Convention]: Monday volume 5x5 at ~90% of Friday's 5RM (about 10% below; Rippetoe's article seen only as a snippet), Wednesday light at ~80% of Monday (confirmed by Barbell Logic), Friday a new 1x5 PR. ~5 lb per week on squat and deadlift; bench and press about every two weeks because they alternate. When Friday stalls, reduce Monday volume rather than forcing the load.

**Hybrid for this program:** percentage targets with an RPE cap, or back-offs set as a percentage of the day's estimated 1RM, capture most of both approaches (PP Q2 inference). The chosen form is in `templates/progression-tracks.md`.

### 2.5 Rep-max and rep-scheme ladders

**GZCL / GZCLP (Lefever)** [Convention]

- Tiers: T1 main compound 85-100%, ~10-15 total reps per workout (1-3 per set); T2 65-85%, 20-30 total reps (5-8 per set); T3 accessories at or under 65%, 30+ total reps (GZCLP table: 45+); tier volume about 1:2:3 T1:T2:T3 reps. "Soft" gradients. Lefever's 2012 post (older) defines the percentages against a **goal weight**; training maxes appear in his 2016 post. The original states 1:2:3 as tier volume without calling it weekly; the weekly framing is from secondary sites.
- GZCLP T1 ladder (author-partnered Boostcamp page): start at 85% of a 5RM, 5x3+, +5 lb (2.5 kg) bench and press / +10 lb (5 kg) squat and deadlift per completed session; on a miss keep the weight and move to 6x2+, then 10x1+; after 10x1+ fails, rest 2-3 days, test a new 5RM and restart 5x3 at **85%** of it. T2 ladder: 3x10, 3x8, 3x6; after 3x6 fails, back to 3x10 about **20 lb (10 kg)** above the last 3x10 weight (thefitness.wiki: +15-20 lb). T3: 3x15 with the last set AMRAP; add the smallest increment at 25 reps; if 3x15 fails, lower 10-15%. The earlier mirror conflict is resolved in favour of this version (SNP add., row 27).

**nSuns** [Convention]: progression keyed to reps on the T1 "1+" set each week: 0-1 reps no increase; 2-3 reps +5 lb; 4-5 reps +5-10 lb; 6+ reps +10-15 lb (Lift Vault; the original spreadsheet was not reachable); T2 +5 lb upper / +10 lb lower; stuck at 1 rep or failing, reduce the training 1RM by 10%.

### 2.6 RPE / RIR-based loading

- **Scale:** RPE 10 = 0 reps in reserve, 9 = 1 RIR, 8 = 2 RIR (Zourdos, Helms et al. 2016, JSCR 30:267-75; `guide/01-training-principles.md`, section 5). This strict 1 RPE = 1 RIR mapping is Zourdos'. Tuchscherer's original scale defined RPE 8 as "2-4 reps left" and RPE 7 as "speed weight," so older RTS material is looser.
- **Load selection:** choose the load that produces the target RPE for the prescribed reps today. Percentages become guidance, not prescription.
- **Reactive Training Systems (Tuchscherer)** [Convention]: work up to a top set at RPE 9-10, drop the load by a fatigue percentage, and repeat sets until the reduced load also reaches the target RPE. Example: 300x3 @9, then sets at 285x3 until that is a 9 = 5% fatigue. Tiers: low ~3% or less, medium ~5%, high ~7% or more (the "5% is average" figure was not re-checked). **RPE-to-%1RM chart** (values consistent across reproductions; Tuchscherer's original chart image was not on the source page): **4 reps @ RPE 8 = 83.7% 1RM**; 5 @ 10 = 86.3%; 6 @ 10 = 83.7%; 1 @ 8 = 92.2%; **8 @ 9 = 76.2%**. Tuchscherer's caveat: the chart "roughly correlates an RPE and rep range to a percentage. It should only be used as a guide, not to attempt to derive a max"; it is most accurate at low reps and high RPE. [EliteFTS: Tuchscherer RPE chapter](https://elitefts.com/blogs/training/rate-of-perceived-exertion-rpe)
- **Barbell Medicine e1RM method** [Convention]: compute an estimated 1RM each workout from the most accurate set available (5 @ RPE 9 or a single @ RPE 8) and set back-off loads as a percentage of today's e1RM instead of a stale tested max. [BBM forum](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/e1rm-calculation-in-the-templates/664). On overshooting: two quotes earlier attributed to BBM coaches ("is not a reason for great concern"; "continuing to blindly add weight to the bar...") were **not found** in the thread the research note cited, and their source is unknown (FFP add.). The confirmed staff advice (Baraki, 2021) is to use an overreaching block as information for the next one: trim "a set or two," subtract 1 point from RPE targets, or rearrange training and rest days. [BBM forum 11314](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/how-to-progress-while-not-exceeding-capacity-or-over-reaching-too-frequently/11314)
- **RPE stops for volume** [Convention, evidence unverified]: "do back-off sets until a set reaches RPE 9, maximum N sets." A thesis on 12 powerlifters reportedly found volume "can be effectively autoregulated using RPE stops" (FFP KQ4); the repository serves a reCAPTCHA, so this is **(unverified)**, and Hickmott 2022 found no study of the chronic effects of RPE stops. The method makes a session shrink automatically on a compressed or tired day, which is why it suits a variable week.

### 2.7 Velocity-based training

- **Strength: the evidence conflicts, and any effect is small.** Hickmott 2022's split (velocity loss <=25% vs >25%) slightly favours <=25% (MD 2.32 kg, SMD 0.23). Jukic 2023's continuous meta-regression finds **no velocity-loss effect on strength** (b = -0.004, 95% CI -0.008 to 0.001, p = 0.097; 17 studies). Zhang 2023 finds a reverse-U peaking at 20-30% (a second Zhang 2023 analysis: 15-30%). Do not treat "20-25% or less" as a firm strength rule.
- **Hypertrophy: modestly favours higher velocity loss**, not robustly. Hickmott: >25% vs <=20%, SMD 0.34. Pareja-Blanco 2017: 40% loss grew vastus lateralis and intermedius more than 20% loss (MRI). Jukic: slope 0.006 per percentage point of loss, from 6 studies, and it lost significance when 2 influential groups were removed.
- **Fatigue and power:** higher velocity loss hurt jump, sprint, and velocity against submaximal loads (Jukic 2023); fatigue and soreness grade 10% < 20% < 30% (Weakley 2024). Lower loss (10-20%) preserves velocity and does fewer reps for similar strength.
- Without a device, approximate with RIR: stopping at 2-4 RIR corresponds to the low-velocity-loss region; grinding to 0-1 RIR corresponds to high velocity loss (PP Q2 inference). Inference for this program: on main lifts, stay at the low-loss end on any week length, because the hypertrophy gain from grinding is small and uncertain while the fatigue cost is consistent; it matters most on 5-6-day weeks, where exposures are closer together.

| [Evidence] Helms et al. 2020 review (J Hum Kinet 74:23-42; 90 studies) | Autoregulation methods | Performance-based load progression, flexible configurations, RIR-based RPE and velocity adjustments are "practical, reliable and show preliminary utility." | [Evidence] Huang et al. 2025 (J Exerc Sci Fit; network meta-analysis); Larsen et al. 2021 (PeerJ; 14 studies, no pooling) | Autoregulation methods vs percentage | Huang ranks APRE > velocity > RPE > percentage and claims all beat percentage; this conflicts with Hickmott's non-significant pooled difference and rests on SUCRA rankings in a lower-profile journal, so treat it as weak. Larsen: all autoregulation methods effective. | Low |

Bottom line: RPE-based loading is at least as good as percentages and more robust to daily variation; percentages are easier to execute while learning RPE. Adding reps and adding load gave similar strength and hypertrophy in the two RCTs that compared them (Plotkin 2022, trained; Chaves 2024, untrained), so double progression is a sound default for accessories and rep-range lifts. Double vs triple progression remains untested, and no trial compares a full linear-progression program against these over a long horizon (PP add., "New evidence").

## 3. Deloads

### Rules (design spine item 5)

1. Deload **roughly every 4-6 weeks, or sooner when readiness indicators say so**. Supported by practice data, not trials: coaches programmed deloads every 4-6 weeks (Bell 2022; its guidelines table allows 4-8), and athletes self-reported one every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks lasting 6.4 ± 1.7 days (Rogerson 2024).
2. **Keep frequency. Halve the sets. Keep some heavy-but-easy work** (top sets around RPE 6-7). Do not take a full week off mid-block if strength is a goal. Keeping frequency matches the Rogerson 2024 survey and the Delphi's 90.5% item. **"Halve the sets" is RP convention, not a tested number:** RP's own example deloads to maintenance volume (about 6 sets), S&C coaches mostly cut volume 0-25% (De Marco 2024, a different population), and no trial compares deload sizes.
3. Reactive triggers that justify an early deload: missed reps at the prescribed RPE in two consecutive sessions on a lift; rising RPE at the same load across the block; soreness that does not clear between exposures; disrupted sleep or appetite; joint pain. Rogerson's athletes named stalled performance, soreness, and joint aches.
4. A deload can double as the first, low-volume week of the next mesocycle (Delphi: 67.9% in round 2, 75.0% in round 3; consensus threshold 70%).

### Reconciling Coleman 2024 with the coaches

Stopping entirely probably cost some lower-body strength; coaches and athletes rarely stop entirely. Keeping frequency and some heavy work while cutting sets reduces fatigue without letting the strength expression detrain. Pancar 2026 is the only trial of a reduced deload and found no cost, but in untrained lifters on isolation exercises. Cutting volume rather than intensity also matches the maintenance evidence that load is the variable to protect (`guide/01-training-principles.md`, section 7). No study compares volume-only, intensity-only, and full-rest deloads head-to-head in trained lifters, no trial compares deload sizes, and the Pritchard 2015 taper review was not reachable, so taper percentages are not given here (PP Q3 gaps).

Timing: the Delphi accepts pre-planned, autoregulated, or combined deloads, and most surveyed athletes plan them with an autoregulated override (Rogerson 2024). For an intermediate whose fatigue tracks life stress, a planned default with reactive triggers fits best; the 4-6-week planned default alone is for someone who will not monitor closely. The deload interval is counted in passes (exposures per lift), so it is the same at 3 and at 6 days a week; a 6-day week does more weekly sets, so watch the reactive triggers more closely there (inference). The exposure-based count is in `guide/03-flexible-scheduling.md`.

## 4. Mesocycle structure

### The hypertrophy block (RP-style volume ramp) [Convention]

- Start near minimum effective volume, add **one to two sets per muscle per week** over **4-6 weeks** until performance stalls or volume nears the recoverable ceiling, then deload about one week and restart lower. Third-party summaries say about one set per week; RP's own example adds about two and deloads to maintenance volume (SNP add., correction 6).
- Signals to end a block: near-zero reps in reserve at prior loads; volume approaching the ceiling; soreness, pump, performance, and joint feedback trending the wrong way.
- Landmark numbers conflict across sources. RP's own chest guide gives intermediate MV 2-4, MEV 4-6, MAV 6-16, MRV 16-24 weekly sets; an unofficial compilation (possibly older RP values, **unverified**) lists chest MV 8 / MEV 10 / MAV 12-20 / MRV 22+, quads MEV 8, back MEV 10 / MRV 25+; a Boxrox summary gives chest 12-20. Treat them as starting hypotheses. The often-attributed 3-to-1 RIR ramp across a block was **not confirmed** in any retrieved source.
- A practical illustration (PP Q4 inference, not a prescription): week 1 at roughly 8-10 hard sets per muscle at 3-4 RIR; add 1-2 sets per muscle per week and tighten to 1-2 RIR by week 4-5; deload in week 5 or 6 at ~50% of sets with loads at ~85-90% of the prior week; restart the next block 1-2 sets above the previous week-1 volume if the block produced PRs, otherwise at the same starting volume.

### The strength block

- The volume ramp inverts: hold weekly sets flat or slightly falling while intensity climbs (65-75% toward 80-90%), matching accumulation, intensification, realization.
- 5/3/1 is a 4-week mesocycle (3 loading weeks plus deload) with the TM rising 5/10 lb per cycle. Block periodization examples run accumulation 2-6 weeks, transmutation up to 4, realization 1-2 (secondary sources; section 1).

### Counting weeks when the week length varies

In this program a "week" of a mesocycle is a count of **exposures per lift**, not a calendar week. In the rotation one pass equals one calendar week for any 3- to 6-day week, because every main lift gets its heavy and volume exposure in every week type; only a 2-day week or a missed session stretches a pass (FFP KQ6). 5/3/1 already works this way: a 4-week cycle takes 5+ weeks at 3 days per week (third-party wording, **unverified** at source; the rotation logic matches Wendler's own posts). The exact counting rule is in `guide/03-flexible-scheduling.md` and `templates/progression-tracks.md`.

Frequency itself: Pelland et al. (Sports Med 2026;56:481-505) found strength rises with weekly frequency, with diminishing returns, while hypertrophy is compatible with negligible frequency effects. So a 5-6-day week may add a little strength through more practice, and a 3-day week costs little muscle if weekly sets are kept.

### Robust rule

Because the landmark numbers conflict, the robust rule is procedural: start lower than you think, add sets until recovery markers (sleep, joint pain, rep performance at a fixed load) degrade, then deload. No RCT tests MEV-to-MRV ramping against flat volume, and no comparative evidence settles 4 vs 6 vs 8-week blocks (PP Q4 gaps).

## 5. Plateaus

Research on plateaus is thin. The best-supported moves are a modest load reset (5-15%) with a change in set/rep structure, a change of relative-intensity zone, or reducing volume on the heaviest day when the top set stalls. Program-hopping is contested; switch programs only after the program's own stall rule has fired at least once without restoring progress.

### Diagnostic order for a stalled lift (PP Q5 inference)

1. Check the **e1RM trend over 3-4 weeks**, not one session. Barbell Medicine: "most of what gets called a stall is impatience with the noise floor at your current strength level."
2. Check **recovery inputs**: sleep, calories, bodyweight trend.
3. Apply the **program's reset rule** (5-15% load drop and rebuild).
4. Change the **rep or intensity zone** for 4-8 weeks (a stalled 5x5 to 4x8, or to heavier triples). One cited study found alternating 12-week low-load and moderate-load blocks helped subjects avoid a plateau. [SBS plateaus](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/plateaus/)
5. Change the **exercise variation** for a block (pause squat, tempo bench). Evidence on how often to rotate variations was not retrieved.
6. Only then change the whole program. A coach heuristic: consider a change every 4-16 weeks, and only for a plateau the program caused.

Volume reduction on the heaviest day is the fix when top sets stall; adding a set is the fix when top sets feel easy but reps do not climb.

### Named stall rules in one place

| Program | Stall definition | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Starting Strength | Same weight fails 3 sessions running | Reset 5-10%, rebuild (site unreachable; **unverified** at source) |
| StrongLifts | 3 failed workouts at a weight | About -10% on that lift (confirmed at source) |
| Greyskull | Top set under 5 reps | -10% (**unverified**) |
| SBS novice tips | Rep scheme stalls | -10-15%, rebuild 4x8 to 6x5 to 6x3; or -15% and add a set |
| Texas Method | Friday PR missed | Reduce Monday volume (5x5 to 6x4 / 8x3, or 4x5 / 3x5); cycle intensity-day rep schemes; hold volume-day weight 2-4 weeks |
| 5/3/1 | AMRAPs stop beating prior cycles; or <5 reps at 100% TM on the TM test | TM to ~90% of current, rebuild (the 10% cut is not confirmed as Wendler's wording) |
| GZCLP | Base reps not achieved | Next rep scheme (5x3 to 6x2 to 10x1), then retest 5RM after 2-3 rest days and restart at 85% |
| nSuns | 0-1 reps on the 1+ set repeatedly | Reduce training 1RM 10% |
| Westside | Accommodation | Rotate max-effort exercise every 1-3 weeks (critics: costs specificity) |

## 6. Training age: the Practical Programming framework and the Barbell Medicine critique

**Rippetoe and Baker** [Convention]: advancement is defined by how long the stress-recovery-adaptation cycle takes, "not a trainee's strength or athleticism." Novices adapt between sessions, so add load every session (+5 lb squat/bench/press, +10 lb deadlift). Intermediates adapt weekly, so plan weekly PRs (Texas Method: ~5 lb/week squat and deadlift; bench and press every two weeks). Advanced lifters adapt over months. Andy Baker (per forum citation): run the Texas Method 16-24 weeks before moving to heavy-light-medium. Starting Strength's own gyms warn that lifters wrongly copy novice methods into intermediate training.

**Barbell Medicine** [Critique]: "all of the adaptations run at the same rate at year one and year ten." What changes with training age is remaining ceiling, measurement noise, and the stimulus required, not biology. Prescribing load increases on a schedule tied to a stage is the error; progression should be **reactive to demonstrated performance**. The companion podcast (Feigenbaum and Baraki, May 2026) cites a 2024 Sports Medicine analysis of 17 years of IPF data with no "kink" in the strength-progress curve by training age (reported secondhand, **unverified**). A likely candidate, Latella et al. 2024 (Sports Med 54:753-774), describes non-linear, decelerating gains (~7.5-12.5% in year 1, up to ~20% after 10 years) and does not mention a "kink" or a 17-year window, so the match is not confirmed. [Barbell Medicine](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/novice-intermediate-advanced-strength-training/)

**Where the evidence sits:** training status does not limit the benefit of periodization. In Williams 2017 the periodized-vs-non-periodized effect was **larger in untrained** participants (b = -0.59), and Moesgaard 2022 reports no training-status split for that comparison. Training status matters for the **style** of periodization: Moesgaard's trained-only finding is that undulating beat linear for strength in trained lifters (ES 0.61, CI lower bound 0.00) but not in untrained ones (ES 0.06). Read together: untrained lifters progress on almost any progressive overload and still gain a little from periodization; trained lifters gain a little from periodization and possibly a little more from undulating it. Neither changes hypertrophy.

**Operational agreement:** both camps say add load when performance shows you can, and lengthen the time between planned PR attempts as progress slows. The disagreement is whether to pre-assign cycle length by stage or let the data choose. For a self-coached intermediate, the reactive version is safer: per-session increments until two consecutive misses, then weekly, then 3-6-week cycles. Rule of thumb consistent with both: novice 1 session; early intermediate 1 week; later intermediate 3-6-week mesocycles; advanced 8-16-week blocks with a peak (PP Q6 inference). These intervals count exposures, so on a 3-day week a "1 week" cycle means one heavy and one volume exposure per lift, the same as on a 6-day week. Practical Programming's own text was not accessible; definitions come from reviews (PP Q6 gaps).

## 7. What to log

Chapter 07 covers tools and apps. The minimum record, and the criteria for judging a block, are here because they are what progression schemes run on.

**Per set:** exercise, load, reps, RPE or RIR, a note on technique or pain.
**Per session:** bodyweight, sleep hours, a 1-5 readiness score.
**Per week:** total hard sets per muscle group (fractional counting, `guide/01-training-principles.md`, section 1).
**Per lift:** an estimated 1RM (e1RM) from the day's most accurate set, which is a heavy, low-rep set at RPE 8 or higher. RIR ratings are most accurate near failure and at high loads (Russo et al. 2025, Physical Therapy Reviews; Halperin 2022; Remmert 2023), so e1RMs from long sets are less trustworthy.

**Judging a block.** Decide the success criterion before the block starts: (a) e1RM on the main lifts up at matched RPE versus week 1; or (b) more reps at the same load; or (c) the same performance at lower RPE; for hypertrophy blocks, (d) measurable circumference or bodyweight change with stable or better rep performance. Because single-session RPE is noisy, average e1RM over the last three sessions of a block and compare with the first three. If none of (a)-(c) occurred after a full block plus deload, run the plateau procedure in section 5.

**How accurate are RPE ratings?** Peer-reviewed figures now replace the vendor claim that experienced lifters rate "within 0.5 RPE," which is no longer used:

| Study | What was measured | Result |
|---|---|---|
| [Evidence] Helms et al. 2017 (JSCR 31:2938-43; 12 powerlifters) | Reported RPE vs the **target** RPE when choosing loads (not vs true failure) | Mean absolute deviation 0.33 ± 0.28. Bench more accurate at 3 @ 9 than 2 @ 8, and bench more accurate than squat on 8 @ 8 sets. [PubMed 28933716](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28933716/) |
| [Evidence] Refalo et al. 2024 (JSCR 38:e78-e85; 24 trained, bench at 75% 1RM) | Predicted vs actual reps to failure | Absolute error 0.65 ± 0.78 reps at 1 and 3 RIR, slight tendency to underpredict. [PubMed 37967832](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37967832/) |
| [Evidence] Halperin et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:377-390; exploratory meta-analysis, 262 effects) | Predicted vs actual reps to failure | Underprediction by 0.95 reps on average (95% CI 0.17-1.73), I2 = 97.9%. Better closer to failure, at <= 12 reps to failure, and in later sets; training status did not matter. [PubMed 34542869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/) |

Practical reading: errors run to **underprediction** (lifters think they are closer to failure than they are), and squat is rated less accurately than bench (Helms 2017; Hermann 2025). A logged "RPE 8" is more often a true 7 than a 9, which makes RPE-capped load increases conservative rather than aggressive. Velocity-RIR profiles beat subjective estimates in early sets and at lighter loads (Hickmott 2024) if a device is available.

Other trackable signals: 5/3/1's "+" sets are rep PRs at a fixed TM percentage; RTS fatigue percents log per-session dose; RP's soreness, pump, performance, and joint feedback adjust volume, with rising RIR at the same load, non-clearing soreness, and flattening performance as deload signals; Coleman 2024's readiness questionnaires favoured the continuous group, a reminder that subjective readiness is informative. No peer-reviewed validation of e1RM-trend or "differential RPE" as a predictor of long-term progress was found (PP Q7 gaps).
