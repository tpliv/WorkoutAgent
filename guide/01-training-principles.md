# 01. Training principles: volume, frequency, intensity, effort, rest, and the maintenance dose

Last updated: 2026-10-21

This chapter states each training variable as a rule and gives the evidence behind it. Strength outcomes (1RM on the main lifts) and hypertrophy outcomes (muscle size) are kept separate throughout, because they respond differently to volume, load, and frequency.

Source note: all evidence comes from `research/frequency-volume-intensity-evidence.md` (cited below as FVI, Q1-Q7, plus its 2026-10-21 addendum) and `research/flexible-frequency-programming.md` (FFP, KQ2-KQ3, plus its addendum). Numbers that are still unverified after the addenda are marked **(unverified)**. Practitioner sources are labelled as such. Pre-2016 work is marked **(older)**.

## 1. Volume: hard sets per muscle per week

### Rules

1. Count **hard sets** (sets taken to 0-3 reps in reserve). Warm-ups do not count.
2. For hypertrophy, target **10-20 hard sets per muscle per week**. Use the lower half when the week is short and the upper half when it is long (table below).
3. For strength on a main lift, **2-6 heavy direct sets per lift per session, 2-3 sessions per week** captures most of the signal. More sets build muscle, not 1RM.
4. Count indirect work at **half credit**: a pressing set counts ~0.5 for triceps and front delts; a row or pulldown set counts ~0.5 for biceps and rear delts (FVI Q2, Pelland's "fractional" counting).
5. Reserve 20+ weekly sets for a lagging muscle or a short overreaching block. Trained-lifter trials at 30-60 sets per week show small extra strength returns at best and, in one case, a deadlift penalty.

### Weekly set targets per muscle group

These numbers are the program's binding targets (design spine item 3). They are deliberately the same across muscle groups: the literature does not support muscle-by-muscle precision (FVI Q3). The fractional-counting column shows why small muscles need fewer *direct* sets to reach the same total.

| Back (lats, mid-back) | 8-12 | 10-14 | 14-20 | 3-5 / 5-8 | 2 / 2-3 | Rows and pulldowns are direct |

Arithmetic note: in a 3-day week with two exposures per muscle, hitting 8-12 weekly sets needs 4-5 sets per exposure; with three exposures (the full-body rotation this program uses, see `guide/03-flexible-scheduling.md`), 3-4 sets per exposure is enough. Both stay inside the 3-5 sets-per-session band.

Per-session ceiling: **~8-10 hard sets per muscle per session** (section 2). More days buy more weekly sets; they do not make each set more effective.

**What this means for the program.** Volume is the main driver of hypertrophy up to roughly 20 sets, and a cheap input for strength. The 5-6-day weeks exist to let weekly sets rise toward 14-20 per muscle without any session exceeding the per-session ceiling. The 3-day weeks run 8-12 sets and lose little (section 7).

### Renaissance Periodization landmarks (coaching convention, not evidence)

RP's MV / MEV / MAV / MRV framework (maintenance, minimum effective, maximum adaptive, maximum recoverable volume) brackets the research range. RP now publishes its own per-muscle tables for 14 muscles (`research/splits-and-named-programs.md`, addendum row 9). The current chest page (modified 2025-07-07, for "serious, intermediate lifters") gives **MV 2-4, MEV 4-6, MAV 6-16, MRV 16-24 sets/week**, an "8-12 set per muscle per session maximum", and 2-4 sessions per week ([RP chest guide](https://rpstrength.com/chest-training-tips-hypertrophy/)). An earlier version of the page, quoted here before 2026-10-21, gave MV ~4-6, MEV ~6 and MRV ~20 sets at 2 sessions / ~25 at 3; that wording is gone. RP's landmarks article separately gives MV "around 6 working sets" when a muscle is trained at least twice weekly, which conflicts with most of the muscle-page MVs (0-4). Third-party tables (FitnessVolt, Arvo) do not match RP's current values. Treat landmark numbers as starting points with roughly +/-5 sets of uncertainty, and note that Pelland's "~4 fractional sets exceed the smallest detectable effect" (4 vs 0 sets +2.21%, confirmed from the OSF tables) sits at or below RP's current MEVs, while Räntilä 2026 and Aube 2022 show no benefit near typical MRVs.

## 2. The per-session set ceiling

### Rules

1. Plan **no more than ~8-10 hard sets per muscle per session**. For direct hard sets on a heavy compound, **6-8** is the safer planning cap.
2. If weekly volume will not fit under that ceiling in the sessions available, cut weekly volume; do not stack more sets into a session.
3. For strength, the first **~2 heavy direct sets per lift per session** do most of the work; additional heavy sets are for practice and volume, not for more 1RM.

### Evidence

| Source | Finding | Status |
|---|---|---|

| Helms, Morgan & Valdez, *The Muscle and Strength Pyramid: Training*, 3rd ed. [PubMed 20300012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20300012/); [PubMed 19661829](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19661829/) | Older, foundational |

The program's 8-10 planning cap sits just under three independent figures: Remmert's ~11 fractional sets (preprint), Helms' ~11 (practitioner) and RP's 8-12 (practitioner). The cap is unchanged.

**What this means for the program.** The ceiling is the mechanism that makes frequency matter: 16 weekly sets fit in two sessions of 8, not one session of 16. It is also why a 3-day week cannot simply carry a 6-day week's volume: 18 quad sets in one session exceeds Remmert's threshold by ~60% and the extra sets are near-zero return at a real recovery cost (FVI Q7).

## 3. Frequency: how often to train each muscle

### Rules

1. **Every muscle at least 2x per week** in every week type. 2-3x in 5-6-day weeks.
2. Treat frequency as a **volume-distribution tool**. Pick weekly sets first, then spread them so no session exceeds the ceiling.
3. **Main lifts 2x per week even in a 3-day week**, because strength has a modest independent frequency benefit (skill practice, higher per-set quality).
4. Below ~10 weekly sets per muscle, once per week is probably fine (Nuckols, Stronger By Science 2021, reviewing Cuthbert et al. 2021; practitioner reading); above it, 2x+ is needed to stay under the ceiling.

**Where the two outcomes differ.** For hypertrophy, 2x vs 3x vs 6x per muscle is indistinguishable at equal weekly sets. For strength, extra exposures carry a small benefit that diminishes quickly: going from 2 to 3 sessions per lift adds about 2% 1RM in Pelland's model, below the smallest detectable effect. So 2x on each main lift is worth protecting in short weeks and 3x is a luxury for long ones. No meta-analysis isolates 2x vs 3x vs 4x+ in trained lifters with volume equated; the evidence is a handful of trials with 8-21 per arm (FVI Q1 gaps).

## 4. Intensity: load and rep ranges

### Rules

1. **Main-lift strength work: 3-6 reps at ~75-90% 1RM.** The research range is 1-6 reps at >=80% (FVI Q4); the program uses 3-6 so that RPE can be judged and volume accumulated.
2. **Hypertrophy work: 6-12 reps**, up to **15-20 on isolation** (occasionally 20-30), as long as the set is hard.
3. Do not chase light-load hypertrophy on compounds; it works, but it costs more fatigue and more reps for the same stimulus.

### Evidence

| Study | Hypertrophy | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn & Krieger 2017 (JSCR; 21 studies; <=60% vs >60% 1RM, all to failure) | Similar between conditions | 1RM significantly greater with high load; isometric strength no difference. [PubMed 28834797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/) |
| Lopez et al. 2021 (MSSE 53(6); network meta-analysis, 28 studies, 747 adults; low >15RM, moderate 9-15RM, high <=8RM) | No difference between loads (P = .113-.469) | High vs low SMD 0.60-0.63; moderate vs low 0.34-0.35; high vs moderate not significant (SMD 0.26-0.28, P = .068). [PubMed 33433148](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/) |
| Schoenfeld, Grgic, Van Every & Plotkin 2021 (Sports 9(2):32) | "Muscular adaptations can be obtained, and in some cases optimized, across a wide spectrum of loading zones." The heavy/moderate/light "repetition continuum" is too rigid. [VU open access](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/42418/) | 1RM strength remains load-specific |
| Cumming et al. 2025 (J Appl Physiol 139(3):685-697; 14 trained, within-subject, 9 wk) | 3-5RM vs 20-25RM to failure: similar hypertrophy | Multi-joint 1RM gains similar (~21%); single-joint 1RM favoured heavy loads (+9% vs -3%). [PubMed 40827709](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40827709/) |
| ACSM 2026 Position Stand | Not affected by load (30-100% 1RM) | Enhanced by loads >=80% 1RM. [PubMed 41843416](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/) |

Confidence: high that hypertrophy is load-independent across ~30-85% 1RM when sets are hard, now including a trained-lifter trial (Cumming 2025); high that 1RM strength needs heavy practice; the high-vs-moderate strength gap is small and not statistically secure, and in Cumming 2025 the load-specificity showed mainly on single-joint tests.

## 5. Proximity to failure (RPE and RIR)

### Rules

1. **Working sets at 0-3 reps in reserve.** Default on compounds: **1-2 RIR (RPE 8-9)**.
2. Strength top sets: RPE 7-9, rarely to failure. Failure adds fatigue without a strength benefit.
3. Hypertrophy work: the last 1-2 sets of an isolation exercise may go to failure; compounds stay at 1-3 RIR to protect volume and recovery.
4. Use the RIR-based RPE scale (RPE 10 = 0 RIR, 9 = 1 RIR, 8 = 2 RIR). Expect your estimate to be accurate within ~1 rep only when you are 0-3 reps from failure; "RPE 7" on a compound is noisy. Anchor most hypertrophy sets at RPE 8-9 because that is where the estimate is reliable.

### Evidence

| Study | Hypertrophy | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele & Zourdos 2024 (Sports Med 54(9); meta-regression on estimated RIR) | "Seems to improve upon terminating sets closer to failure"; modest, possibly non-linear | In all best-fit models "the confidence intervals of the marginal slopes for estimated RIR contained a null point estimate." RIR values were estimated from study descriptions; exact slopes not retrievable. [Abertay repository](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/) |
| Grgic et al. 2022 (J Sport Health Sci 11(2):202-211; 15 studies) | Failure vs non-failure ES 0.22 (95% CI -0.11 to 0.55), NS; trained subgroup favoured failure, ES 0.15 (0.03-0.26) (subgroup size **unverified**) | ES -0.09 (95% CI -0.22 to 0.05), NS; with volume not equated, non-failure better, ES -0.32 (-0.57 to -0.07). [PubMed 33497853](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33497853/) |
| Refalo et al. 2023 (Sports Med 53(3):649-665; 15 studies) | Set failure (any definition) vs non-failure ES 0.19 (0.00-0.37), p = 0.045, a "trivial advantage"; momentary muscular failure vs non-failure ES 0.12 (-0.13 to 0.37), NS. [PubMed 36334240](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/) | n/a |
| Refalo et al. 2024 (J Sports Sci 42(1):85-101; 18 trained, within-subject, 8 wk) | Failure vs 1-2 RIR: identical quad thickness gains (0.181 vs 0.182 cm); failure caused more velocity and rep loss. Supports the 1-2 RIR default. [PubMed 38393985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/) | n/a |
| ACSM 2026 Position Stand | Not affected by training to failure. [PubMed 41843416](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/) | n/a |
| Davies, Orr, Halaki & Hackett 2016 (Sports Med 46(4):487-502; 8 studies) **(older; online Dec 2015)** | n/a | Pooled ES 0.34 favouring **non**-failure (p = 0.02; a 0.6-1.3% larger gain); volume-uncontrolled ES 0.41; volume-controlled NS. Authors: the difference is "unlikely to be meaningful." Vieira et al. 2021 (JSCR 35(4):1165-1175; 13 studies) agrees: overall SMD -0.08 (NS), non-failure better only with unequal volume. [PubMed 26666744](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26666744/); [PubMed 33555822](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33555822/) |
| Zourdos, Helms et al. 2016 (JSCR 30(1):267-275; 29 lifters) | RIR-based RPE scale; strong inverse relationship between bar velocity and RPE (r = -0.88 in experienced lifters); traditional Borg RPE only reaches 6.8-9.0 at failure. This paper did not quantify rep-prediction error. [PubMed 26049792](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26049792/) | Same |
| Helms et al. 2017 (JSCR 31(10):2938-2943), Halperin et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:377-390), Refalo et al. 2024 (JSCR 38(3):e78-e85) and Russo et al. 2025 (systematic review) | RIR accuracy is better close to failure and at higher loads; it degrades as reps rise and %1RM falls. Squat is rated less accurately than bench (`research/periodization-and-progression.md`, addendum). [PubMed 34542869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/); [PubMed 37967832](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37967832/) | Same |

Confidence: moderate. No high-quality trial compares RPE-anchored and %1RM-anchored programming head-to-head for hypertrophy in intermediates (FVI Q4 gaps).

## 6. Rest intervals

### Rules

1. **2-3 minutes between sets of compounds.**
2. **60-90 seconds between sets of isolation and machine work.** Going under 60 s costs reps and therefore effective volume.
3. On time-constrained days, antagonist supersets with 60-90 s between movements preserve the >60 s per-muscle rest while cutting session time. The exact protocol is an inference (FVI Q5), but the superset meta-analysis of Zhang et al. 2025 (Sports Med 55(4):953-975; 19 studies) found no chronic strength or hypertrophy difference overall and more reps with agonist-antagonist pairs (`research/exercise-selection-and-session-design.md`, addendum). Session pairing details are in `guide/05-exercise-selection.md`, section 6.

### Evidence

| Study | Finding | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Singer, Wolf, Schoenfeld et al. 2024 ("Give it a rest," Front Sports Act Living; Bayesian meta-analysis) | "Small hypertrophic benefit to employing inter-set rest interval durations >60 s, perhaps mediated by reductions in volume load"; no appreciable difference when resting >90 s. 9 studies, 19 measurements. Pooled pre-post SMD 0.48 (0.19-0.81) short vs 0.56 (0.24-0.86) longer rest; controlled short-vs-longer SMDs arm 0.13 (-0.27 to 0.51), thigh 0.17 (-0.13 to 0.43), whole body -0.08 (-0.45 to 0.29). No strength outcomes. [PubMed 39205815](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205815/); [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/text) | Hypertrophy |
| Grgic, Lazinica, Mikulic, Krieger & Schoenfeld 2017 (Eur J Sport Sci; systematic review) | Both short and long rest viable for untrained; "a possible advantage for the use of long rest intervals" in trained participants. [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/44281/) | Hypertrophy |
| Schoenfeld et al. 2016 (JSCR 30(7):1805-1812; 21 trained men, 8 wk, 1 vs 3 min; 3 full-body sessions/wk, 3 x 8-12RM of 7 exercises) | 3-min group gained more 1RM squat and bench; anterior-thigh thickness significantly greater; triceps trend (p = 0.06). The viral "93% more growth" figure is undocumented. [PubMed 26605807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/) | Strength and hypertrophy |

Confidence: moderate for hypertrophy (one Bayesian meta-analysis); low-moderate for strength (one trained-subject trial plus mechanism). No meta-analysis quantifies rest effects on strength specifically.

## 7. The maintenance dose and what it means for short weeks

### Rules

1. **Intensity (load) is the variable to protect.** Every maintenance finding is conditional on relative load being kept.
2. A week at **one-third to one-half of peak weekly volume at normal loads maintains and usually still builds** for a lifter under about 40. A 3-day week at 8-12 sets per muscle is a slow-progress week, not a lost week.
3. When cutting, keep the main-lift top sets heavy (RPE 8-9) and cut accessory and back-off sets first.
4. **Age flag:** lifters in their late 40s and 50s should plan short weeks at the one-half end of the range and keep two sessions per muscle. The older groups in the maintenance studies needed more.
5. A single bad week (<=7 days off) costs no measurable strength or size; return to the rotation without make-up sessions. It may slow lower-body strength *gains* slightly (Coleman 2024), which is a reason not to take weeks off on purpose, not a reason to make up sessions.

### Evidence

| Study | Finding | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Spiering, Mujika, Sharp & Foulis 2021 (JSCR 35(5):1449-1458; narrative review) | Muscle size in younger people maintained up to 32 weeks with as little as **1 session/week and 1 set/exercise**, "as long as exercise intensity (relative load) is maintained"; older people likely need up to 2 sessions/week and 2-3 sets/exercise; "intensity appears to be the key variable"; insufficient data for athletes. [PubMed 33629972](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/) | Moderate (review) |
| Bickel, Cross & Bamman 2011 (MSSE 43(7):1177-1187; 70 adults) **(older)** | 16 weeks of 3x/week training, then 32 weeks of detraining, 1/3 dose, or 1/9 dose. In absolute terms the 1/3 dose was 1 session/week of 3 sets x 3 lower-body exercises (**9 sets/week**) at 8-12RM; the 1/9 dose was 1 set x 3 exercises (**3 sets/week**). Lower body only. Young (20-35): both doses preserved myofiber hypertrophy and the **1/3 dose produced additional hypertrophy**. Older (60-75): **neither** reduced dose preserved hypertrophy. Strength: full detraining cut knee-extension 1RM by 7% over 32 weeks, yet it stayed 23% above baseline; both maintenance doses preserved 1RM and added 7-8% above the phase-1 gains, with no age interaction. [PubMed 21131862](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/) | Moderate; subjects were untrained before the 16-week run-in |
| Androulakis-Korakakis 2020/2021 (trained men; powerlifters) | 1 set of 6-12 at 70-85% 1RM 2-3x/week, or ~3-6 heavy sets of 1-5 per week at >80%, still increased 1RM over 6-12 weeks. | Moderate |
| RP maintenance volume (chest) | Current chest page: MV 2-4 sets/week for intermediates; RP's landmarks article: ~6 sets/week when trained at least twice weekly. Both are consistent with Bickel's 3-9 sets. The help-centre "~4-6 sets" quote cited here earlier could not be reached (403) and is **(unverified)**. [RP chest guide](https://rpstrength.com/chest-training-tips-hypertrophy/); [RP landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth/) | Coaching convention |
| Short breaks, 1-3 weeks of no training (FFP addendum) | No significant 1RM loss in trained or recently trained people: Hortobágyi et al. 1993 **(older)** (12 power athletes, 14 days off: bench -1.7%, squat -0.9%, NS; eccentric force -12%); Hwang et al. 2017 (20 trained men, 2 weeks off: no leg-press loss, lean mass kept); Ogasawara et al. 2011 **(older)** (3 weeks off: no significant 1RM or CSA loss). Coleman et al. 2024 (39 trained, a 1-week break mid-program): no hypertrophy difference, but lower-body strength gains were probably smaller (posterior probability 0.85 for squat 1RM). [PubMed 8371654](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8371654/); [PubMed 28328712](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28328712/); [PubMed 21771261](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21771261/); [PMC10809978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10809978/) | Moderate; complete cessation, small samples |
| Longer breaks, 4+ weeks | Izquierdo et al. 2007 **(older)** (46 active men after 16 weeks of RT): 4 weeks of complete cessation cut maximal strength 6-9%, while a 4-week taper (volume cut, intensity kept) added 2%. Losses grow with duration (Bosquet et al. 2013 meta-analysis, **older**; McMaster et al. 2013, elite rugby and football, **older**: strength held up to ~3 weeks, then decays). Losses are regained quickly: Halonen et al. 2024 (55 untrained adults, 10 weeks off mid-program) regained them rapidly and finished level with continuous trainers. [PubMed 17685721](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17685721/); [PubMed 23529287](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/); [PubMed 39364857](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39364857/) | Moderate; complete cessation, mixed populations |

The one-week "3-7% strength dip that returns in 1-2 sessions" figure quoted here before 2026-10-21 has no support: its source page now disputes it (FFP addendum, correction 8). It has been removed.

### What it means for the 3-day week

- A 12-18-set week cut to 4-6 sets per muscle is at Bickel's 1/3 dose as a ratio. In absolute terms, 4-6 sets per week sits between Bickel's two doses (3 and 9 sets/week, one session), both of which held size in young adults. The program's 3-day target of 8-12 sets sits around or above the 9-set 1/3 dose. Older adults held size on neither dose, which is why rule 4 keeps them at the one-half end.
- The 3-day week should **not** be built by compressing 6-day volume into fewer sessions (section 2). It should run lower total volume at the same loads.
- The volume-equated frequency trials (Colquhoun and Saric 3 vs 6; Hamarsland 2 vs 4) say that where sets *are* equal, the number of sessions does not matter. The maintenance trials say that where sets are *not* equal, keeping load protects what you have. Together they justify holding intensity constant across week types and letting accessory volume float.
- Caveats: no study has tested alternating high- and low-frequency weeks within one program, and no maintenance study covers lifters with more than 16 weeks of prior training (FVI Q6-Q7 gaps). The recommendation is assembled from steady-state comparisons.

How the sessions are laid out, what to cut first, and how to resume a rotation after a gap are owned by `guide/03-flexible-scheduling.md` and `templates/`.

## 8. Strength versus hypertrophy: one-table summary

| Variable | For 1RM strength on the main lifts | For muscle size |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly volume | Saturates early: 2-6 heavy direct sets per lift per session, 2-3x/week. Schoenfeld 2019: 1 set = 5 sets. Weekly PUOS ~3 fractional sets (Pelland 2026, OSF data). | Positive to ~20 sets/week with diminishing returns (Schoenfeld 2017; Baz-Valle 2022; Pelland 2026); >=10 sets/week (ACSM 2026). |
| Per-session ceiling | ~2 direct sets per session before returns become undetectable (Remmert 2025, preprint). | ~11 fractional sets (Remmert 2025, preprint; Helms ~11; RP 8-12); plan at 8-10. |
| Frequency | Small independent benefit, diminishing (Pelland 2026: 1 to 2 days +4.1%, 2 to 3 +2.0%); 2x per lift worth protecting; >=2 sessions/week (ACSM 2026). | None detectable at equal volume (Schoenfeld 2019; Saric 2019; Hamarsland 2022; ACSM 2026). |
| Load | Specific: heavy (>=~75-80%) beats light (Lopez 2021; Schoenfeld 2017; ACSM 2026 >=80%), mainly on single-joint tests in Cumming 2025. | Load-independent ~30-85% when sets are hard (Cumming 2025 in trained lifters). |
| Proximity to failure | No relationship with RIR (Robinson 2024); failure slightly worse when volume uncontrolled (Davies 2016; Grgic 2022). | Modest benefit from closer to failure (Robinson 2024), but failure = 1-2 RIR in trained lifters (Refalo 2024). |
| Rest | 3 min > 1 min in trained men (Schoenfeld 2016). | >60 s helps; nothing extra past ~90 s (Singer 2024). |
| Maintenance | Retained through months of detraining (Bickel 2011: -7% over 32 weeks, still +23% over baseline); no significant loss over 1-3 weeks off (Hortobágyi 1993; Hwang 2017); keep load. | 1 session, 1 set/exercise per week at full load holds size in young adults (Spiering 2021; Bickel 2011: 3-9 sets/week); not in older adults. |
