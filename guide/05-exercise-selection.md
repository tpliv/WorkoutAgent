# 05. Exercise selection and session design

Last updated: 2026-10-21 (section 5 ramp shortened the same day)

This chapter answers: which exercises to put in a session, in what order, with what rest, warm-up, and time budget, and what to swap in when the equipment or the week changes. It does not lay out the week. The workout rotation, the per-exercise progression tracks, and the weekly set targets live in [chapter 03](03-flexible-scheduling.md) and the `templates/` folder. Nothing here overrides those.

Evidence base: `research/exercise-selection-and-session-design.md` (KQ1-KQ9). Two caveats apply throughout, and the research note makes both.

- Most "best exercise" claims rest on single small trials (n = 14-63, often untrained or women-only) or on EMG, and EMG has repeatedly failed to predict growth (Plotkin 2023; the incline/flat pec data). Treat exercise-specific effect sizes as low-to-moderate confidence unless a row says otherwise.
- Several primary texts could not be fetched when the note was compiled and were read only through abstracts or secondary reviews. Those items are flagged "secondary" below.

Program-wide defaults from the design spine that this chapter builds on: rest 2-3 min on compounds and 60-90 s on isolation; 0-3 reps in reserve (RIR) on working sets with 1-2 RIR the default on compounds; main-lift strength work at 3-6 reps and about 75-90% 1RM; hypertrophy work at 6-12 reps (up to 15-20 on isolation) as long as sets are hard.

## 1. Movement-pattern taxonomy: what each pattern covers and what it misses

Build every session from these patterns. The "missed" column is the reason isolation work exists in this program. Cells without a citation are standard anatomy (see research note KQ1, Inferences).

| Pattern | Representative exercises | Prime movers covered | Muscles NOT reliably covered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat (knee-dominant, bilateral) | Back squat, front squat, hack squat, leg press | Quadriceps, adductors, gluteus maximus; glute and adductor coverage rises with depth (Kubo 2019, via Stronger By Science, secondary) | Biarticular hamstrings, calves, side and rear delts |
| Hinge (hip-dominant) | Deadlift, Romanian deadlift (RDL), good morning, hip thrust | Glutes, hamstrings (RDL eccentric biceps femoris EMG 305 mV vs 93 mV for prone curl; McAllister 2014), erectors | Quads (hip thrust, RDL), calves |
| Horizontal push | Flat and incline bench press, push-up, dips, machine chest press | Pectoralis major (incline biases the clavicular head; Chaves via Stronger By Science), anterior deltoid, triceps lateral/medial heads | Medial and posterior deltoid; triceps long head (Maeo 2022, secondary) |
| Horizontal pull | Barbell, dumbbell, cable, and chest-supported rows | Lats, mid-traps/rhomboids (Lehman 2004, Dynamic Medicine), rear delts (45-degree incline row; Sweeney thesis), biceps, forearms | Biceps at long muscle length; lower lats biased more by vertical pulls |
| Vertical push | Overhead press, dumbbell shoulder press, landmine press | Anterior deltoid, upper traps, triceps; medial deltoid only partly (BarBend, secondary) | Posterior deltoid; medial deltoid relative to raises |
| Vertical pull | Pull-up, chin-up, lat pulldown | Lats, teres major, biceps (more in chin-up), lower traps | Mid-traps/rhomboids (rows are better; Lehman 2004) |
| Lunge / single-leg | Split squat, walking lunge, step-up, single-leg RDL | Quads, glutes (including medius), adductors, hamstrings (single-leg RDL) | Calves |
| Carry | Farmer's walk, suitcase carry | Grip/forearms, traps, obliques, erectors | Nothing uniquely; no hypertrophy trials exist for carries |
| Core | Plank, cable crunch, hanging leg raise, Pallof press, back extension | Rectus abdominis, obliques, erectors | n/a |

Evidence behind the "missed" column (research note KQ1):

- Squat vs hip thrust, 9 weeks, volume-matched, untrained: squat grew quads (+3.6 ± 1.5 cm²) and adductors (+2.5 ± 0.7 cm²) more; glute growth was similar. Hamstrings were not a primary beneficiary of either (Plotkin et al. 2023, Frontiers in Physiology, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170/full). Authors declared product-related competing interests.
- Shoulder press and lateral raise both produced higher anterior and medial deltoid activation than bench press and dumbbell fly, i.e. horizontal pressing does not meaningfully train the side delt (2020 EMG study, PMC7706677, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7706677).
- Reverse pec deck produced much higher posterior deltoid activity than incline pulldown or seated row (Barbell Medicine EMG synthesis, https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-rear-delt-exercises/; practitioner source).
- Standing (knee-extended) calf raise grew lateral gastrocnemius 12.4% vs 1.7% and medial gastrocnemius 9.2% vs 0.6% compared with seated, 12 weeks, 14 untrained adults (Kinoshita, Maeo et al. 2023, Frontiers in Physiology, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1272106/full).

How this changes with available days: the patterns do not change. In a 3-day week every pattern still appears at least twice (spine rule 3, see chapter 03); what shrinks is the number of isolation additions per session, not the pattern coverage.

## 2. Commonly undertrained muscles and what to add

A compound-only program leaves the muscles below under-stimulated. Each addition is justified by the cited finding (research note KQ1, Inferences table). In a 3-day week, add these as the last 1-2 exercises of a session, paired to save time (section 6). In a 5-6-day week each gets its own slot on 2-3 days.

| Undertrained muscle | Why compounds miss it | Add (first choice) | Add (alternative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medial (side) deltoid | Presses are anterior-delt dominant; horizontal presses give low medial activation (PMC7706677; BarBend, secondary) | Dumbbell or cable lateral raise with slight external rotation (Coratella et al. 2020, IJERPH, EMG in 10 bodybuilders) | Lean-away cable raise, machine lateral raise, upright row to chest height |
| Posterior (rear) deltoid | Rows hit it only partially (Barbell Medicine; Sweeney thesis) | Reverse pec deck / rear-delt fly | Seated rear lateral raise, 45-degree incline row, face pull |
| Hamstrings (biarticular heads) | Squats grow them little; curls at short length grow less (Maeo 2021) | Seated leg curl (long length) | RDL or stiff-leg deadlift; Nordic curl (RDL matched Nordic for biceps femoris architecture) |
| Gastrocnemius / soleus | No standard compound loads plantarflexion | Standing calf raise with a full stretch (Kinoshita 2023; Kassiano 2023) | Leg-press calf raise; seated calf raise for soleus |
| Biceps | Rows and chin-ups load them, but not at long length; regional growth differs by exercise (KQ2) | Incline dumbbell curl (proximal) + preacher curl (distal) | Bayesian cable curl, chin-up |
| Triceps long head | Pressing and pushdowns keep the shoulder neutral (Maeo 2022, secondary) | Overhead cable or dumbbell extension | Skull crusher with arms angled back |
| Upper chest (clavicular pec) | Flat-only pressing left the upper pec behind (Chaves via Stronger By Science, secondary) | 30-degree incline press | Low-to-high cable fly |
| Mid-traps / rhomboids | Vertical pulls and presses do not load scapular retraction (Lehman 2004) | Seated or chest-supported row | Face pull, reverse fly |
| Adductors / glute max | Partial-depth squats (Kubo 2019, secondary) | Squat to full depth | Adductor machine, sumo deadlift, Cossack squat |

Gaps flagged by the research note: no longitudinal trial compares lateral raise with overhead press for medial-delt growth, and no hypertrophy trial exists for any rear-delt exercise or for carries. Those rows rest on EMG and expert consensus.

## 3. Muscle-by-muscle exercise table

Confidence scale from the research note (KQ2): High = multiple RCTs or meta-analytic; Moderate = one RCT with MRI or ultrasound; Low = EMG or expert consensus only. Substitutions read barbell -> dumbbell -> machine/cable -> bodyweight.

| Muscle | Top evidence-based choice(s) | Key study and effect | Confidence | Substitutions (BB -> DB -> machine/cable -> bodyweight) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quadriceps | Deep squat; leg press or hack squat; leg extension (reclined seat back for rectus femoris) | Kubo 2019: deep vs half squat, similar quad growth, more glute/adductor (secondary). Plotkin 2023: squat +3.6 cm² quad vs hip thrust. Baz-Valle et al. (SportRxiv 407, preprint): 40-degree hip angle favoured rectus femoris | Moderate | Back/front squat -> goblet or DB front squat -> hack squat, leg press, leg extension -> sissy squat, split squat, wall sit |
| Gluteus maximus | Deep squat or hip thrust (equivalent); deadlift | Plotkin 2023: similar glute growth, wide CIs. Kubo 2019: deep squat glute +6.7% vs +2.2% half squat | Moderate | BB hip thrust -> DB or single-leg hip thrust -> glute drive, cable pull-through -> glute bridge, step-up |
| Hamstrings | Seated leg curl; RDL or stiff-leg deadlift; Nordic curl | Maeo et al. 2021 (PubMed 33009197, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/): seated +14% vs prone +9% hamstring volume, 20 adults, 12 weeks, MRI. | Pectoralis major | Flat press (mid pec) plus 30-degree incline (upper pec); include both | Chaves via Stronger By Science (https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=57858): incline-only grew upper pec substantially more; the only longitudinal study on press angle (secondary) | Moderate (single trial) | BB bench -> DB flat/incline bench -> machine press, cable fly -> push-up, deficit or ring push-up, dips |

| Medial deltoid | Lateral raise (DB or cable), slight external rotation | Coratella 2020 EMG; no hypertrophy RCT vs press | Low | Upright row (to chest) -> DB lateral raise -> cable or machine lateral raise -> band lateral raise, side-lying raise |
| Posterior deltoid | Reverse pec deck; rear-delt fly; face pull; 45-degree incline row | Barbell Medicine EMG synthesis; Sweeney thesis (16 lifters) | Low | Wide-grip BB row -> bent-over DB rear fly -> reverse pec deck, cable face pull -> band pull-apart, prone Y/T raise |
| Latissimus dorsi | Pull-up or chin-up; lat pulldown; row | Lehman et al. 2004 (Dynamic Medicine, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1476-5918-3-4): wide pulldown and seated row best lat-to-biceps ratio; hypertrophy head-to-head trial registered (NCT07360236), not yet published | Low | BB row -> DB row -> lat pulldown, cable row -> pull-up, inverted row |
| Mid-traps / rhomboids | Seated or chest-supported row | Lehman 2004: highest mid-trap EMG in seated row | Low | Pendlay row -> chest-supported DB row -> cable row, reverse fly -> inverted row |
| Biceps | Incline DB curl (proximal) + preacher curl (distal) | Int J Sports Med 2025 (PubMed 39809454, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39809454/): 63 women, 8 weeks, incline grew proximal thickness more, preacher grew distal more. 2023 trial in 31 trained women leaned to preacher, not significant (BarBend, secondary) | Moderate | BB curl -> incline or preacher DB curl -> cable or Bayesian curl, machine preacher -> chin-up, underhand inverted row |
| Triceps | Overhead extension (long head) + pushdown or close-grip press (lateral/medial heads) | Maeo et al. 2022: overhead-arm extension about +19.9% vs +13.9% neutral-arm triceps volume, 12 weeks (via sci-sport and Biolayne REPS, secondary). Stasinaki 2018 overhead vs pushdown: no significant difference (BarBend, secondary) | Moderate | Close-grip bench, skull crusher -> DB overhead extension -> cable overhead extension, pushdown -> dips, diamond push-up |
| Erectors | Deadlift, back extension, good morning | Standard practice | Low | Deadlift -> DB RDL -> 45-degree back extension -> reverse hyper, bird dog |
| Abdominals / obliques | Cable crunch, hanging leg raise, Pallof press, carries | Standard practice | Low | Weighted sit-up -> DB side bend -> cable crunch, ab wheel -> plank, dead bug, hollow hold |

The one rule that falls out of this table (research note KQ2, Inferences): when choosing between two exercises for the same muscle, pick the one that loads the target muscle at long length (seated over prone curl, overhead over neutral-arm triceps, incline over preacher for proximal biceps, standing over seated calf, deep over shallow squat). The expected advantage is modest, roughly 1.5x relative growth in single trials. The Wolf et al. 2023 meta-analysis (IJSC; SportRxiv preprint) found partial and full ROM about equally effective overall, with the long-length-partial subgroup imprecise (SMD -0.28, 95% QI -0.81 to 0.16, k = 8), and a 2025 within-subject trial in 30 trained adults found full ROM and lengthened partials similar (Biolayne REPS issue 34, secondary). So the default is full ROM with a hard stretch, not deliberately truncated partials.

## 4. Exercise order, rest, and tempo by goal

### 4.1 Order

A meta-analysis of 11 good-to-excellent quality studies found exercise order significantly affects strength gains (the exercise done first gains most) but not hypertrophy (Nunes et al. 2020/2021, European Journal of Sport Science 21(2):149-157, https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4059795?lng=en). The secondary claim that the strength effect is larger when a single-joint exercise goes first was not confirmed from the abstract.

Order template for any session (research note KQ3):

1. Priority or weak-point lift, fresh. This is the one lift whose strength or size you most want to move.
2. Primary heavy compound for the day (squat, hinge, press, or pull), if different from item 1.
3. Secondary compound (opposite pattern or unilateral).
4. Isolation work for the undertrained muscles in section 2. These tolerate fatigue and short rest.

Because hypertrophy is order-insensitive, putting an isolation exercise first for a lagging muscle costs nothing elsewhere and improves strength on that exercise. The main lifts keep their progression tracks regardless of slot (spine rule 2), but a main lift placed later in a session will feel heavier at the same load; see chapter 03 for how the progression handles a missed rep target.

How this changes with available days: in a 3-day week items 1-3 are usually two main lifts plus one secondary compound, and item 4 is 1-2 paired isolations. In a 5-6-day week a session can be a single main lift plus three or four accessories, and weak-point priority rotates across days.

### 4.2 Rest and tempo

Evidence (research note KQ4):

- 1-min vs 3-min rest, 21 trained men, 8 weeks, 3 x 8-12 to failure: 3-min group gained significantly more squat and bench 1RM and more anterior-thigh thickness; triceps trend only; thickness measured at one site (Schoenfeld et al. 2016, JSCR, via Brookbush Institute review). The viral "93% more growth" figure is not supported by the data.
- Systematic review: evidence equivocal, possible advantage for longer rest in trained lifters, few studies (Grgic et al. 2017, EJSS).
- Bayesian meta-analysis of 9 randomized studies: short rest (1 min) blunted myofibrillar protein synthesis vs 5 min and reduced volume load; a secondary source describes the pooled effect of longer rest as modest; the exact pooled estimate was not retrieved (Singer, Wolf, Schoenfeld et al. 2024, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full).
- Repetition duration: hypertrophy similar for anything from 0.5 to 8 s per rep; very slow (10 s) reps inferior (Schoenfeld, Ogborn, Krieger 2015, Sports Medicine, meta-analysis of 8 studies, n = 239, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25601394/). A PeerJ critique notes the included studies varied load and failure status, so the result cannot be attributed to tempo alone.
- Antagonist paired sets: 1-min rest between the paired exercises gave significantly greater training efficiency than 2 or 3 min; 2 min was a balance (paired-set rest study, https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4075858).

| Goal / exercise type | Rest between straight sets | Tempo | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main-lift strength work (3-6 reps, ~75-90% 1RM) | 2-3 min default (spine rule 6); extend to 3-5 min for a near-maximal top set if the next set would otherwise lose reps | Controlled eccentric, explosive concentric | Schoenfeld 2016 strength advantage for 3 min over 1 min; load maintenance |
| Hypertrophy compound (6-12 reps) | 2-3 min | 2-3 s eccentric, 1 s concentric, brief pause at the stretch | Schoenfeld 2016 thigh growth; Singer 2024; any 0.5-8 s rep is fine |
| Hypertrophy isolation (10-20 reps) | 60-90 s | 2-3 s eccentric with a brief stretch pause | Rest matters less when load and volume are easy to maintain |
| Antagonist paired sets | 60 s between the pair's two exercises, 2 min between pairs | As above | Paz 2014; paired-set rest study |
| Muscular endurance / conditioning | 30-60 s | Continuous | Standard practice; little hypertrophy evidence |

Do not prescribe 10-s reps. They cut load and were inferior in the 2015 meta-analysis.

How this changes with available days: rest intervals do not shrink in a 3-day week. If time is short, pair accessories (section 6) rather than cutting compound rest below 2 min.

## 5. Warm-up protocol

Evidence (research note KQ5):

- Stretching systematic review: immediately after stretching, performance changed by about -3.7% (static), +1.3% (dynamic), -4.4% (PNF). Static holds of 60 s or more per muscle produced larger deficits (-4.6%) than shorter holds (-1.1%). Stretching inside a warm-up followed by dynamic activity has limited performance effects (Behm et al. 2016, Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism, https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1360011143567622912).
- 5-s and 30-s static stretches inside a full sport-specific warm-up had no group-level performance effect in 20 athletes (Blazevich et al. 2016, ECSS abstract).
- Specific warm-up, 40 trained men, 3 x 6 at 80% 1RM after each of three warm-ups (2 x 6 at 40% and 80% of the training load; 6 at 80%; 6 at 40%): on bench, the 2-set 40%/80% warm-up gave a shorter time to peak velocity and more total work than the 40% warm-up; on squat, bar speed was higher after the 80% warm-up than the 40% one. The authors conclude that few reps at low loads are not enough (Ribeiro et al. 2020, PubMed 32971729, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32971729/; abstract re-checked 2026-10-21, see the KQ5 addendum).
- Review of 11 studies: maximal strength and reps can be positively affected when the specific warm-up uses loads close to maximum; some studies found no difference between a specific warm-up and none (Motricidade 2021 review).
- Heavier warm-up sets can potentiate the following working sets in trained lifters (Biolayne REPS issue 29, secondary).
- Time-efficient-training recommendations on warm-up and stretching exist (Iversen et al. 2021, Sports Medicine) but were read only through citing papers (secondary).

### 5.1 The protocol

| Step | What | Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| General | 3-5 min light cardio or dynamic movement | 3-5 min | Optional for a well-trained lifter in a warm gym; no study isolates its effect from the specific warm-up |
| Dynamic mobility | 4-6 movements for the day's patterns (leg swings, hip openers, band pull-aparts, arm circles, bodyweight squats) | 2-3 min | Dynamic stretching: +1.3% (Behm 2016) |
| Optional static holds | Ankles or hips for squat depth, pecs or lats for pressing | Under 30 s per muscle | Follow with dynamic work. Never hold 60 s or more before lifting (Behm 2016; Blazevich 2016) |
| Ramp-up sets, first compound only | Empty bar (45 lb / 20 kg) x 5, then 80% x 2-3, then working sets. Add 60% x 3 between them once the top set is 225 lb (about 102 kg) or more | 2-3 min | Follows the two-set structure of Ribeiro 2020 (one light set, one near the working load). Percentages are of the day's top working load (the V load on a volume day), not 1RM |
| Later exercises | One feeder set at about 60-70% | 1 min each | Enough once the body is warm |

The 225-lb rule: when the top set reaches 225 lb (about 102 kg), the jump from the bar to 80% is large (45 to 180 lb), so a 60% x 3 step goes between them. This step is coaching convention, not evidence; no retrieved study tests it (research note KQ5, addendum).

Rounding: round each ramp load to the nearest loadable step (5 lb with 2.5-lb plates); an exact half-step rounds up, and no set goes below the empty bar. Example at a 135-lb squat: 45 x 5, 110 x 2 (80% = 108, rounds to 110).

Total: about 5-6 min before the first working set of the day (general and mobility plus the short ramp), which is the figure used in the time budgets in section 6. The earlier four-step ramp made this about 8 min.

Foam rolling, if used, belongs here rather than after training: pre-exercise rolling improved flexibility about 4% with negligible effects on strength (see chapter 06, section 6).

How this changes with available days: the warm-up is identical in 3-day and 6-day weeks. In a 45-min session, cut general cardio first, never the 80% set.

## 6. Session design: what fits in 45, 60, and 90 minutes

### 6.1 Evidence

- Superset vs traditional-set meta-analysis: similar total reps (SMD -0.03, p = 0.92) and volume load (SMD 0.05, p = 0.86) with shorter sessions and higher training efficiency (SMD 1.74, p = 0.01), at the cost of higher blood lactate and RPE (Weakley et al. 2024/2025, PMC12011898, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011898/). A secondary summary adds that chronic strength, endurance and hypertrophy did not differ (MTN Tactical, secondary).
- One trial finished sessions in 36% less time with supersets, with similar strength and hypertrophy gains (SportRxiv preprint "Less time, same gains").
- Agonist-antagonist paired sets: more knee-extension reps after knee flexion with 0, 30 s or 1 min rest than in a traditional protocol (Paz et al. 2014, PubMed 25148302, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25148302/). A brief review calls paired-set training efficacious and efficient for strength and power (Robbins et al. 2010), though a 20-man study found the intensity used may not increase target-muscle performance (RBCM study).
- Per-session volume meta-regression (Pelland et al., 67 studies, Bayesian): returns plateau around 11 fractional sets per muscle per session for hypertrophy and about 2 direct sets per session for strength (Biolayne REPS issue 36 and SportRxiv 460; preprint and secondary review, peer-reviewed abstract not retrieved).

### 6.2 Time budget

Arithmetic from the research note (KQ6, Inferences; these per-exercise times are inferences, not measured): a working set with setup takes about 45 s; add rest from section 4; about 5-6 min warm-up (section 5.1; 8 min before the ramp was shortened); about 3 min transitions. A straight-set compound with 2.5-min rest costs about 3.2 min per set; an isolation set with 75-s rest about 2 min; an antagonist paired set (two exercises, 60 s between, 2 min after the pair) about 4.5 min per pair, i.e. about 2.25 min per set.

| Session length | Straight sets only | With antagonist or unrelated-muscle supersets for accessories | Suggested structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 45 min | 1 compound x 4 sets + 2 isolations x 3 sets (about 10 hard sets), 3 exercises | 1-2 compounds x 3-4 sets + 2-3 paired accessories x 3 sets (about 14-16 sets), 4-5 exercises | Priority compound as straight sets, everything else paired |
| 60 min | 2 compounds x 3-4 sets + 2 isolations x 3 sets (about 14 sets), 4 exercises | 2 compounds + 3-4 paired accessories (about 18-20 sets), 5-6 exercises | Typical upper/lower or full-body day |
| 90 min | 3 compounds x 3-4 sets + 3 isolations x 3 sets (about 20 sets), 6 exercises | 3 compounds + 4-5 paired accessories (about 24-26 sets), 7-8 exercises | Body-part or push/pull/legs day |

Per-muscle ceiling: the spine caps a session at about 8-10 hard sets for one muscle; the Pelland per-session plateau of about 11 fractional sets agrees. Extra weekly volume goes on another day, which is the whole reason the 5-6-day rotation exists (see chapter 03).

How this changes with available days:

- 3-day week: every muscle twice, 3-5 sets per muscle per session (spine). That means more exercises per session, so plan on 60-90 min and pair every accessory. A 45-min session in a 3-day week covers the main lifts only and drops accessories first (spine rule 4).
- 5-6-day week: 5-8 sets per muscle per session (spine), fewer muscles per session, so 45-60 min sessions are normal and straight sets are affordable.

### 6.3 Superset and paired-set rules

1. Keep the heaviest compound of the day as straight sets to protect load.
2. Pair antagonists (chest/back, biceps/triceps, quads/hamstrings) or unrelated muscles (calves with lateral raises, abs with rear delts). Never pair two exercises for the same muscle if the goal is to save time without losing reps.
3. Use 60 s between the pair's two exercises and 2 min between pairs.
4. RPE caveat: supersets raise RPE and lactate at the same external work (Weakley). Rate RIR on each exercise against its own last set, not against how the whole pair felt, and expect to need 1 extra minute between pairs when both exercises are compounds. Do not let the higher session RPE push a progression decision; progression follows the reps-at-load rules in chapter 03.
5. Per-muscle set counting is unchanged. A paired set is still one hard set for each muscle involved.

## 7. Substitution rules and travel fallbacks

### 7.1 Evidence

- Free weights vs machines, 13 studies, 1,016 adults: free-weight training gave greater free-weight strength (SMD -0.210, 95% CI -0.391 to -0.029, p = 0.023); machine training tended toward greater machine strength (SMD 0.291, 95% CI -0.017 to 0.600, p = 0.064); no significant difference in hypertrophy or jump performance (Haugen et al. 2023, BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation, https://doaj.org/article/a7282dba5e674176bde2a8239832d2f7). Stronger By Science notes the non-significant machine result may partly reflect fewer machine studies (6 vs 8) (https://www.strongerbyscience.com/free-weights-vs-machines/).
- Strength is ROM- and exercise-specific: full squats transferred to full, parallel and half 1RM better than partial training (Pallares 2019, 53 trained men, 10 weeks; Kubo 2019; both via Barbell Medicine and Stronger By Science, secondary). Preacher curl gave larger strength gains on the preacher test than incline curl (PubMed 39809454).
- Lat exercise variants change prime-mover EMG only a little, so pulldown, row and pull-up variants are broadly interchangeable for the lats (Lehman 2004).

### 7.2 Rules

1. Match the pattern: squat for squat, hinge for hinge, horizontal push for horizontal push.
2. Match the muscle length where the exercise is hardest. Replace a seated leg curl with an RDL, not a prone curl; replace an overhead cable extension with a dumbbell overhead extension, not a pushdown.
3. Match effort, not load. Keep the same RIR and rep range. Absolute load will change; treat the substitute as a new baseline. The research note did not source any load-conversion ratios between implements (the often-quoted "DB bench is about 35-40% of barbell load per hand" is a practitioner rule, unverified).
4. For strength goals, keep the actual main lift at least once a week, because strength is modality- and ROM-specific (Haugen 2023; Pallares 2019). Substitute accessories or travel weeks, not the lift that carries a progression track.
5. For hypertrophy goals, substitute freely among barbell, dumbbell, machine and cable (Haugen 2023 found no hypertrophy difference).

How this changes with available days: a 3-day week is where rule 4 bites hardest. With only three sessions, every main lift appears at most once or twice, so a substituted main lift means a missed exposure on its progression track. In a 5-6-day week a travel-day substitution costs little because the lift comes round again within days. Chapter 03 says how the rotation handles a skipped exposure.

### 7.3 Substitution matrix

Progress bodyweight versions by adding range, tempo, pauses, unilateral loading, or reps to near failure. The bodyweight column is a practitioner inference; the research note found no study of bodyweight-only travel periods on maintenance.

| Barbell | Dumbbell | Machine / cable | Bodyweight / travel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Back squat | Goblet squat, DB front squat | Hack squat, pendulum squat, leg press | Bulgarian split squat, pistol or box pistol, sissy squat, 1.5-rep air squat |
| Front squat | DB front-rack squat | Hack squat (upright), leg extension | Heel-elevated narrow squat, wall sit + step-ups |

## 8. Variation policy by exercise tier

Evidence (research note KQ8):

- Systematic review, 8 studies, n = 241 young men: some systematic variation enhances regional hypertrophy and dynamic strength; excessive random variation, redundant exercises, or very frequent changes may hinder adaptation (Kassiano et al. 2022, JSCR 36(6):1753-1762; Biolayne REPS issue 7 notes the small evidence base).
- Volume-equated MRI study, 49 men: groups that varied exercises grew all four quadriceps heads while constant-exercise groups did not grow some heads; overall CSA gains did not differ (Fonseca et al. 2014, pre-2016 foundational work).
- 21 trained men, 8 weeks, fixed vs app-randomized exercise selection each session: similar bench and squat 1RM gains and similar vastus lateralis and rectus femoris growth; vastus intermedius grew significantly only in the fixed group (no between-group difference); the varied group's intrinsic motivation rose (moderate effect) (Baz-Valle et al. 2019, PLoS ONE 14(12), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6934277).
- Strength gains are exercise- and ROM-specific (Nunes 2021; Kubo/Pallares; preacher vs incline), which argues for keeping the lifts you test fixed.

| Tier | Examples | Rotation rule | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary strength lifts | Squat, bench press, deadlift, overhead press, the main row, the main pull-up/pulldown | Fixed for a 4-12 week block. Change variants (pause squat, close-grip bench) only at a block boundary, and only one at a time | These carry the progression tracks (spine rule 2); strength is specific to the exercise and ROM |
| Hypertrophy compounds | Flat and incline press; back squat and leg press; RDL and hip thrust | 1-2 variants per pattern per week, constant within a block | Regional growth differs by variant (incline vs flat; deep vs half squat) |
| Isolation / accessory | Curls, triceps extensions, lateral and rear-delt raises, leg curls, calf raises | Rotate every 4-8 weeks or when double progression stalls. Swap for a variant that changes the region or the muscle length (preacher <-> incline curl; seated curl <-> RDL), not a redundant one | Kassiano 2022; Fonseca 2014 |
| Session-to-session random choice | Picking a different curl each day | Allowed for motivation; expect no growth advantage and possibly blunted growth in some regions | Baz-Valle 2019 |

No study defines an optimal rotation interval in weeks; the 4-12 week figure follows block-length conventions. All variation studies are in young men.

How this changes with available days: variation policy is per block, not per week. A 3-day week inside a block keeps the same exercises as the 5-6-day weeks and simply drops accessories (spine rule 4); it is not a reason to change exercises. Templates in `templates/` list which accessories are cut first.

## 9. Technique cue sheet

Coaching sources converge on a short list of faults per lift and recommend giving a lifter one or two cues at a time (research note KQ9). None of this is trial evidence: no study links a specific cue to injury risk or long-term performance, and the Stronger By Science and Barbell Medicine technique guides could not be fetched when the note was compiled. Items marked * are standard coaching practice without a retrieved citation, exactly as the research note marks them. The entire pull-up row is marked * because no pull-up technique source surfaced.

| Lift | Setup cues | Execution cues | Common faults -> fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | Bar on traps (high) or rear delts (low); stance shoulder-width or wider, toes 15-30 degrees out*; tripod foot (big toe, little toe, heel) | Big breath, brace 360 degrees*; knees track over toes; sit between the hips; full depth; drive the floor away with chest up | Hips shoot up -> knees forward early in the ascent, big-toe pressure (Breaking Muscle); knee cave -> "spread the floor"*, lighter load; forward lean -> tighter upper back; weight on heels only -> tripod foot (Livestrong); wobbling descent -> "confident hips" (Juggernaut Training Systems) |
| Bench press | Shoulder blades retracted and depressed; arch from thoracic extension, not the lumbar spine; feet planted for leg drive; grip about 1.5x shoulder width* | Bar to lower sternum; slight inverted-J path (toward the face, then up), not vertical; forearms vertical at the bottom*; drive legs into the floor; press back toward the face | Straight-up bar path -> cue the J-path (Vitruve); shoulders roll forward or sternum flattens -> re-pin the scapulae, restore leg drive (PowerliftingTechnique); butt lifts -> lower the arch or move the feet; bouncing -> pause on the chest (FitnessVolt; Stronger By Science bench outline) |
| Deadlift | Bar over midfoot*; hips higher than knees, lower than shoulders*; lats engaged, slack pulled out of the bar*; neutral spine | Push the floor away; bar stays on the legs; hips and shoulders rise together; finish with the glutes, not lumbar hyperextension* | Hips shoot up -> patience off the floor, leg-drive cue; rounding -> brace, lighter load; bar drifts forward -> lats tight; jerking the bar -> pull the slack out first |
| Overhead press | Grip just outside the shoulders; forearms vertical; glutes and abs braced; pelvis stacked under the ribs | Pull the chin back; bar straight up past the face; head through at lockout; tall chest (thoracic extension rather than a rigid neutral spine) | Excessive back-lean -> brace glutes and abs, cue tall chest (BarBend); bar loops forward -> chin back (Powerlifting Technique newsletter; Breaking Muscle); elbows flare* -> forearms vertical |
| Row (bent-over) | Hinge to a 45-75 degree torso; neutral spine; bar below the knees | Elbows drive past the torso; pull to lower chest or upper abdomen; squeeze the shoulder blades at the top | Torso swing -> lighter load or a chest-supported variant; lumbar rounding -> raise the torso angle, brace (CSCS technique standard) |
| Pull-up* | Shoulder-width or slightly wider grip; full hang with scapulae depressed | Start by pulling the shoulder blades down; drive elbows to the ribs; chin over the bar; control the eccentric | Kipping or half ROM -> band assistance to full ROM; shrugging -> depress the scapulae first |
| Romanian deadlift | Soft knees; bar against the thighs; lats tight | Push hips back until the hamstrings stretch; bar glides down the thighs; spine neutral; return by driving the hips forward | Squatting the RDL -> knees fixed, hips back; rounding -> stop at the hamstring stretch; bar drifts away -> lats, bar on thighs (Acibadem RDL article; secondary) |

Squat depth note: full-depth squats are safe, grew glutes and adductors more than half squats, and transferred better to all tested depths (Stronger By Science squat-depth review; Barbell Medicine; secondary). Depth is limited by mobility and by the per-rep loading, not by a safety rule.
