# 05. Exercise selection and session design

Last updated: 2026-10-21 (section 5 ramp shortened the same day)

> **Revision 2026-10-21.** Brought in line with the primary-source check in `research/exercise-selection-and-session-design.md`, section "Addendum 2026-10-21: primary-source verification" (Corrections, New evidence, Implications for the guide). Main changes: lateral raise cued with neutral rotation; overhead press credited with medial-delt activation; overhead extension made the default for all triceps heads; the long-length rule restated with pooled data; soleus, calf-partial, squat-depth, exercise-order, rest, warm-up, superset and free-weight evidence corrected; a unilateral-substitution rule added (section 7.2); several citations upgraded from secondary to primary or renamed to the correct first author.

This chapter answers: which exercises to put in a session, in what order, with what rest, warm-up, and time budget, and what to swap in when the equipment or the week changes. It does not lay out the week. The workout rotation, the per-exercise progression tracks, and the weekly set targets live in [chapter 03](03-flexible-scheduling.md) and the `templates/` folder. Nothing here overrides those.

Evidence base: `research/exercise-selection-and-session-design.md` (KQ1-KQ9). Two caveats apply throughout, and the research note makes both.

- Most "best exercise" claims rest on single small trials (n = 14-63, often untrained or women-only) or on EMG, and EMG has repeatedly failed to predict growth (Plotkin 2023; the incline/flat pec data). Treat exercise-specific effect sizes as low-to-moderate confidence unless a row says otherwise.
- Several primary texts could not be fetched when the note was compiled. The 2026-10-21 addendum verified most of them against the primary record; items still resting on abstracts, preprints or secondary reviews are flagged "secondary" or "unverified" below.

Program-wide defaults from the design spine that this chapter builds on: rest 2-3 min on compounds and 60-90 s on isolation; 0-3 reps in reserve (RIR) on working sets with 1-2 RIR the default on compounds; main-lift strength work at 3-6 reps and about 75-90% 1RM; hypertrophy work at 6-12 reps (up to 15-20 on isolation) as long as sets are hard.

## 1. Movement-pattern taxonomy: what each pattern covers and what it misses

Build every session from these patterns. The "missed" column is the reason isolation work exists in this program. Cells without a citation are standard anatomy (see research note KQ1, Inferences).

| Pattern | Representative exercises | Prime movers covered | Muscles NOT reliably covered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat (knee-dominant, bilateral) | Back squat, front squat, hack squat, leg press | Quadriceps, adductors, gluteus maximus; glute and adductor coverage rises with depth (Kubo et al. 2019, Eur J Appl Physiol) | Biarticular hamstrings and rectus femoris (neither grew at either squat depth in Kubo 2019), calves, side and rear delts |
| Hinge (hip-dominant) | Deadlift, Romanian deadlift (RDL), good morning, hip thrust | Glutes, hamstrings (RDL and glute-ham raise gave the highest hamstring EMG; McAllister et al. 2014, JSCR, pre-2016; the exact biceps femoris values and units in the research note are unverified), erectors | Quads (hip thrust, RDL), calves |
| Horizontal push | Flat and incline bench press, push-up, dips, machine chest press | Pectoralis major (incline biases the clavicular head; Chaves et al. 2020, Int J Exerc Sci), anterior deltoid, triceps lateral/medial heads | Medial deltoid (bench 5% MVIC vs 30% for a lateral raise; Campos et al. 2020) and posterior deltoid; triceps long head (Maeo et al. 2023) |
| Horizontal pull | Barbell, dumbbell, cable, and chest-supported rows | Lats, mid-traps/rhomboids (Lehman et al. 2004, Dynamic Medicine, pre-2016, isometric EMG), rear delts (45-degree incline row; Sweeney thesis), biceps, forearms | Biceps at long muscle length; lower lats biased more by vertical pulls |
| Vertical push | Overhead press, dumbbell shoulder press, landmine press | Anterior deltoid, upper traps, triceps; medial deltoid about as active as in a lateral raise (shoulder press 27.9% vs raise 30.3% MVIC; Campos et al. 2020, EMG only) | Posterior deltoid. No growth trial compares the press with raises for the medial delt |
| Vertical pull | Pull-up, chin-up, lat pulldown | Lats, teres major, biceps (more in chin-up), lower traps | Mid-traps/rhomboids (rows are better; Lehman 2004) |
| Lunge / single-leg | Split squat, walking lunge, step-up, single-leg RDL | Quads, glutes (including medius), adductors, hamstrings (single-leg RDL) | Calves |
| Carry | Farmer's walk, suitcase carry | Grip/forearms, traps, obliques, erectors | Nothing uniquely; no hypertrophy trials exist for carries |
| Core | Plank, cable crunch, hanging leg raise, Pallof press, back extension | Rectus abdominis, obliques, erectors | n/a |

Evidence behind the "missed" column (research note KQ1):

- Squat vs hip thrust, 9 weeks, volume-matched, untrained: squat grew quads (+3.6 ± 1.5 cm²) and adductors (+2.5 ± 0.7 cm²) more; glute growth was similar. Hamstrings were not a primary beneficiary of either (Plotkin et al. 2023, Frontiers in Physiology, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170/full). Authors declared product-related competing interests.
- Shoulder press and lateral raise both produced higher anterior and medial deltoid activation than bench press and dumbbell fly. Medial delt %MVIC: lateral raise 30.3, shoulder press 27.9, bench 5.0, fly 3.4; 13 trained men, 1 x 12 at 60% 1RM. Horizontal pressing does not meaningfully train the side delt; overhead pressing activates it about as much as a raise. EMG only (Campos et al. 2020, J Hum Kinet 75:5-14, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33312291/).
- Reverse pec deck produced much higher posterior deltoid activity than incline pulldown or seated row (Barbell Medicine EMG synthesis, https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/best-rear-delt-exercises/; practitioner source).
- Standing (knee-extended) calf raise grew lateral gastrocnemius 12.4% vs 1.7% and medial gastrocnemius 9.2% vs 0.6% compared with seated, 12 weeks, 14 untrained adults, within-subject, MRI. Soleus growth was similar: +2.1% standing vs +2.9% seated (p = 0.41) (Kinoshita, Maeo et al. 2023, Frontiers in Physiology, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1272106/full).

How this changes with available days: the patterns do not change. In a 3-day week every pattern still appears at least twice (spine rule 3, see chapter 03); what shrinks is the number of isolation additions per session, not the pattern coverage.

## 2. Commonly undertrained muscles and what to add

A compound-only program leaves the muscles below under-stimulated. Each addition is justified by the cited finding (research note KQ1, Inferences table). In a 3-day week, add these as the last 1-2 exercises of a session, paired to save time (section 6). In a 5-6-day week each gets its own slot on 2-3 days.

| Undertrained muscle | Why compounds miss it | Add (first choice) | Add (alternative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medial (side) deltoid | Horizontal presses give little medial-delt activation (bench 5% vs raise 30% MVIC; Campos 2020). The overhead press reached 28%, about the same as a raise, so raises are added for extra medial-delt volume at low fatigue cost, not because the press misses the muscle | Dumbbell or cable lateral raise, neutral humeral rotation (thumbs level, not up). Coratella et al. 2020 (IJERPH, EMG, 10 bodybuilders): concentric medial-delt activity was higher with neutral than external rotation (ES 1.47); the abstract's external-rotation conclusion conflicts with its own results. Low confidence | Lean-away cable raise, machine lateral raise, upright row to chest height |
| Posterior (rear) deltoid | Rows hit it only partially (Barbell Medicine; Sweeney thesis) | Reverse pec deck / rear-delt fly | Seated rear lateral raise, 45-degree incline row, face pull |
| Hamstrings (biarticular heads) | Squats grow them little; curls at short length grow less (Maeo 2021) | Seated leg curl (long length) | RDL or stiff-leg deadlift; Nordic curl (RDL matched Nordic for biceps femoris long-head architecture and size; Crawford et al. 2025, Med Sci Sports Exerc) |
| Gastrocnemius / soleus | No standard compound loads plantarflexion | Standing calf raise with a full stretch (Kinoshita 2023; Kassiano 2023) | Leg-press calf raise; seated calf raise as variety or for knee comfort, not for extra soleus growth (soleus grew equally standing and seated; Kinoshita 2023) |
| Biceps | Rows and chin-ups load them, but not at long length; regional growth differs by exercise (KQ2) | Incline dumbbell curl (proximal) + preacher curl (distal) | Bayesian cable curl, chin-up |
| Triceps (long head most) | Pressing and pushdowns keep the shoulder neutral; overhead extension grew the long head more and the lateral and medial heads more too (Maeo et al. 2023) | Overhead cable or dumbbell extension | Skull crusher with arms angled back |
| Upper chest (clavicular pec) | Flat-only pressing left the upper pec behind (Chaves et al. 2020, Int J Exerc Sci) | 30-degree incline press | Low-to-high cable fly |
| Mid-traps / rhomboids | Vertical pulls and presses do not load scapular retraction (Lehman 2004) | Seated or chest-supported row | Face pull, reverse fly |
| Adductors / glute max | Partial-depth squats (Kubo 2019) | Squat to full depth | Adductor machine, sumo deadlift, Cossack squat |

Gaps flagged by the research note: no longitudinal trial compares lateral raise with overhead press for medial-delt growth, and no hypertrophy trial exists for any rear-delt exercise or for carries. Those rows rest on EMG and expert consensus.

| Erectors | Deadlift, back extension, good morning | Standard practice | Low | Deadlift -> DB RDL -> 45-degree back extension -> reverse hyper, bird dog |
| Abdominals / obliques | Cable crunch, hanging leg raise, Pallof press, carries | Standard practice | Low | Weighted sit-up -> DB side bend -> cable crunch, ab wheel -> plank, dead bug, hollow hold |

The one rule that falls out of this table (research note KQ2, Inferences): when choosing between two exercises for the same muscle, pick the one that loads the target muscle at long length (seated over prone curl, overhead over neutral-arm triceps, incline over preacher for proximal biceps, standing over seated calf, deep over shallow squat). How big the advantage is depends on how much the swap changes muscle length:

- Single trials show large effects, mostly on two-joint muscles: seated curl (Maeo 2021), overhead triceps (Maeo 2023), standing calf (Kinoshita 2023) and reclined leg extension (Larsen 2025), each about 1.4-1.6x the relative growth of the shorter-length option.
- Pooled across all muscle-length manipulations, the average effect is trivial to small. Varović et al. 2025 (Int J Sports Med 46(14):1027-1036, Bayesian, 12 studies): regional SMDs 0.05-0.09, mostly inside the practical-equivalence region; the average length contrast was only 21.8%. Wolf et al. 2023 (Int J Strength Cond 3(1), doi:10.47206/ijsc.v3i1.182, 24 studies): full vs partial ROM hypertrophy SMD 0.05.
- Long-length partials beat short-length partials (Strey et al. 2026, Sport Sci Health 22:33, 8 studies, ES 0.28, 95% CI 0.04-0.52, mostly quadriceps).
- Lengthened partials are roughly equal to full ROM, not better. Wolf 2023 long-length-partial subgroup: SMD -0.28, 95% CI -0.81 to 0.16 (imprecise). Wolf et al. 2025 (PeerJ 13:e18904; 30 trained adults, within-subject, upper body): similar growth and strength. McMahon et al. 2026 (JSCR ahead of print; knee extension, n = 45): lengthened partials at 55% 1RM matched full ROM at 80% 1RM and beat shortened partials.

So: pick the long-length variant when it changes muscle length a lot, mainly for two-joint muscles, and do not expect a universal 1.5x. The default is full ROM with a hard stretch. Long-length partials are an acceptable alternative or last-set extension; short-length partials are not. Static stretching is no substitute for loaded long-length work: 42 studies gave a small hypertrophy effect (d = 0.20), and only at high durations and frequencies (Warneke et al. 2024, Sports Med Open 10:45).

## 4. Exercise order, rest, and tempo by goal

### 4.1 Order

A meta-analysis of 11 studies found no pooled order effect across all strength tests (ES -0.11, p = 0.306) and no effect on hypertrophy (ES 0.03, p = 0.862). The effect is specific: the exercise done first gains the most strength. Multi-joint strength favoured multi-joint-first (ES 0.32, p = 0.034); single-joint strength favoured single-joint-first (ES -0.58, p = 0.032) (Nunes et al. 2021, European Journal of Sport Science 21(2):149-157, online 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/).

Order template for any session (research note KQ3):

1. Priority or weak-point lift, fresh. This is the one lift whose strength or size you most want to move.
2. Primary heavy compound for the day (squat, hinge, press, or pull), if different from item 1.
3. Secondary compound (opposite pattern or unilateral).
4. Isolation work for the undertrained muscles in section 2. These tolerate fatigue and short rest.

Because hypertrophy is order-insensitive, putting an isolation exercise first for a lagging muscle costs no growth and improves strength on that exercise. The trade-off is strength on whatever moves later, so do not put it ahead of a main lift whose track you are pushing. The main lifts keep their progression tracks regardless of slot (spine rule 2), but a main lift placed later in a session will feel heavier at the same load; see chapter 03 for how the progression handles a missed rep target.

How this changes with available days: in a 3-day week items 1-3 are usually two main lifts plus one secondary compound, and item 4 is 1-2 paired isolations. In a 5-6-day week a session can be a single main lift plus three or four accessories, and weak-point priority rotates across days.

### 4.2 Rest and tempo

Evidence (research note KQ4):

- 1-min vs 3-min rest, 21 trained men, 8 weeks, 3 x 8-12 to failure: 3-min group gained significantly more squat and bench 1RM and more anterior-thigh thickness; triceps trend only (p = 0.06); elbow flexors not different; thickness measured at one site; 3 total-body sessions a week, 7 exercises (Schoenfeld et al. 2016, JSCR 30(7):1805-1812, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/). The viral "93% more growth" figure is not supported by the data.
- Systematic review of 6 studies: evidence equivocal, possible advantage for longer rest (> 60 s) in trained lifters (Grgic et al. 2017, EJSS 17(8):983-993).
- Bayesian meta-analysis, 9 studies, 19 measurements: pooled pre-post hypertrophy SMD 0.48 (95% CrI 0.19-0.81) with short rest vs 0.56 (0.24-0.86) with longer rest; controlled short-vs-longer rest SMDs were arm 0.13 (95% CrI -0.27 to 0.51), thigh 0.17 (-0.13 to 0.43), whole body -0.08 (-0.45 to 0.29). There was a small benefit from resting more than 60 s and **no appreciable hypertrophy difference beyond about 90 s**; training to failure or not did not change this. Short rest also reduced volume load (Singer, Wolf, Schoenfeld et al. 2024, Frontiers in Sports and Active Living 6:1429789, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205815/).
- Repetition duration: hypertrophy similar for anything from 0.5 to 8 s per rep; very slow (> 10 s) reps inferior (Schoenfeld, Ogborn, Krieger 2015, Sports Medicine 45(4):577-585, meta-analysis of 8 studies, pre-2016 foundational; the n = 239 figure is not in the abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25601394/). A PeerJ critique notes the included studies varied load and failure status, so the result cannot be attributed to tempo alone.
- Antagonist paired sets: 1-min rest between the paired exercises gave significantly greater training efficiency than 2 or 3 min; 2 min was a balance (paired-set rest study, https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4075858).

| Goal / exercise type | Rest between straight sets | Tempo | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main-lift strength work (3-6 reps, ~75-90% 1RM) | 2-3 min default (spine rule 6); extend to 3-5 min for a near-maximal top set if the next set would otherwise lose reps | Controlled eccentric, explosive concentric | Schoenfeld 2016 strength advantage for 3 min over 1 min; load maintenance |
| Hypertrophy compound (6-12 reps) | 2-3 min on main lifts and heavy free-weight compounds; about 90 s or more acceptable on other compounds (machine press, leg press, cable row) when reps hold | 2-3 s eccentric, 1 s concentric, brief pause at the stretch | 2-3 min is for keeping load and reps across sets (Schoenfeld 2016 strength and thigh data), not for extra growth: Singer 2024 found no hypertrophy benefit beyond about 90 s. Any 0.5-8 s rep is fine |
| Hypertrophy isolation (10-20 reps) | 60-90 s; lean toward 90 s if reps drop sharply at 60 s | 2-3 s eccentric with a brief stretch pause | Singer 2024: small benefit above 60 s, none detectable beyond 90 s. Rest matters less when load and volume are easy to maintain |
| Antagonist paired sets | 60 s between the pair's two exercises, 2 min between pairs | As above | Maia et al. 2014 (acute, reps only, n = 15); paired-set rest study |
| Muscular endurance / conditioning | 30-60 s | Continuous | Standard practice; little hypertrophy evidence |

Do not prescribe 10-s reps. They cut load and were inferior in the 2015 meta-analysis.

How this changes with available days: main-lift rest does not shrink in a 3-day week; it stays 2-3 min. If time is short (a 3-day week or a 45-min session), pair accessories first (section 6), then cut rest on non-main-lift hypertrophy compounds to about 90 s, provided the rep target still holds. Never cut main-lift rest below 2 min.

## 5. Warm-up protocol

Evidence (research note KQ5):

- Stretching systematic review: immediately after stretching, performance changed by about -3.7% (static), +1.3% (dynamic), -4.4% (PNF). Static holds of 60 s or more per muscle produced larger deficits (-4.6%) than shorter holds (-1.1%). Stretching inside a warm-up followed by dynamic activity has limited performance effects (Behm et al. 2016, Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism, https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1360011143567622912).
- 5-s and 30-s static stretches inside a full sport-specific warm-up had no group-level performance effect in 20 athletes (Blazevich et al. 2016, ECSS abstract).
- Specific warm-up, 40 trained men, 3 x 6 at a training load of 80% 1RM after each of three warm-ups: 1 x 6 at 40% plus 1 x 6 at 80% of the training load (two-set), 1 x 6 at 80% only, or 1 x 6 at 40% only. Squat mean propulsive velocity was higher after the single 80% set than after the single 40% set. In the bench, the two-set 40%/80% warm-up gave shorter time to peak velocity and more work than the single 40% set. Prior work found about +3% upper-body power after specific bench warm-ups (Ribeiro et al. 2020, IJERPH 17(18):6882, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7558980/).
- Review of 11 studies: maximal strength and reps can be positively affected when the specific warm-up uses loads close to maximum; some studies found no difference between a specific warm-up and none (Motricidade 2021 review).
- Heavier warm-up sets can potentiate the following working sets in trained lifters (Biolayne REPS issue 29, secondary).
- Time-efficient-training review: use exercise-specific warm-ups only, and stretch only if flexibility is a goal (Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld, Fimland 2021, Sports Medicine 51(10):2079-2095, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/). This is why general cardio is optional below.

### 5.1 The protocol

| Step | What | Time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| General | 3-5 min light cardio or dynamic movement | 3-5 min | Optional for a well-trained lifter in a warm gym; no study isolates its effect from the specific warm-up, and Iversen 2021 recommends exercise-specific warm-ups only |
| Dynamic mobility | 4-6 movements for the day's patterns (leg swings, hip openers, band pull-aparts, arm circles, bodyweight squats) | 2-3 min | Dynamic stretching: +1.3% (Behm 2016) |
| Optional static holds | Ankles or hips for squat depth, pecs or lats for pressing | Under 30 s per muscle | Follow with dynamic work. Never hold 60 s or more before lifting (Behm 2016; Blazevich 2016) |
| Ramp-up sets, first compound only | Empty bar (45 lb / 20 kg) x 5, then 80% x 2-3, then working sets. Add 60% x 3 between them once the top set is 225 lb (about 102 kg) or more | 2-3 min | Follows the two-set structure of Ribeiro 2020 (one light set, one near the working load). Percentages are of the day's top working load (the V load on a volume day), not 1RM |
| Later exercises | One feeder set at about 60-70% | 1 min each | Enough once the body is warm |

The 225-lb rule: when the top set reaches 225 lb (about 102 kg), the jump from the bar to 80% is large (45 to 180 lb), so a 60% x 3 step goes between them. This step is coaching convention, not evidence; no retrieved study tests it (research note KQ5, addendum).

Rounding: round each ramp load to the nearest loadable step (5 lb with 2.5-lb plates); an exact half-step rounds up, and no set goes below the empty bar. Example at a 135-lb squat: 45 x 5, 110 x 2 (80% = 108, rounds to 110).

Total: about 5-6 min before the first working set of the day (general and mobility plus the short ramp), which is the figure used in the time budgets in section 6. The earlier four-step ramp made this about 8 min.

Foam rolling, if used, belongs here rather than after training: pre-exercise rolling improved flexibility about 4% with negligible effects on strength (see chapter 06, section 6).

How this changes with available days: the warm-up is identical in 3-day and 6-day weeks. In a 45-min session, cut general cardio first, never the 80% set.

## 6. Session design: what fits in 45, 60, and 90 minutes

### 6.1 Evidence

- Superset vs traditional-set meta-analysis, 19 studies, 313 participants: acutely, similar total reps (SMD -0.03, p = 0.92) and volume load (SMD 0.05, p = 0.86) with shorter sessions and higher training efficiency (SMD 1.74, p = 0.01), at the cost of higher blood lactate and RPE (SMD 0.77). Chronically, strength (SMD 0.10, p = 0.36) and hypertrophy (SMD -0.05, p = 0.87) did not differ. The pairing matters: agonist-antagonist supersets increased reps (SMD 0.68), while same-muscle ("similar biomechanical") supersets cut volume load (SMD -1.08). Creatine kinase did not differ (SMD 0.22, p = 0.36) despite the conclusion's mention of more muscle damage (Zhang, Weakley, Li, Li, García-Ramos 2025, Sports Medicine 55(4):953-975, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39903375/).
- Supersets of multi-joint lifts (leg press, bench, pulldown, seated row), RCT, 10 weeks, 2x/week: superset training gave a smaller pulldown 1RM gain (-5.2 kg, p = 0.033) and a near-significant smaller row gain (-4.8 kg, p = 0.073); leg press, bench and body-composition changes were similar (Iversen et al. 2024, JSCR 38(8):1372-1378, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39072654/). Strength (S) cost, no hypertrophy (H) difference.
- One trial finished sessions in 36% less time with supersets, with similar strength and hypertrophy gains; 43 trained men and women, 8 weeks (SportRxiv preprint "Less time, same gains", unverified at primary: the preprint server could not be reached; details from a repository record and search snippets).
- Agonist-antagonist paired sets: more knee-extension reps after knee flexion with 0, 30 s or 1 min rest than in a traditional protocol; 3- and 5-min rest gave no rep advantage. Acute single-session study, 15 recreationally trained men, reps and EMG only (Maia, Willardson, Paz, Miranda 2014, JSCR 28(9):2529-2535, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25148302/). A brief review calls paired-set training efficacious and efficient for strength and power (Robbins et al. 2010), though a 20-man study found the intensity used may not increase target-muscle performance (RBCM study).
- Per-session volume meta-regression: returns plateau around 11 fractional sets per muscle per session for hypertrophy and about 2 direct sets per session for strength (Remmert, Pelland, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos, "Is There Too Much of a Good Thing?", SportRxiv preprint, doi:10.51224/srxiv.537, not peer-reviewed; the values are confirmed by the FAU press release and the authors' OSF data files, `research/frequency-volume-intensity-evidence.md`, addendum 2026-10-21). This is not the Pelland et al. weekly-volume paper, which is published separately (Pelland et al. 2026, Sports Medicine 56(2):481-505, doi:10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w).

### 6.2 Time budget

Arithmetic from the research note (KQ6, Inferences; these per-exercise times are inferences, not measured): a working set with setup takes about 45 s; add rest from section 4; about 5-6 min warm-up (section 5.1; 8 min before the ramp was shortened); about 3 min transitions. A straight-set compound with 2.5-min rest costs about 3.2 min per set; an isolation set with 75-s rest about 2 min; an antagonist paired set (two exercises, 60 s between, 2 min after the pair) about 4.5 min per pair, i.e. about 2.25 min per set.

| Session length | Straight sets only | With antagonist or unrelated-muscle supersets for accessories | Suggested structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 45 min | 1 compound x 4 sets + 2 isolations x 3 sets (about 10 hard sets), 3 exercises | 1-2 compounds x 3-4 sets + 2-3 paired accessories x 3 sets (about 14-16 sets), 4-5 exercises | Priority compound as straight sets, everything else paired |
| 60 min | 2 compounds x 3-4 sets + 2 isolations x 3 sets (about 14 sets), 4 exercises | 2 compounds + 3-4 paired accessories (about 18-20 sets), 5-6 exercises | Typical upper/lower or full-body day |
| 90 min | 3 compounds x 3-4 sets + 3 isolations x 3 sets (about 20 sets), 6 exercises | 3 compounds + 4-5 paired accessories (about 24-26 sets), 7-8 exercises | Body-part or push/pull/legs day |

Per-muscle ceiling: the spine caps a session at about 8-10 hard sets for one muscle; the per-session plateau of about 11 fractional sets in the Remmert et al. preprint (unpublished; values confirmed by the FAU press release and the authors' data files) agrees. Extra weekly volume goes on another day, which is the whole reason the 5-6-day rotation exists (see chapter 03).

How this changes with available days:

- 3-day week: every muscle twice, 3-5 sets per muscle per session (spine). That means more exercises per session, so plan on 60-90 min and pair every accessory. A 45-min session in a 3-day week covers the main lifts only and drops accessories first (spine rule 4).
- 5-6-day week: 5-8 sets per muscle per session (spine), fewer muscles per session, so 45-60 min sessions are normal and straight sets are affordable.

### 6.3 Superset and paired-set rules

1. Keep the heaviest compound of the day as straight sets to protect load. Never superset a heavy (H) top set. Supersetting multi-joint lifts cost some pulldown strength in a 10-week trial (-5.2 kg; Iversen 2024). If two main lifts are alternated as paired sets, do it for volume (V) work only, and keep about 3.5 min between sets of the same lift.
2. Pair antagonists (chest/back, biceps/triceps, quads/hamstrings) or unrelated muscles (calves with lateral raises, abs with rear delts). Never pair two exercises for the same muscle if the goal is to save time without losing reps. Antagonist pairs kept or added reps (SMD 0.68); same-muscle pairs lost volume load (SMD -1.08) (Zhang et al. 2025).
3. Use 60 s between the pair's two exercises and 2 min between pairs.
4. RPE caveat: supersets raise RPE (SMD 0.77) and lactate at the same external work (Zhang et al. 2025). Rate RIR on each exercise against its own last set, not against how the whole pair felt, and expect to need 1 extra minute between pairs when both exercises are compounds. Do not let the higher session RPE push a progression decision; progression follows the reps-at-load rules in chapter 03.
5. Per-muscle set counting is unchanged. A paired set is still one hard set for each muscle involved.

## 7. Substitution rules and travel fallbacks

### 7.1 Evidence

- Free weights vs machines, 13 studies, 1,016 adults: free-weight training gave greater free-weight strength (SMD -0.210, 95% CI -0.391 to -0.029, p = 0.023); machine training tended toward greater machine strength (SMD 0.291, 95% CI -0.017 to 0.600, p = 0.064); no significant difference in hypertrophy or jump performance (Haugen et al. 2023, BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37582807/). The hypertrophy null rests on only **5 of the 13 studies** (SMD -0.055, 95% CI -0.397 to 0.287, p = 0.751), so treat it as moderate, not high, confidence. Stronger By Science notes the non-significant machine result may partly reflect fewer machine studies (6 vs 8) (https://www.strongerbyscience.com/free-weights-vs-machines/).
- Strength is ROM- and exercise-specific. Full squats were the only training to raise 1RM at full, parallel and half depth; half squats gave no gains and more pain and stiffness (Pallares et al. 2020, Eur J Sport Sci 20(1):115-124, 53 trained men, 10 weeks). Full-squat training raised full-squat 1RM more than half-squat training (+31.8% vs +11.3%, p = 0.003), but half-squat training did **not** significantly raise half-squat 1RM more (+32.0% vs +24.2%, p = 0.132) (Kubo et al. 2019). Curl strength was specific in both directions: each group gained more on its own curl's 3RM (Kassiano et al. 2025, Int J Sports Med).
- Unilateral vs bilateral, meta-analysis of 9 studies (moderate risk of bias): no hypertrophy difference (ES -0.21, 95% CI -3.56 to 3.13, p = 0.57). Strength was specific: bilateral training raised bilateral strength more (ES 0.56), unilateral training raised unilateral strength more (ES -0.65) (Kassiano et al. 2025, Sports Medicine 55(4):923-936, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39794667/).
- Lat exercise variants change prime-mover EMG only a little, so pulldown, row and pull-up variants are broadly interchangeable for the lats (Lehman 2004).

### 7.2 Rules

1. Match the pattern: squat for squat, hinge for hinge, horizontal push for horizontal push.
2. Match the muscle length where the exercise is hardest. Replace a seated leg curl with an RDL, not a prone curl; replace an overhead cable extension with a dumbbell overhead extension, not a pushdown.
3. Match effort, not load. Keep the same RIR and rep range. Absolute load will change; treat the substitute as a new baseline. The research note did not source any load-conversion ratios between implements (the often-quoted "DB bench is about 35-40% of barbell load per hand" is a practitioner rule, unverified).
4. For strength goals, keep the actual main lift at least once a week, because strength is modality- and ROM-specific (Haugen 2023; Pallares 2020). Substitute accessories or travel weeks, not the lift that carries a progression track.
5. For hypertrophy goals, substitute freely among barbell, dumbbell, machine and cable (Haugen 2023 found no hypertrophy difference, in 5 studies; moderate confidence).
6. Unilateral swaps are fine for hypertrophy: split squat for leg press, single-arm row for cable row, single-leg RDL for RDL (Kassiano 2025, no hypertrophy difference). Strength is specific, so do not swap a bilateral main lift that has a progression track for a unilateral one (this is rule 4 again). The same finding supports the unilateral options in the bodyweight/travel column of section 7.3.

How this changes with available days: a 3-day week is where rule 4 bites hardest. With only three sessions, every main lift appears at most once or twice, so a substituted main lift means a missed exposure on its progression track. In a 5-6-day week a travel-day substitution costs little because the lift comes round again within days. Chapter 03 says how the rotation handles a skipped exposure.

### 7.3 Substitution matrix

Progress bodyweight versions by adding range, tempo, pauses, unilateral loading, or reps to near failure. The bodyweight column is a practitioner inference; the research note found no study of bodyweight-only travel periods on maintenance.

| Barbell | Dumbbell | Machine / cable | Bodyweight / travel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Back squat | Goblet squat, DB front squat | Hack squat, pendulum squat, leg press | Bulgarian split squat, pistol or box pistol, sissy squat, 1.5-rep air squat |
| Front squat | DB front-rack squat | Hack squat (upright), leg extension | Heel-elevated narrow squat, wall sit + step-ups |
| Deadlift | Heavy DB RDL, trap-bar deadlift | Hip thrust machine, 45-degree back extension, cable pull-through | Single-leg RDL (backpack), Nordic curl, glute bridge march |

## 8. Variation policy by exercise tier

Evidence (research note KQ8):

- Systematic review, 8 studies, n = 241 young men: some systematic variation enhances regional hypertrophy and dynamic strength; excessive random variation, redundant exercises, or very frequent changes may hinder adaptation (Kassiano et al. 2022, JSCR 36(6):1753-1762; Biolayne REPS issue 7 notes the small evidence base).
- Volume-equated MRI study, 49 physically active individuals: groups that varied exercises grew all four quadriceps heads while constant-exercise groups did not grow some heads; overall CSA gains did not differ (Fonseca et al. 2014, pre-2016 foundational work).
- 21 trained men, 8 weeks, fixed vs app-randomized exercise selection each session: similar bench and squat 1RM gains and similar vastus lateralis and rectus femoris growth; vastus intermedius grew significantly only in the fixed group (no between-group difference); the varied group's intrinsic motivation rose (moderate effect) (Baz-Valle et al. 2019, PLoS ONE 14(12), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6934277).
- Strength gains are exercise- and ROM-specific (Nunes 2021; Kubo 2019 and Pallares 2020; preacher vs incline, Kassiano 2025; unilateral vs bilateral, Kassiano 2025), which argues for keeping the lifts you test fixed.

| Tier | Examples | Rotation rule | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary strength lifts | Squat, bench press, deadlift, overhead press, the main row, the main pull-up/pulldown | Fixed for a 4-12 week block. Change variants (pause squat, close-grip bench) only at a block boundary, and only one at a time | These carry the progression tracks (spine rule 2); strength is specific to the exercise and ROM |
| Hypertrophy compounds | Flat and incline press; back squat and leg press; RDL and hip thrust | 1-2 variants per pattern per week, constant within a block | Regional growth differs by variant (incline vs flat; deep vs half squat) |
| Isolation / accessory | Curls, triceps extensions, lateral and rear-delt raises, leg curls, calf raises | Rotate every 4-8 weeks or when double progression stalls. Swap for a variant that changes the region or the muscle length (preacher <-> incline curl; seated curl <-> RDL), not a redundant one | Kassiano 2022; Fonseca 2014 |
| Session-to-session random choice | Picking a different curl each day | Allowed for motivation; expect no growth advantage and possibly blunted growth in some regions | Baz-Valle 2019 |

No study defines an optimal rotation interval in weeks; the 4-12 week figure follows block-length conventions. The variation studies are mostly in young men.

How this changes with available days: variation policy is per block, not per week. A 3-day week inside a block keeps the same exercises as the 5-6-day weeks and simply drops accessories (spine rule 4); it is not a reason to change exercises. Templates in `templates/` list which accessories are cut first.

## 9. Technique cue sheet

Coaching sources converge on a short list of faults per lift and recommend giving a lifter one or two cues at a time (research note KQ9). None of this is trial evidence: no study links a specific cue to injury risk or long-term performance, and the Stronger By Science and Barbell Medicine technique guides could not be fetched when the note was compiled. Items marked * are standard coaching practice without a retrieved citation, exactly as the research note marks them. The entire pull-up row is marked * because no pull-up technique source surfaced.

| Lift | Setup cues | Execution cues | Common faults -> fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | Bar on traps (high) or rear delts (low); stance shoulder-width or wider, toes 15-30 degrees out*; tripod foot (big toe, little toe, heel) | Big breath, brace 360 degrees*; knees track over toes; sit between the hips; full depth; drive the floor away with chest up | Hips shoot up -> knees forward early in the ascent, big-toe pressure (Breaking Muscle); knee cave -> "spread the floor"*, lighter load; forward lean -> tighter upper back; weight on heels only -> tripod foot (Livestrong); wobbling descent -> "confident hips" (Juggernaut Training Systems) |
| Bench press | Shoulder blades retracted and depressed; arch from thoracic extension, not the lumbar spine; feet planted for leg drive; grip about 1.5x shoulder width* | Bar to lower sternum; slight inverted-J path (toward the face, then up), not vertical; forearms vertical at the bottom*; drive legs into the floor; press back toward the face | Straight-up bar path -> cue the J-path (Vitruve); shoulders roll forward or sternum flattens -> re-pin the scapulae, restore leg drive (PowerliftingTechnique); butt lifts -> lower the arch or move the feet; bouncing -> pause on the chest (FitnessVolt; Stronger By Science bench outline) |
| Deadlift | Bar over midfoot*; hips higher than knees, lower than shoulders*; lats engaged, slack pulled out of the bar*; neutral spine | Push the floor away; bar stays on the legs; hips and shoulders rise together; finish with the glutes, not lumbar hyperextension* | Hips shoot up -> patience off the floor, leg-drive cue; rounding -> brace, lighter load; bar drifts forward -> lats tight; jerking the bar -> pull the slack out first |
| Overhead press | Grip just outside the shoulders; forearms vertical; glutes and abs braced; pelvis stacked under the ribs | Pull the chin back; bar straight up past the face; head through at lockout; tall chest (thoracic extension rather than a rigid neutral spine) | Excessive back-lean -> brace glutes and abs, cue tall chest (BarBend); bar loops forward -> chin back (Powerlifting Technique newsletter; Breaking Muscle); elbows flare* -> forearms vertical |
| Row (bent-over) | Hinge to a 45-75 degree torso; neutral spine; bar below the knees | Elbows drive past the torso; pull to lower chest or upper abdomen; squeeze the shoulder blades at the top | Torso swing -> lighter load or a chest-supported variant; lumbar rounding -> raise the torso angle, brace (CSCS technique standard) |
| Pull-up* | Shoulder-width or slightly wider grip; full hang with scapulae depressed | Start by pulling the shoulder blades down; drive elbows to the ribs; chin over the bar; control the eccentric | Kipping or half ROM -> band assistance to full ROM; shrugging -> depress the scapulae first |
| Romanian deadlift | Soft knees; bar against the thighs; lats tight | Push hips back until the hamstrings stretch; bar glides down the thighs; spine neutral; return by driving the hips forward | Squatting the RDL -> knees fixed, hips back; rounding -> stop at the hamstring stretch; bar drifts away -> lats, bar on thighs (Acibadem RDL article; secondary) |

Squat depth note: full-depth squats are safe, grew glutes and adductors more than half squats (Kubo 2019), and transferred better to all tested depths, while half squats brought more pain and stiffness (Pallares 2020). Half squats were not shown to be best even for half-squat strength (Kubo 2019, p = 0.132). Depth is limited by mobility and by the per-rep loading, not by a safety rule.
