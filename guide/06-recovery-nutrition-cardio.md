# 06. Recovery, fatigue management, sleep, nutrition, and cardio

Last updated: 2026-10-21

This chapter covers everything outside the lifting session that decides whether the program works: how long a muscle needs between hard sessions, how to tell a productive hard week from an unproductive one, sleep, food, supplements, cardio, and recovery modalities. It applies the rules to the two week types this program alternates between (3 sessions vs 5-6 sessions). The weekly layouts themselves live in [chapter 03](03-flexible-scheduling.md) and `templates/`; this chapter never prescribes its own.

Evidence base: `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` (KQ1-KQ7), plus the warm-up and stretching findings in `research/exercise-selection-and-session-design.md` (KQ5) for section 6. One caveat applies throughout and the research note makes it: full-text fetches of most primary papers were blocked when the note was compiled, so many figures come from abstracts, repository records, and secondary summaries (Examine, Stronger By Science, Biolayne REPS, MySportScience). Items known only through a secondary source are flagged "secondary". The note's search budget ran out before every sub-question was answered; the unanswered ones are listed as gaps in each section.

This is general training guidance, not medical advice. Section 8 says when to stop reading and see someone.

Units in this chapter: kilograms (kg), grams (g), kilocalories (kcal), hours (h).

## 1. Recovery between same-muscle sessions

### 1.1 The timeline

| Time after a hard session | What the data show | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 24 h | Volume load still depressed when the next session comes this soon | 14 trained males, sets of 8-10RM to concentric failure (PubMed 32594858, via nufind record, secondary) |
| 48 h | Mostly restored; first-set volume load still slightly down | Same study. Authors concluded at least 48 h was needed to re-establish performance |
| 72 h | Fully restored; jump height and isometric strength were above baseline | Same study |
| 24-48 h, non-failure training | Recovery significantly faster than after to-failure sets, even with total volume matched | Pareja-Blanco et al. 2017, Eur J Appl Physiol, 10 trained men (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/) |
| Out to 96 h, squat vs bench vs deadlift | Recovery time course was similar across the three lifts | Belcher et al. 2019, well-trained males, sets to failure at 80% 1RM (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30779596/) |

Pareja-Blanco's conclusion, quoted by the research note: "RT leading to failure considerably increases the time needed for the recovery of neuromuscular function and metabolic and hormonal homeostasis." This is why the program's default is 1-2 RIR on compounds (spine rule 6), not failure.

All of these are small primary studies (n = 10-14). The research note found no meta-analysis pooling recovery time by volume or exercise type.

### 1.2 Frequency does not matter when volume is equated

- Meta-analysis, 25 studies, 836 participants, 6-30 weeks: "resistance training frequency does not significantly or meaningfully impact muscle hypertrophy when volume is equated"; a frequency effect appeared only when volume was not equated (Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019; via Menno Henselmans summary and a 2021 Frontiers in Physiology review, secondary).
- 3 vs 6 sessions per week, volume-equated, resistance-trained men, 6 weeks: similar strength gains (Grgic et al. 2018, https://researchgate.net/profile/Jozo-Grgic/publication/327435932).
- A Grgic, Schoenfeld & Latella review found site-specific hypertrophy was generally similar for once-weekly vs 2-3x weekly training when volume was matched (ECU repository record).
- A 2024 Pelland et al. preprint (not peer reviewed) meta-regressed 67 studies; hypertrophy kept rising past 10 weekly sets per muscle (Biolayne REPS issue 31, secondary; https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908).

### 1.3 What this means for the two week types

| Week type | Implication | Spine / chapter 03 rule it supports |
|---|---|---|
| 3-day week | Each muscle is hit twice with 48-96 h between touches, which sits inside the full-recovery window. Sets can go to 1-2 RIR without compromising the next session | Spine: 3-day week, every muscle 2x, 3-5 sets/muscle/session |
| 5-6-day week | The extra days are for spreading volume thinner per session, not for adding full-fatigue sessions on consecutive days. Keep each muscle to 2-3 touches with about 48-72 h between hard sessions for the same muscle | Spine: 5-6-day week, 5-8 sets/muscle/session, every muscle 2-3x; chapter 03 orders the rotation so consecutive days alternate muscle groups |
| Same muscle on consecutive days (unavoidable in some 6-day rotations) | Stop 2-4 reps short of failure on the second day and keep that day's per-muscle volume at the low end of the range (5 sets, not 8), so the session is sub-maximal in fatigue cost | Research note KQ1, Inferences |
| Deadlift vs squat spacing | No evidence basis for a longer standalone recovery window after deadlifts when load and proximity to failure are similar (Belcher 2019). The driver is total systemic volume and how close to failure you went | Research note KQ1, Inferences |

Gap: a direct study of consecutive-day vs non-consecutive-day training for the same muscle (e.g. Yang et al. 2018) could not be searched. The consecutive-day rule above is an inference from the 24-h data and the failure-vs-non-failure data.

## 2. Fatigue monitoring: what to watch and what to ignore

### 2.1 Overreaching vs overtraining

- Scoping review of 47 studies: "short-term periods of OR achieved with either high-volume or high-intensity RT can elicit functional OR (FOR), but there is also evidence that chronic high-volume and/or intensity RT can lead to non-functional overreaching (NFOR). There is minimal evidence to suggest that true OTS has occurred in strength sports or RT." (Bell et al. 2020, J Sports Sci, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32602418/)
- Diagnostic criteria for FOR, NFOR and overtraining syndrome in resistance training are unstandardised, so the literature itself cannot say where the line is (Grandou et al. 2020, Sports Med, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31820373/).
- In a high-power squat overreaching block, bar velocity at 70% 1RM dropped and recovered within 1 week; daily questionnaires picked up changes in perceived recovery, mood and sleep (IJES abstract, WKU).
- Functional overreaching means improved capability in the days and weeks after a short-term increase in training demand (Bell et al. 2022, coaches' study).

| Term | What it is | Expected course | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional overreaching (FOR) | Planned short-term overload; performance dips then rebounds | Resolves in about 1 week of lighter training | This is what a hard 5-6-day week is supposed to produce. The following lighter week (a 3-day week or a deload per chapter 03) is the recovery |
| Non-functional overreaching (NFOR) | Performance stays down for weeks after the overload ends | Weeks | Cut volume (spine rule 4: accessories first), hold intensity, check sleep and food |
| Overtraining syndrome (OTS) | Months-long decrement with systemic symptoms | Months | Minimal evidence this occurs in lifting. If performance and mood have been down for more than a few weeks with adequate sleep and food, see a professional (section 8) |

### 2.2 Which signals are validated

Review of 90 studies on regulating and monitoring resistance training: barbell velocity and sessional, RIR-based, and post-set Borg RPE were strongly associated with training performance; velocity- and RIR-RPE-based intensity and volume modifications are "practical, reliable and show preliminary utility," while many physiological measures are lab-restricted (Greig et al. 2020, J Hum Kinet, https://johk.pl/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/10078-74-2020-v74-2020-03.pdf).

| Signal | Evidence quality for lifting | How to use it | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reps at a fixed load, or bar velocity at a fixed load | Strong | Log every main-lift top set. Two or more sessions in a row below the expected reps or speed is the first red flag | Greig 2020; squat overreaching study |
| RPE / RIR drift | Strong (RIR-based RPE associated with performance) | Same load and reps feeling 1+ RPE harder across several sessions. No validated threshold exists; use "two sessions in a row" as the working rule | Greig 2020; research note gap |
| Daily mood and perceived-recovery questionnaire | Moderate; "consistently described as an early marker of maladaptation" | A 1-5 rating each morning takes 10 s. A week-long downward trend counts | Kölling et al. 2018 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5928142/); squat overreaching study |
| Objective sleep efficiency (wearable) | Moderate, from endurance athletes | Overreaching reduced sleep efficiency by -2.0% (95% CI -3.2 to -0.8%); subjective sleep quality did not change. No included study was low risk of bias | 2024 PLoS ONE meta-analysis (DOAJ record, endurance athletes) |
| Subjective sleep complaints | Moderate | Trouble falling or staying asleep during a hard block is a marker whether cause or effect | Kölling 2018 |
| Heart rate variability (HRV) | Weak for lifting | Log it if you like, never decide on it alone. HRV-guided training improved HRV itself but effects on performance were "consistently small but non-significant" (endurance). "A lack of research connecting the effective utilization of HRV to guide training, specifically with strength training" | 2021 HRV-guided-training meta-analysis (Georgia Southern record); 2024 review PMC11204851; Alabama dissertation (secondary) |
| Blood or hormonal markers | Lab-restricted, not practical | Ignore for self-coaching | Greig 2020 |

### 2.3 The decision rule

Red flags, in order of evidence (research note KQ2, Inferences):

1. Rep performance or bar speed at a fixed load falls across two or more sessions.
2. RPE drifts up for the same prescribed load and reps.
3. Daily mood or perceived-recovery scores worsen.
4. Objective sleep efficiency worsens.

Any two together for a week means the heavy week has tipped past functional overreaching. The next week should be a 3-day week or a deload week, following chapter 03's rules (deload: keep frequency, halve sets, keep some heavy but easy work). Because FOR resolved within about a week in the squat overreaching study, alternating a 5-6-day week with a lighter 3-day week is a defensible structure, provided the heavy week's performance dip is small and reverses in the light week. If it does not reverse, treat it as NFOR.

Gaps: no resistance-specific threshold (percent of 1RM, percent velocity loss, RPE points) separates FOR from NFOR; deload-week trials (Coleman et al. 2024) could not be searched.

## 3. Sleep

### 3.1 Numbers

| Finding | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Maximal strength after sleep loss | -2.85% on average (77 studies) | Craven et al. 2022 meta-analysis, via Stronger By Science review (https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=57132) and a 2025 systematic review (PMC12263768); secondary |
| Strength endurance after sleep loss | -9.85%, from only five studies | Same |
| Muscle protein synthesis after one night of total sleep deprivation | -18% (0.072 vs 0.059 %/h, p = 0.040) | Lamon et al. 2021, Physiological Reports, randomized crossover, 13 young adults (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33400856/) |
| Cortisol / testosterone after the same night | +21% / -24% | Lamon 2021: "a single night of total sleep deprivation is sufficient to induce anabolic resistance and a procatabolic environment" |
| Acute and chronic sleep deprivation on strength, power, endurance | Significant reductions on some measures; mixed after a single night | 2025 systematic review, 13 studies (LJMU) |
| Injury risk, adolescents sleeping under 8 h | Adjusted risk ratio 1.7 (95% CI 1.0-3.0); self-reported data | Milewski et al. 2014 (pre-2016), via systematic review table PMC6446394 |
| Injury risk, adults | Current evidence does not establish insufficient sleep as an independent risk factor in adults | Quality in Sport review |

Flag: the common 7-9 h adult target cannot be derived from these studies. The research note says so explicitly. Consumer-site percentage tables linking sleep hours to injury (e.g. d1training.com) are unsourced and are not used here.

### 3.2 What to do with it

- Protect sleep most on the night before and the night after the heaviest sessions of a 5-6-day week. An 18% drop in protein synthesis after one bad night is the largest single recovery penalty in this chapter.
- After a short night, maximal strength is relatively robust (-3%) while strength endurance is not (-10%). So if the rotation's next workout is flexible within chapter 03's rules, a heavy low-rep main-lift session is a better choice than a high-rep volume session. Do not skip ahead in the rotation to do this; chapter 03 says what is allowed.
- Treat worsening sleep during a hard block as a fatigue signal (section 2), not as a separate problem.

Gaps: no trial of sleep extension on hypertrophy and no study linking habitual sleep duration to long-term gains in trained adults were found.

## 4. Nutrition

### 4.1 One-glance table

| Item | Target | Notes and confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protein, daily | 1.6-2.2 g/kg bodyweight/day | Supplementing beyond about 1.62 g/kg/day added no further fat-free mass in a 49-study meta-analysis, but that breakpoint was not itself significant (p = 0.079) and its 95% CI spanned 1.03-2.20 g/kg, so it is imprecise, not a ceiling. Use the top of the range in a deficit, in the heavy week, and in the 40s-50s (section 7) | Morton et al. 2018, BJSM (nutrition-evidence record); Stronger By Science critique (https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=61316); Nunes et al. 2022, J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle (PMC8978023): LBM effect SMD 0.22 (95% CI 0.14-0.30), significant at 1.6+ g/kg/day in adults under 65 |
| Protein, per meal | About 0.4 g/kg per meal, 4 or more meals per day; up to 0.55 g/kg per meal at the 2.2 g/kg end | For an 80-kg lifter: about 32 g per meal, 4 meals, 128+ g/day | Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018, JISSN (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430/) |
| Protein timing around training | Total daily intake matters far more than timing. Have the pre- and post-training meals within roughly 3-4 h of each other | A small timing effect for protein within 1 h post-exercise disappeared after adjusting for total protein; a follow-up RCT found pre- vs post-exercise protein equivalent; the "window" may stretch 5-6 h depending on the pre-workout meal | Schoenfeld, Aragon & Krieger 2013 meta-analysis (PMC3879660); Schoenfeld et al. 2017, PeerJ (https://peerj.com/articles/2825); Aragon & Schoenfeld 2013 |
| Pre-sleep protein | 30-40 g casein before bed | Acutely raises overnight muscle protein synthesis and metabolic rate without affecting fat breakdown; optional, counts toward the daily total | ISSN nutrient-timing position stand, Kerksick et al. 2017 (PMC5596471) |
| Calories for muscle gain | 10-20% surplus; aim for +0.25-0.5% bodyweight per week | Advanced lifters toward the lower end. A surplus of about 358-478 kcal/day (1,500-2,000 kJ) is called conservative | Iraki et al. 2019, Sports (PMC6680710); Examine FAQ (secondary); Slater et al. 2019, Frontiers in Nutrition |
| Calories for fat loss | Deficit near or under about 500 kcal/day | On average a 500 kcal/day deficit produced neither loss nor gain of lean mass; larger deficits cost lean mass roughly linearly but did not impair strength. Recomposition (gain muscle while losing fat) is most likely in beginners or overweight trainees, not lean intermediates | Murphy & Koehler 2021/2022, Scand J Med Sci Sports meta-analysis, via MySportScience, Examine and Biolayne REPS (secondary); Slater 2019 |
| Creatine monohydrate | 3-5 g/day, every day, training or not | Saturation in about 3-4 weeks at 3-5 g/day; optional load of 20 g/day in four doses for 5-7 days. The ISSN stand reports safety at up to 30 g/day for 5 years. The 3-5 g figure is from secondary summaries; the stand's full text was not retrieved | ISSN creatine position stand, Kreider et al. 2017, JISSN 14:18, via Exercise & Sport Nutrition Lab and MD Saude summaries (secondary) |
| Caffeine | 3-6 mg/kg before the heaviest sessions | "Consistently been shown to improve exercise performance" at this dose. For an 80-kg lifter: 240-480 mg. Timing (~60 min pre) and strength-specific effect sizes were not searched | ISSN caffeine position stand, Guest et al. 2021 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7777221/) |
| Carbohydrate | Not set here | Daily carbohydrate targets by training volume could not be retrieved from the ISSN nutrient-timing stand; gap | Research note KQ4, Gaps |

Supporting detail: a 105-article analysis (5,411 subjects) found lean body mass improved with as little as about 5 g/day of supplementary protein, rising further above 50 g/day (Tagawa et al. 2020, Nutrition Reviews, medRxiv preprint). The protein effect was smaller with age and larger in trained individuals (Morton 2018).

### 4.2 How calories change with the week type

| Week type | Calorie setting | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 5-6-day week | Maintenance or a 10-20% surplus | Highest weekly volume, highest recovery demand; data on trained lifters suggest a deficit would most likely impair gains (Slater 2019) |
| 3-day week | This is the week to run a deficit if fat loss is a goal; keep it near or under about 500 kcal/day | Lower volume means a smaller recovery penalty; a 500 kcal/day average deficit held lean mass flat in the meta-analysis (Murphy & Koehler) |
| Either week | Protein at 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day, 4+ feedings; creatine daily | Protein and creatine do not change with the schedule |

Flags and gaps: protein needs in a deficit for lean, trained lifters (Helms et al. 2014, 2.3-3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass) could not be searched and should be verified before relying on them; carbohydrate targets were not retrieved; caffeine timing and strength effect sizes (Grgic et al. 2018) were not searched.

### 4.3 Estimating maintenance and running a recomposition

Added 2026-10-21. Source: `research/body-recomposition.md` (KQ1, KQ2).

| Step | Rule | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Estimate maintenance | Resting rate (Mifflin-St Jeor, or Katch-McArdle if you have a lean-mass scan) times an activity factor of about 1.375-1.725; or 30-35 kcal/kg | Practitioner formulas; a starting point only |
| Correct it | Track intake for 2-3 weeks and adjust by the weekly-average bodyweight trend | Practitioner convention |
| Recomposition setting | A deficit of about 300-400 kcal/day averaged over the week, protein about 2.2 g/kg | Inference from Longland 2016 (2.4 g/kg protein kept lean mass rising in a deficit) and Murphy & Koehler (about 500 kcal/day held lean mass flat) |
| Who it suits | Returning lifters and people with recent fat gain respond fastest; lean, continuously trained lifters slowest | Barakat 2020 review (abstract); Halonen 2024 (faster regain in the first 5 weeks of retraining) |
| How long | Fastest in the first 2-3 months of retraining; after about 6 months switch to a deliberate surplus or deficit | Inference; no month-by-month trial found |

With the week types: put the larger deficit in 3-day weeks and keep 5-6-day weeks near maintenance, as in 4.2. The user's own numbers live in `data/body-composition.md`.

## 5. Concurrent training: adding cardio without losing lifting gains

### 5.1 Interference evidence

| Study | Finding | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Schumann et al. 2022, Sports Medicine 52:601-612 (https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/) | Concurrent training "does not compromise muscle hypertrophy and maximal strength development." Only explosive strength suffered, and more so when cardio and lifting were in the same session than when separated by at least 3 h. Frequency (more vs fewer than 5 weekly sessions) did not moderate the effect; result held for cycling and running | Explosive strength SMD -0.28 (95% CI -0.48 to -0.08, p = 0.007); same-session penalty p = 0.043; separated 3+ h p > 0.05. Secondary blogs report 43 studies with SMD -0.06 for maximal strength and -0.01 for hypertrophy; these counts were not verified against the paper (train.fitness, secondary) |
| Lundberg et al. 2022, Sports Medicine, 15 studies, fibre level | Small negative effect on fibre hypertrophy; type I fibre growth impaired when the cardio was running but not cycling; no differences by frequency, training status, or same-session order | Fibre hypertrophy SMD -0.23 (95% CI -0.46 to -0.00, p = 0.050); running on type I fibres SMD -0.81 (95% CI -1.26 to -0.36) |
| Wilson et al. 2012, JSCR, 21 studies, 422 effect sizes (pre-2016 foundational) | Interference "is a factor of the modality, frequency, and duration of the endurance training selected." Running, not cycling, produced significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength. Duration correlated most strongly | Frequency r about -0.26 to -0.35; duration r about -0.29 to -0.75 |
| Murlasits, Kneffel & Thalib 2018, J Sports Sci 36:1212-1219 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/) | Lifting before cardio in the same session preserved lower-body strength gains; VO2max unaffected by order | Lower-body 1RM +3.96 kg (95% CI 0.81-7.10 kg) for strength-first; VO2max difference 0.39 ml/kg/min (95% CI -1.03 to 1.81) |
| Methenitis 2018, JSSM 17:623 (https://www.jssm.org/jssm-17-623.xml-Fulltext) | With moderate-intensity cardio, opposite intra-session orders showed no difference in maximal strength or hypertrophy at 12 and 24 weeks; order is a tool to protect the acute strength session, not a long-term lever | - |
| WHO 2020 guidelines, Bull et al. 2020, BJSM (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7719906/) | All adults: 150-300 min moderate or 75-150 min vigorous aerobic activity per week, or an equivalent mix, plus regular muscle-strengthening activity. Reduce sedentary time (no threshold quantified) | The "muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days/week" wording could not be confirmed from the retrieved text; the aerobic figures are confirmed |

Bottom line: at the doses below, a lifter who does not train for power can largely ignore interference. Power and explosiveness are the only outcomes with a documented cost.

### 5.2 Weekly dose and scheduling

Program-wide cardio rule (spine rule 8): 2-3 sessions per week toward 150 min moderate; lift before cardio when in the same session, or separate by 3+ h; cycling or incline walking over running on heavy leg weeks.

| | 3-day lifting week | 5-6-day lifting week |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly cardio dose | Accumulate most of the week's minutes here: 150 min moderate (e.g. 3 x 50 min or 5 x 30 min) or 75 min vigorous | Minimum health floor: about 75 min vigorous or about 150 min moderate, or a mix, in short sessions |
| Session length | 30-50 min is fine | 20-30 min. Duration is the variable that correlates most strongly with interference (r up to -0.75, Wilson 2012) |
| Placement | Four non-lifting days exist, so put cardio on separate days (about 24 h from a lower-body session) | Little room for separate days. Do cardio after lifting, never before, on upper-body days; or keep it at least 3 h from a lower-body session (Schumann 2022). Chapter 03 marks which rotation days are upper-body |
| Modality | Cycling, incline walking, rowing, or running; running is acceptable here if leg hypertrophy is not the priority | Cycling, incline walking or rowing. Running impaired type I fibre growth where cycling did not (Lundberg 2022; Wilson 2012) |
| Same-session order | Lift first if combined | Lift first, always (+3.96 kg lower-body 1RM, Murlasits 2018) |

Gaps: minimum-separation data (Robineau et al. 2016: 0 vs 6 vs 24 h) could not be searched; Schumann's 3-h cut-point is the best available. ACSM-specific figures were not retrieved; WHO figures are used.

## 6. Recovery modalities, ranked by evidence

The research note's central finding (KQ6): the lifting itself is the best-supported injury-prevention tool, and nothing on the recovery-modality list changes long-term adaptation except cold-water immersion, which may slightly hurt it.

| Rank | Modality | Evidence | Verdict for this program |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Balanced strength training | 25 RCTs, about 26,600 participants, 3,464 injuries: strength training RR 0.315 (95% CI 0.207-0.480), "reduced sports injuries to less than 1/3 and overuse injuries could be almost halved" (Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen 2014, BJSM, https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/11/871; pre-2016 foundational). Strength-only follow-up: RR 0.338 across six trials, 7,738 participants, no publication bias (Lauersen et al. 2018, BJSM) | Already doing it. Keep the pattern coverage from chapter 05 balanced; that is the injury-prevention intervention |
| 2 | Proprioception / balance training | RR 0.550; multi-component programmes RR 0.655 (Lauersen 2014) | Add 5 min of single-leg balance or unstable-surface work only if there is a lower-body injury history |
| 3 | Massage | "Massage seems to be the most effective method for reducing DOMS and perceived fatigue" in a meta-analysis of 99 studies covering massage, cold-water immersion, compression, active recovery, stretching, cryotherapy, contrast water, and electrostimulation (Dupuy et al. 2018, Frontiers in Physiology, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29755363/; the 99-study count is from a secondary summary) | Best option for soreness and perceived fatigue. Does not change adaptation. Use if available and affordable |
| 4 | Active recovery (light cardio between sessions) | Small effect on soreness only (Dupuy 2018, via secondary summary) | A 20-30 min easy cycle on a rest day doubles as cardio minutes (section 5) |
| 5 | Foam rolling | 21 studies: pre-exercise rolling improved sprint about 0.7% and flexibility about 4% with negligible effects on jump and strength; post-exercise rolling slightly reduced losses in sprint and strength and reduced pain perception; effects overall "rather minor and partly negligible" (Wiewelhove et al. 2019, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6465761/) | A warm-up tool more than a recovery tool. 1-2 min per muscle before lifting is cheap and mildly useful for range of motion |
| 6 | Stretching | Injury prevention RR 0.963 (95% CI 0.846-1.095), the only measure without consistently favourable results (Lauersen 2014). Acutely, static holds of 60 s or more before lifting cut performance about -4.6%; holds under 30 s inside a dynamic warm-up have negligible effect (Behm et al. 2016, via the exercise-selection note KQ5) | A mobility or comfort tool, not injury prevention. If used before lifting, keep holds under 30 s and follow with dynamic work (chapter 05, section 5) |
| 7 | Cold-water immersion after lifting | Resistance training alone produced greater relative hypertrophy than cold-water immersion plus training: cSMD -0.22 (95% CrI -0.47 to 0.04), "likely greater than zero but small and uncertain"; 8 studies, 116 participants aged 20-26, 2 women (Piñero et al. 2024, Eur J Sport Sci, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11235606/). Dupuy 2018 ranks it second for soreness | Avoid for several hours after hypertrophy-focused sessions. If used for comfort, reserve it for the lighter 3-day week or after cardio-only days. Effects on strength were not examined |

Gaps: warm-up injury-prevention evidence (Fradkin et al. 2010) and a dedicated active-recovery meta-analysis were not retrieved.

## 7. Age 30s-50s

The research note is direct about this: evidence specific to 30-50-year-old lifters is thin, and the recommendations below are extrapolated from young and 60+ data.

| Question | What the evidence says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Does middle age mean more muscle damage? | No. A 2025 meta-analysis of 36 studies comparing older and younger adults found strength change after damaging exercise did not differ by age; younger adults showed more soreness and higher creatine kinase at 24 h. "Advancing age is not associated with greater symptoms of EIMD" | 2025 meta-analysis (Cardiff Met record) |
| Does a 44-year-old recover differently from a 26-year-old? | No meaningful difference in any recovery outcome after a 60-min downhill run, 14 men in each group | Frontiers in Physiology 2022 (https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.916924/full) |
| Resistance-exercise damage and age | Of 12 comparisons, 4 found worse symptoms in older adults, 2 the opposite, 6 no difference; authors concluded resistance training can be practiced in older populations without concern for impaired recovery | Fernandes et al. review (Newcastle repository) |
| Where an age effect does show | Slower strength recovery, not more soreness, in about 64-year-olds vs about 25-year-olds after fast eccentric elbow-flexor contractions; "the time course of the exercise recovery process could be prolonged with age" in adults 65+ | PubMed 18584196; Hayes et al. 2023, Sports Medicine - Open (PMC10317890) |
| Protein per meal | Muscle protein synthesis plateaued at about 0.40 g/kg per meal in older men vs 0.24 g/kg in young men; some older men may need up to about 0.60 g/kg | Moore et al. 2015, summarised in Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018 (PMC5828430) |
| Protein per day | The supplementation effect on fat-free mass "was reduced with increasing age" (Morton 2018). In adults 65+ the LBM effect was significant at 1.2-1.59 g/kg/day (Nunes 2022) | Morton 2018; Nunes 2022 |
| Injury | Strength training nearly halves overuse injuries in the pooled RCTs, at any age | Lauersen 2014 |

What to do:

- Do not assume you need a longer recovery window than the 48-72 h in section 1. The evidence does not show more muscle damage in middle age. Where an age effect exists it is slower strength recovery, which argues for watching the performance signals in section 2, not for blanket extra rest days.
- Protein toward the upper end, about 2.0 g/kg/day and 0.4-0.5 g/kg per meal, is a low-risk hedge against the age-related blunting of the protein response.
- Joint care comes from the injury-prevention evidence (balanced strength work, proprioception if there is a history), not from stretching.
- How this changes with available days: nothing age-specific changes between a 3-day and a 6-day week. If performance signals say the 6-day week is too much, the fix is the same as for any lifter: the next week is a 3-day week or a deload (section 2.3).

Gaps: no study specific to 30-50-year-old lifters' recovery time course, warm-up modifications, or joint care; masters-athlete guidance from Stronger By Science and Barbell Medicine could not be retrieved.

---

## 8. When to see a professional

This guide gives general training guidance only. See a physician, sports-medicine doctor, physiotherapist, or registered dietitian when:

- Performance and mood have been down for more than a few weeks despite a deload, adequate sleep, and adequate food (possible non-functional overreaching or something unrelated to training; section 2).
- Pain is sharp, localised, worsening, or present at rest, or a joint swells, locks, or gives way. Soreness that peaks at 24-72 h and fades is normal; pain that changes how you move is not.
- Sleep problems persist outside hard training blocks.
- You plan a calorie deficit larger than about 500 kcal/day, have a history of disordered eating, or have a medical condition affected by diet, protein intake, creatine, or caffeine (kidney disease, hypertension, pregnancy, heart rhythm problems, or medication interactions).
- You are starting a program after a long break, an injury, or a cardiovascular event, or you are over 40 and have not had a recent check-up and plan vigorous cardio.
