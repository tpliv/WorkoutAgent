# 06. Recovery, fatigue management, sleep, nutrition, and cardio

Last updated: 2026-10-21

**Revision 2026-10-21:** this chapter was corrected against the primary sources. Several authors, study counts and claims were fixed. The caffeine dose was lowered to fit adult safety limits. Most "secondary" flags were removed. Details: `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md`, section "Addendum 2026-10-21: primary-source verification".

This chapter covers everything outside the lifting session that decides whether the program works: how long a muscle needs between hard sessions, how to tell a productive hard week from an unproductive one, sleep, food, supplements, cardio, and recovery modalities. It applies the rules to the two week types this program alternates between (3 sessions vs 5-6 sessions). The weekly layouts themselves live in [chapter 03](03-flexible-scheduling.md) and `templates/`; this chapter never prescribes its own.

Evidence base: `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` (KQ1-KQ7 and the 2026-10-21 verification addendum), plus the warm-up and stretching findings in `research/exercise-selection-and-session-design.md` (KQ5) for section 6. When the note was first compiled, full-text fetches of most primary papers were blocked, so many figures came from abstracts and secondary summaries. The 2026-10-21 addendum re-checked them against PubMed, PMC and publisher pages. Items still known only through a secondary source are flagged "secondary"; items checked only at abstract level say so. Remaining gaps are listed in each section.

This is general training guidance, not medical advice. Section 8 says when to stop reading and see someone.

Units in this chapter: kilograms (kg), grams (g), milligrams (mg), kilocalories (kcal), hours (h).

## 1. Recovery between same-muscle sessions

### 1.1 The timeline

| Time after a hard session | What the data show | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 24 h | Volume load still depressed when the next session comes this soon (ES -0.90) | Goulart et al. 2021, Eur J Sport Sci 21:935-943: 14 trained males, 5 sets of 8-10RM to concentric failure on squat and leg press, so lower body only (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32594858/) |
| 48 h | Mostly restored; first-set volume load still down (ES -0.63) | Same study. Authors concluded at least 48 h was needed to re-establish performance |
| 72 h | Fully restored; jump height (ES 0.25) and isometric strength (ES 0.14) were above baseline; perceived recovery was also better at 72 h | Same study |
| 24-48 h, non-failure training | Recovery significantly faster than after to-failure sets, even with total volume matched (3x10 to failure vs 6x5 and 3x5 not to failure, bench press and full squat) | Morán-Navarro et al. 2017, Eur J Appl Physiol 117:2387-2399, 10 trained men (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/) |
| Out to 96 h, squat vs bench vs deadlift | Recovery time course was broadly similar across the three lifts. Squat bar speed at 70% 1RM was still down 8.6% at 72 h; deadlift speed did not fall | Belcher et al. 2019, Appl Physiol Nutr Metab, 12 well-trained men, 4 sets to failure at 80% 1RM (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30779596/) |

Morán-Navarro's conclusion, quoted by the research note: "RT leading to failure considerably increases the time needed for the recovery of neuromuscular function and metabolic and hormonal homeostasis." This is why the program's default is 1-2 RIR on compounds (spine rule 6), not failure.

All of these are small primary studies (n = 10-14). The 24-72 h timeline comes from lower-body exercises only. The research note found no meta-analysis pooling recovery time by volume or exercise type.

### 1.2 Frequency does not matter for hypertrophy when volume is equated; strength gains a little

- Meta-analysis, 25 studies, 6-30 weeks: "resistance training frequency does not significantly or meaningfully impact muscle hypertrophy when volume is equated"; a frequency effect appeared only when volume was not equated (Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019, J Sports Sci, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/). The 25-study count and the quote are verified; the 836-participant figure was not checked.
- 3 vs 6 sessions per week, volume-equated, 27 resistance-trained men, 6 weeks: strength gains did not differ (upper body +4% vs +6%, lower body +22% vs +18%, 3x vs 6x). Hypertrophy was measured directly (muscle thickness at 4 sites) and was similar, except that only the 3x group grew the elbow flexors (+7%) (Saric et al. 2019, J Strength Cond Res 33(7S):S122-S129, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30363041/).
- A Grgic, Schoenfeld & Latella review found site-specific hypertrophy was generally similar for once-weekly vs 2-3x weekly training when volume was matched (J Sci Med Sport 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30236847/; metadata verified, conclusion not re-read).
- Pelland et al. 2026, Sports Med 56:481-505 (peer reviewed; 67 studies, 2,058 participants). Frequency: the hypertrophy effect was "compatible with negligible", but strength rose with frequency, with diminishing returns. Volume: both strength and hypertrophy rose with diminishing returns, sharper for strength (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/). The earlier preprint summary put continued hypertrophy gains past 10 weekly sets per muscle (Biolayne REPS issue 31, secondary).

So, for hypertrophy, frequency is only a way to distribute volume. For strength, more frequent practice of a lift gives a small, diminishing benefit (Pelland 2026). That is one reason the main lifts stay at 2x per week even in a 3-day week.

### 1.3 What this means for the two week types

| Week type | Implication | Spine / chapter 03 rule it supports |
|---|---|---|
| 3-day week | Each muscle is hit twice with 48-96 h between touches, which sits inside the full-recovery window. Sets can go to 1-2 RIR without compromising the next session | Spine: 3-day week, every muscle 2x, 3-5 sets/muscle/session |
| 5-6-day week | The extra days are for spreading volume thinner per session, not for adding full-fatigue sessions on consecutive days. Keep each muscle to 2-3 touches with about 48-72 h between hard sessions for the same muscle | Spine: 5-6-day week, 5-8 sets/muscle/session, every muscle 2-3x; chapter 03 orders the rotation so consecutive days alternate muscle groups |
| Same muscle on consecutive days (unavoidable in some 6-day rotations) | Stop 2-4 reps short of failure on the second day and keep that day's per-muscle volume at the low end of the range (5 sets, not 8), so the session is sub-maximal in fatigue cost | Research note KQ1, Inferences |
| Deadlift vs squat spacing | No evidence basis for a longer standalone recovery window after deadlifts when load and proximity to failure are similar (Belcher 2019). The driver is total systemic volume and how close to failure you went | Research note KQ1, Inferences |

Gap: a direct study of consecutive-day vs non-consecutive-day training for the same muscle (e.g. Yang et al. 2018) could not be searched. The consecutive-day rule above is an inference from the 24-h data and the failure-vs-non-failure data.

## 2. Fatigue monitoring: what to watch and what to ignore

### 2.1 Overreaching vs overtraining

- Scoping review of 47 studies: "short-term periods of OR achieved with either high-volume or high-intensity RT can elicit functional OR (FOR), but there is also evidence that chronic high-volume and/or intensity RT can lead to non-functional overreaching (NFOR). There is minimal evidence to suggest that true OTS has occurred in strength sports or RT." (Bell et al. 2020, J Sports Sci, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32602418/)
- Diagnostic criteria for FOR, NFOR and overtraining syndrome in resistance training are unstandardised, so the literature itself cannot say where the line is. The same review of 22 studies concluded that "no marker other than a sustained decrease in performance has been established as a reliable indicator of overtraining in resistance exercise" (Grandou et al. 2020, Sports Med, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31820373/).
- Squat overreaching block (Fry et al. 2014, conference abstract, not a peer-reviewed paper; supplement study funded by Nutricia). The overload was extreme: 15 sessions in 7.5 days of 10x5 speed squats at 70% 1RM; 11 overreaching subjects, 6 controls. Bar velocity at 70% 1RM dropped and recovered within about 1 week. Perceived recovery fell by more than 45%. Mood (abridged POMS) did **not** change (https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijesab/vol11/iss2/20).
- Functional overreaching means improved capability in the days and weeks after a short-term increase in training demand (Bell et al. 2022, Front Sports Act Living, coaches' interview study).

| Term | What it is | Expected course | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional overreaching (FOR) | Planned short-term overload; performance dips then rebounds | Should resolve with a lighter week. The only lifting data point (the extreme-overload abstract above) saw bar speed recover in about 1 week | This is what a hard 5-6-day week is supposed to produce. The following lighter week (a 3-day week or a deload per chapter 03) is the recovery |
| Non-functional overreaching (NFOR) | Performance stays down for weeks after the overload ends | Weeks | Cut volume (spine rule 4: accessories first), hold intensity, check sleep and food |
| Overtraining syndrome (OTS) | Months-long decrement with systemic symptoms | Months | Minimal evidence this occurs in lifting. If performance and mood have been down for more than a few weeks with adequate sleep and food, see a professional (section 8) |

### 2.2 Which signals are validated

Review of 90 studies on regulating and monitoring resistance training: barbell velocity and sessional, RIR-based, and post-set Borg RPE were strongly associated with training performance (r ≥ 0.68); velocity- and RIR-RPE-based intensity and volume modifications are "practical, reliable and show preliminary utility," while many physiological measures are lab-restricted (Helms et al. 2020, J Hum Kinet 74:23-42, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33312273/; the same paper chapter 04 cites).

| Signal | Evidence quality for lifting | How to use it | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reps at a fixed load, or bar velocity at a fixed load | Strong. A sustained performance drop is the only established overtraining marker (Grandou 2020) | Log every main-lift top set. Two or more sessions in a row below the expected reps or speed is the first red flag | Helms et al. 2020; Grandou 2020; squat overreaching abstract |
| RPE / RIR drift | Strong (RIR-based RPE associated with performance) | Same load and reps feeling 1+ RPE harder across several sessions. No validated threshold exists; use "two sessions in a row" as the working rule | Helms et al. 2020; research note gap |
| Daily perceived-recovery (and mood) questionnaire | Limited. An opinion article calls it "consistently described as an early marker of maladaptation". In the only lifting data, perceived recovery fell but mood did not change | A 1-5 perceived-recovery rating each morning takes 10 s. A week-long downward trend is a supporting signal, not proof on its own | Lastella et al. 2018, Front Physiol 9:436, opinion article (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5928142/); squat overreaching abstract |
| Objective sleep efficiency (wearable) | Low to moderate, from endurance athletes. The objective result pools only 3 actigraphy studies | Overreaching reduced sleep efficiency by -2.0% (95% CI -3.2 to -0.8%); subjective sleep quality did not change significantly (8 studies, Glass' Δ -0.27, p = 0.08). No included study was low risk of bias | Murphy, Svansdottir, Dupuy & Louis 2024, PLoS ONE 19(5):e0303748 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38809828/) |
| Subjective sleep complaints | Limited (opinion article; subjective sleep quality was not significantly changed in the 2024 meta-analysis) | Trouble falling or staying asleep during a hard block is a marker whether cause or effect | Lastella et al. 2018 |
| Heart rate variability (HRV) | Weak for lifting | Log it if you like, never decide on it alone. HRV-guided training improved HRV itself (SMD 0.50) but not VO2max or endurance performance (both SMD 0.20, not significant), in endurance training only. "A lack of research connecting the effective utilization of HRV to guide training, specifically with strength training" | Manresa-Rocamora et al. 2021, Int J Environ Res Public Health 18:10299 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34639599/); Addleman et al. 2024, J Funct Morphol Kinesiol 9:93 (PMC11204851); Alabama dissertation (secondary) |
| Blood or hormonal markers | Lab-restricted, not practical | Ignore for self-coaching | Helms et al. 2020 |

### 2.3 The decision rule

Red flags, in order of evidence (research note KQ2, Inferences):

1. Rep performance or bar speed at a fixed load falls across two or more sessions.
2. RPE drifts up for the same prescribed load and reps.
3. Daily perceived-recovery scores worsen.
4. Objective sleep efficiency worsens.

Flag 1 carries the most weight. A sustained drop in performance is the only established marker of overtraining in lifting (Grandou 2020); flags 2-4 support it but do not replace it.

Any two together for a week means the heavy week has tipped past functional overreaching. The next week should be a 3-day week or a deload week, following chapter 03's rules (deload: keep frequency, halve sets, keep some heavy but easy work).

Alternating a 5-6-day week with a lighter 3-day week is a reasonable structure, but its evidence basis is weak. The "recovers within about a week" figure comes from one conference abstract (Fry 2014) with a far harsher overload (15 sessions in 7.5 days) than a 5-6-day lifting week, and no trial has tested this alternation. Use it provided the heavy week's performance dip is small and reverses in the light week; judge that by performance (flag 1). If it does not reverse, treat it as NFOR.

Gaps: no resistance-specific threshold (percent of 1RM, percent velocity loss, RPE points) separates FOR from NFOR; deload-week trials (Coleman et al. 2024) could not be searched.

## 3. Sleep

### 3.1 Numbers

| Finding | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Maximal strength after acute sleep loss (≤6 h of sleep in 24 h) | -2.85% on average (95% CI -4.47 to -1.23; 66 outcomes) | Craven et al. 2022, Sports Med 52:2669-2690, meta-analysis of 69 publications (77 participant groups) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9584849/) |
| Strength endurance after acute sleep loss | -9.85% (95% CI -19.6 to -0.13), from only 5 studies and 62 men; unstable when one study is removed | Same |
| Time of day after sleep loss | Morning tasks were "largely unaffected"; performance fell about 0.4% for each hour awake before the task | Same |
| Muscle protein synthesis after one night of no sleep (total sleep deprivation) | -18% (0.072 vs 0.059 %/h, p = 0.040); protein-breakdown markers did not change | Lamon et al. 2021, Physiological Reports, randomized crossover, 13 young adults (7 men, 6 women) (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33400856/) |
| Cortisol / testosterone after the same night | +21% / -24% | Lamon 2021: "a single night of total sleep deprivation is sufficient to induce anabolic resistance and a procatabolic environment" |
| Myofibrillar protein synthesis after 5 nights of 4 h in bed | 1.24 vs 1.53 %/day. Three HIIT sessions during the restriction kept it at control levels | Saner et al. 2020, J Physiol 598:1523-1536, 24 young men (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32078168/) |
| Acute and chronic sleep deprivation on strength, power, endurance | Significant reductions on some measures; mixed after a single night | Easow et al. 2025, Sleep Breath 29:242, systematic review of 13 studies (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12263768/) |
| Injury risk, adolescents sleeping under 8 h | Risk ratio 1.7 (95% CI 1.0-3.0, p = 0.04); 112 athletes, mean age 15; retrospective survey plus records | Milewski et al. 2014 (pre-2016), J Pediatr Orthop (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25028798/) |
| Injury risk, adults | Current evidence does not establish insufficient sleep as an independent risk factor in adults | Quality in Sport review (not re-checked) |

Flag: the common 7-9 h adult target cannot be derived from these studies. The research note says so explicitly. Consumer-site percentage tables linking sleep hours to injury (e.g. d1training.com) are unsourced and are not used here.

### 3.2 What to do with it

- Protect sleep most on the night before and the night after the heaviest sessions of a 5-6-day week. An 18% drop in protein synthesis after one night of no sleep (Lamon 2021) is the largest single acute recovery penalty in this chapter. Several short nights in a row also lower myofibrillar protein synthesis (5 nights of 4 h; Saner 2020).
- After a short night, train earlier in the day where the schedule allows. In Craven 2022, morning tasks were largely unaffected, and performance fell about 0.4% for each hour awake before the task.
- After a short night, maximal strength is relatively robust (-3%) while strength endurance is not (-10%, from 5 studies). So if the rotation's next workout is flexible within chapter 03's rules, a heavy low-rep main-lift session is a better choice than a high-rep volume session. Do not skip ahead in the rotation to do this; chapter 03 says what is allowed.
- Treat worsening sleep during a hard block as a fatigue signal (section 2), not as a separate problem.

How this changes with available days: a 3-day week has rest days, so after a short night the next workout can wait a day (the rotation does not move on). A 5-6-day week has less slack, so the earlier-in-the-day and low-rep-first rules above matter more.

Gaps: no trial of sleep extension on hypertrophy and no study linking habitual sleep duration to long-term gains in trained adults were found.

## 4. Nutrition

### 4.1 One-glance table

| Item | Target | Notes and confidence | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protein, daily | 1.6-2.2 g/kg bodyweight/day. In a deficit, lean lifters: 2.3-3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass | Supplementing beyond about 1.62 g/kg/day added no further fat-free mass in a 49-study meta-analysis, but that breakpoint was not itself significant (p = 0.079) and its 95% CI spanned 1.03-2.20 g/kg, so it is imprecise, not a ceiling. Use the top of the range in the heavy week and in the 40s-50s (section 7). In a deficit, go higher, scaling up as the deficit grows and the lifter gets leaner (Helms 2014; older, only 6 studies) | Morton et al. 2018, BJSM (PMC5867436; breakpoint confirmed in its supplementary table); Stronger By Science critique (https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=61316); Nunes et al. 2022, J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle (PMC8978023): LBM effect SMD 0.22 (95% CI 0.14-0.30), significant at 1.6+ g/kg/day in adults under 65; Helms et al. 2014, Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/) |

| Protein timing around training | Total daily intake matters far more than timing. | Calories for fat loss | Deficit near or under about 500 kcal/day; lose about 0.5-1% of bodyweight per week | Deficits cost lean mass (ES -0.57, p = 0.02); a deficit of about 500 kcal/day "prevented gains in lean mass", and the authors advise avoiding deficits larger than 500 kcal/day. | Creatine monohydrate | 3-5 g/day, every day, training or not | Optional load: 5 g four times a day (20 g/day, about 0.3 g/kg/day) for 5-7 days. | Caffeine | Optional. Start at about 3 mg/kg, about 60 min before the heaviest sessions. Stay within about 200 mg per single dose and 400 mg per day from all sources (healthy, non-pregnant adults). Avoid it before evening sessions | For an 80-kg lifter, 3 mg/kg is 240 mg, above the 200 mg single-dose figure, so cap the dose at 200 mg; as little as 2 mg/kg (160 mg) may work. Count coffee, tea and energy drinks toward the daily 400 mg. The ISSN stand's performance range is 3-6 mg/kg, but its top end (480 mg at 80 kg) exceeds both safety figures and is not used here; 9 mg/kg brings frequent side effects. Effect is small: 1RM SMD 0.20 overall, upper body 0.21 (significant), lower body 0.15 (not significant); jump power SMD 0.17. Caffeine can disturb sleep, and 100 mg may do so in some adults. The 200/400 mg figures are from secondary reports of the EFSA opinion (the EFSA pages could not be fetched). Health conditions: section 8 | ISSN caffeine position stand, Guest et al. 2021 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7777221/); Grgic et al. 2018, JISSN 15:11, 10 studies per outcome (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29527137/); EFSA 2015, EFSA Journal 13(5):4102 (pre-2016; secondary) |
| Carbohydrate | At least 3-5 g/kg/day | For an 80-kg lifter: 240-400 g/day or more. A narrative-review recommendation for bodybuilders, not a trial result. Supporting detail: a meta-regression of 105 articles (5,402 participants, total protein intakes of 0.5-3.5 g/kg/day, not limited to trained lifters) found that each extra 0.1 g/kg/day of protein added 0.39 kg of lean body mass below a total of 1.3 g/kg/day, and 0.12 kg above it (Tagawa et al. 2021, Nutr Rev 79:66-75, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33300582/). The protein effect was smaller with age and larger in trained individuals (Morton 2018).

### 4.2 How calories change with the week type

| Week type | Calorie setting | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 5-6-day week | Maintenance or a 10-20% surplus | Highest weekly volume, highest recovery demand; data on trained lifters suggest a deficit would most likely impair gains (Slater 2019) |
| 3-day week | This is the week to run a deficit if fat loss is a goal; keep it near or under about 500 kcal/day | Lower volume means a smaller recovery penalty. Even about 500 kcal/day prevented lean-mass gains, and the meta-analysis authors advise not going beyond it (Murphy & Koehler 2022) |
| Either week | Protein at 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day (higher in a deficit, see 4.1), at least about 0.4 g/kg per meal over 3-4+ meals; creatine daily | Protein and creatine do not change with the schedule. Caffeine, if used, goes before the heaviest sessions only, within the limits in 4.1 |

Flags and gaps: Murphy & Koehler's meta-regression slope and the exact deficit with zero lean-mass change are unverified (full text blocked). The EFSA caffeine safety figures are known only from secondary sources.

### 4.3 Estimating maintenance and running a recomposition

Added 2026-10-21. Source: `research/body-recomposition.md` (KQ1, KQ2).

| Step | Rule | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Estimate maintenance | Resting rate (Mifflin-St Jeor, or Katch-McArdle if you have a lean-mass scan) times an activity factor of about 1.375-1.725; or 30-35 kcal/kg | Practitioner formulas; a starting point only |
| Correct it | Track intake for 2-3 weeks and adjust by the weekly-average bodyweight trend | Practitioner convention |
| Recomposition setting | A deficit of about 300-400 kcal/day averaged over the week, protein about 2.2 g/kg | Inference from Longland 2016 (2.4 g/kg protein kept lean mass rising in a deficit) and Murphy & Koehler (about 500 kcal/day held lean mass flat) |
| Who it suits | Returning lifters and people with recent fat gain respond fastest; lean, continuously trained lifters slowest | Barakat 2020 review (abstract); Halonen 2024 (faster regain in the first 5 weeks of retraining) |
| How long | Fastest in the first 2-3 months of retraining; after about 6 months switch to a deliberate surplus or deficit | Inference; no month-by-month trial found |

With the week types: put the larger deficit in 3-day weeks and keep 5-6-day weeks near maintenance, as in 4.2. The user's own numbers live in `data/body-composition.md`.

## 5. Concurrent training: adding cardio without losing lifting gains

### 5.1 Interference evidence

| Study | Finding | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Schumann et al. 2022, Sports Medicine 52:601-612, 43 studies (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/) | Concurrent training "does not compromise muscle hypertrophy and maximal strength development." Only explosive strength suffered, and more so when cardio and lifting were in the same session than when separated by at least 3 h. Frequency (more vs fewer than 5 weekly sessions) did not moderate the effect; result held for cycling and running. Age (<40 vs >40) did not moderate it; training status compared only untrained vs active | Maximal strength SMD -0.06 (95% CI -0.20 to 0.09, p = 0.446); hypertrophy SMD -0.01 (p = 0.919); explosive strength SMD -0.28 (95% CI -0.48 to -0.08, p = 0.007); same-session penalty p = 0.043; separated 3+ h p > 0.05 |
| Huiberts, Wüst & van der Zwaard 2024, Sports Med 54:485-503, 59 studies, 1,346 adults aged 18-50 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37847373/) | Concurrent training blunted lower-body maximal strength in men; no effect in women. Hypertrophy data were insufficient | Male lower-body strength SMD -0.43 (95% CI -0.64 to -0.22) |
| Held et al. 2026, Sports Med 56:1489-1512, umbrella review: 17 meta-analyses, 144 studies, 1,492 participants (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41762427/) | Strength, power and hypertrophy were comparable to lifting alone, and aerobic capacity was better. Sequence was not significant, but trends favoured lifting first for strength | Aerobic capacity SMD 0.77; lifting-first trend for strength SMD 1.69 (not significant) |
| Lundberg et al. 2022, Sports Medicine 52:2391-2403, 15 studies, fibre level (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35476184/) | Small negative effect on fibre hypertrophy; type I fibre growth impaired when the cardio was running but not cycling; no differences by frequency, training status, or same-session order | Fibre hypertrophy SMD -0.23 (95% CI -0.46 to -0.00, p = 0.050); type I -0.34 and type II -0.13 overall (both not significant); running on type I fibres SMD -0.81 (95% CI -1.26 to -0.36) |
| Wilson et al. 2012, JSCR, 21 studies, 422 effect sizes (pre-2016 foundational) | Interference "is a factor of the modality, frequency, and duration of the endurance training selected." Running, not cycling, produced significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength. Duration correlated most strongly | Frequency r about -0.26 to -0.35; duration r about -0.29 to -0.75. These are between-study correlations, not a within-person dose-response |
| Murlasits, Kneffel & Thalib 2018, J Sports Sci 36:1212-1219 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/) | Lifting before cardio in the same session preserved lower-body strength gains; VO2max unaffected by order | Lower-body 1RM +3.96 kg (95% CI 0.81-7.10 kg) for strength-first; VO2max difference 0.39 ml/kg/min (95% CI -1.03 to 1.81) |
| Robineau et al. 2016, JSCR 30:672-683, 58 amateur rugby players, 7 weeks, strength always first (older) (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25546450/) | Bench press and half-squat gains were lower with 0 h between strength and cardio than with 6 h, 24 h, or strength only. Fast isokinetic strength was also lower at 6 h. VO2peak gains were best at 24 h. Authors advise at least 6 h between sessions | - |
| Panissa et al. 2018, J Sports Sci Med 17:623-632, 12-week original study, n = 19 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6243621/) | HIIT runs before lifting, twice a week, did not impair 1RM at 8 weeks, but only the lifting-only group kept gaining relative strength from week 8 to 12. Its introduction cites 24-week trials (Eklund et al. 2015, Int J Sports Med 36:120-129; Schumann et al. 2014; both older) in which opposite intra-session orders gave similar maximal strength and hypertrophy at 12 and 24 weeks. That order claim is second-hand here | - |
| WHO 2020 guidelines, Bull et al. 2020, BJSM (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719906/) | All adults: 150-300 min moderate or 75-150 min vigorous aerobic activity per week, or an equivalent mix, plus muscle-strengthening activity at moderate or greater intensity for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week. Reduce sedentary time (no threshold quantified) | Confirmed in the primary text |

Bottom line: at the doses below, cardio costs a lifter little. Hypertrophy and overall maximal strength were not compromised in the largest analyses (Schumann 2022; Held 2026). Two qualifications. Explosive strength suffers (SMD -0.28). And in men, concurrent training showed a small-to-moderate lower-body maximal-strength cost (Huiberts 2024, SMD -0.43). So a male lifter should not ignore interference on lower-body days: lift first, and keep cardio at least 3 h, and 6 h where possible, from lower-body sessions (Schumann 2022; Robineau 2016).

### 5.2 Weekly dose and scheduling

Program-wide cardio rule (spine rule 8): 2-3 sessions per week toward 150 min moderate; lift before cardio when in the same session, or separate by at least 3 h, 6 h where possible; cycling or incline walking over running on heavy leg weeks.

| | 3-day lifting week | 5-6-day lifting week |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly cardio dose | Accumulate most of the week's minutes here: 150 min moderate (e.g. 3 x 50 min or 5 x 30 min) or 75 min vigorous | Minimum health floor: about 75 min vigorous or about 150 min moderate, or a mix, in short sessions |
| Session length | 30-50 min is fine | 20-30 min. Duration is the variable that correlates most strongly with interference across studies (r up to -0.75, Wilson 2012) |
| Placement | Four non-lifting days exist, so put cardio on separate days (about 24 h from a lower-body session; VO2peak gains were best at 24 h in Robineau 2016) | Little room for separate days. Do cardio after lifting, never before, on upper-body days; or keep it at least 3 h, and 6 h where possible, from a lower-body session (Schumann 2022; Robineau 2016). Chapter 03 marks which rotation days are upper-body |
| Modality | Cycling, incline walking, rowing, or running; running is acceptable here if leg hypertrophy is not the priority | Cycling, incline walking or rowing. Running impaired type I fibre growth where cycling did not (Lundberg 2022; Wilson 2012) |
| Same-session order | Lift first if combined | Lift first, always (+3.96 kg lower-body 1RM, Murlasits 2018; same trend in Held 2026) |

Gaps: ACSM-specific figures were not retrieved; WHO figures are used.

## 6. Recovery modalities, ranked by evidence

The research note's central finding (KQ6): the lifting itself is the best-supported injury-prevention tool, and nothing on the recovery-modality list changes long-term adaptation except cold-water immersion, which may slightly hurt it.

| Rank | Modality | Evidence | Verdict for this program |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Balanced strength training | 25 RCTs, about 26,600 participants, 3,464 injuries: strength training RR 0.315 (95% CI 0.207-0.480), "reduced sports injuries to less than 1/3 and overuse injuries could be almost halved" (Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen 2014, BJSM, https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/11/871; pre-2016 foundational). Strength-only follow-up: RR 0.338 (95% CI 0.238-0.480) across six trials, 7,738 participants, no publication bias; a 10% increase in strength-training volume cut injury risk by more than 4 percentage points (Lauersen et al. 2018, BJSM, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30131332/). These were sports-injury prevention trials in athletes aged roughly 12-40, not lifting injuries in gym lifters | Already doing it. Keep the pattern coverage from chapter 05 balanced; that is the injury-prevention intervention. The size of the benefit for a lifter in their 30s-50s is extrapolated |
| 2 | Proprioception / balance training | RR 0.550; multi-component programmes RR 0.655 (Lauersen 2014) | Add 5 min of single-leg balance or unstable-surface work only if there is a lower-body injury history |
| 3 | Massage | "Massage seems to be the most effective method for reducing DOMS and perceived fatigue" in a meta-analysis of 99 studies covering massage, cold-water immersion, compression, active recovery, stretching, cryotherapy, contrast water, and electrostimulation. Active recovery, massage, compression, immersion, contrast water and cryotherapy all reduced DOMS (g -0.40 to -2.26); massage and cold exposure reduced inflammation markers most (Dupuy et al. 2018, Frontiers in Physiology, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29755363/) | Best option for soreness and perceived fatigue. Does not change adaptation. Use if available and affordable |
| 4 | Active recovery (light cardio between sessions) | Reduced DOMS in the same 99-study meta-analysis (Dupuy 2018) | A 20-30 min easy cycle on a rest day doubles as cardio minutes (section 5) |
| 5 | Foam rolling | 21 studies: pre-exercise rolling improved sprint about 0.7% and flexibility about 4% with negligible effects on jump and strength (+1.8%, g = 0.12); post-exercise rolling slightly reduced losses in sprint and strength and reduced pain perception; effects overall "rather minor and partly negligible" (Wiewelhove et al. 2019, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6465761/) | A warm-up tool more than a recovery tool. 1-2 min per muscle before lifting is cheap and mildly useful for range of motion |
| 6 | Stretching | Injury prevention RR 0.963 (95% CI 0.846-1.095), the only measure without consistently favourable results (Lauersen 2014). Acutely, static holds of 60 s or more before lifting cut performance about -4.6%; holds under 30 s inside a dynamic warm-up have negligible effect (Behm et al. 2016, via the exercise-selection note KQ5) | A mobility or comfort tool, not injury prevention. If used before lifting, keep holds under 30 s and follow with dynamic work (chapter 05, section 5) |
| 7 | Cold-water immersion after lifting | Resistance training alone produced greater relative hypertrophy than cold-water immersion plus training: cSMD -0.22 (95% CrI -0.47 to 0.04), "likely greater than zero but small and uncertain"; 8 studies, 116 participants aged 20-26, 2 women (Piñero et al. 2024, Eur J Sport Sci, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11235606/). Dupuy 2018 ranks it second for soreness | Avoid for several hours after hypertrophy-focused sessions. If used for comfort, reserve it for the lighter 3-day week or after cardio-only days. Effects on strength were not examined |

Gaps: warm-up injury-prevention evidence (Fradkin et al. 2010) and a dedicated active-recovery meta-analysis were not retrieved.

## 7. Age 30s-50s

The research note is direct about this: evidence specific to 30-50-year-old lifters is thin, and the recommendations below are extrapolated from young and 60+ data.

| Question | What the evidence says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Does middle age mean more muscle damage? | No. A 2025 meta-analysis of 36 studies comparing older and younger adults found strength change after damaging exercise did not differ by age; younger adults showed more soreness and higher creatine kinase at 24 h. "Advancing age is not associated with greater symptoms of EIMD" | Fernandes et al. 2025, J Aging Phys Act 33:606-624 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40174882/) |
| Does a 44-year-old recover differently from a 26-year-old? | No meaningful difference in any recovery outcome after a 60-min downhill run, 14 amateur male athletes in each group. The stimulus was running, followed for only 48 h, so this says little about recovery from lifting | Markus et al. 2022, Front Physiol 13:916924 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35774290/) |
| Resistance-exercise damage and age | Of 12 comparisons, 4 found worse symptoms in older adults, 2 the opposite, 6 no difference; authors concluded resistance training can be practiced in older populations without concern for impaired recovery | Fernandes et al. review (Newcastle repository; not re-checked) |
| Where an age effect does show | Slower strength recovery, not more soreness, in about 64-year-olds vs about 25-year-olds after fast eccentric elbow-flexor contractions (10 men per group); "the time course of the exercise recovery process could be prolonged with age" in adults 65+ | Chapman et al. 2008 (pre-2016), Eur J Appl Physiol 104:531-539 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18584196/); Hayes et al. 2023, Sports Medicine - Open (PMC10317890) |
| Protein per meal | Muscle protein synthesis plateaued at about 0.40 g/kg body mass per meal in men about 71 years old vs 0.24 g/kg in men about 22. The "up to about 0.60 g/kg" figure is per kg of lean body mass in the older men, not per kg of bodyweight. Single doses of isolated protein, acute protein synthesis only | Moore et al. 2015 (pre-2016), J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med Sci 70:57-62 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25056502/) |
| Protein per day | The supplementation effect on fat-free mass "was reduced with increasing age" (Morton 2018). In adults 65+ the LBM effect was significant at 1.2-1.59 g/kg/day (Nunes 2022) | Morton 2018; Nunes 2022 |
| Injury | Strength training reduced sports injuries to under a third and nearly halved overuse injuries in pooled RCTs. The trials were in athletes aged roughly 12-40; none tested lifters in their 30s-50s | Lauersen 2014 (pre-2016); Lauersen 2018 |

What to do:

- Do not assume you need a longer recovery window than the 48-72 h in section 1. The evidence does not show more muscle damage with age, though most of it compares young adults with 60+ adults, not people in their 30s-50s. Where an age effect exists it is slower strength recovery, which argues for watching the performance signals in section 2, not for blanket extra rest days.
- Protein toward the upper end, about 2.0 g/kg/day and 0.4-0.5 g/kg per meal, is a low-risk hedge against the age-related blunting of the protein response.
- Joint care comes from the injury-prevention evidence (balanced strength work, proprioception if there is a history), not from stretching.
- How this changes with available days: nothing age-specific changes between a 3-day and a 6-day week. If performance signals say the 6-day week is too much, the fix is the same as for any lifter: the next week is a 3-day week or a deload (section 2.3).

Gaps: no study specific to 30-50-year-old lifters' recovery time course, warm-up modifications, or joint care; masters-athlete guidance from Stronger By Science and Barbell Medicine could not be retrieved.

## 8. When to see a professional

This guide gives general training guidance only. See a physician, sports-medicine doctor, physiotherapist, or registered dietitian when:

- Performance and mood have been down for more than a few weeks despite a deload, adequate sleep, and adequate food (possible non-functional overreaching or something unrelated to training; section 2).
- Pain is sharp, localised, worsening, or present at rest, or a joint swells, locks, or gives way. Soreness that peaks at 24-72 h and fades is normal; pain that changes how you move is not.
- Sleep problems persist outside hard training blocks.
- You plan a calorie deficit larger than about 500 kcal/day, have a history of disordered eating, or have a medical condition affected by diet, protein intake, or creatine (kidney disease, for example, or medication interactions).
- You use or plan to use caffeine and have a heart condition or heart rhythm problem, high blood pressure, or an anxiety or sleep disorder; are pregnant; or take medication. The 200 mg single-dose and 400 mg/day figures in section 4.1 apply to healthy, non-pregnant adults only. Palpitations, chest pain, or feeling faint after caffeine are a reason to stop and get checked.
- You are starting a program after a long break, an injury, or a cardiovascular event, or you are over 40 and have not had a recent check-up and plan vigorous cardio.
