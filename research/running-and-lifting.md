# Running and lifting: race recovery, race-week lifting, strength for running, and scheduling runs around the rotation

Last updated: 2026-11-06

Scope: a lifter who also runs as a hobby. The lifter runs under 10 miles a week, races 10 miles on Sun 8 Nov 2026, and lifts 3-6 days a week on the six-slot rotation (L1 squat heavy, U1, L2 deadlift heavy, U2, L3 squat and deadlift volume, U3; `guide/03-flexible-scheduling.md` §1). Five questions: recovery after a 60-100 min race (KQ1), lifting in race week (KQ2), whether lifting helps running (KQ3), what recreational running costs strength and hypertrophy (KQ4), and where runs go in each week type (KQ5). The general interference meta-analyses (Schumann 2022, Lundberg 2022, Wilson 2012, Murlasits 2018, Huiberts 2024, Held 2026, Robineau 2016) are in `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` KQ5 and its 2026-10-21 addendum. They are cited here only where this note adds running-specific detail.

Method: PubMed searches (NCBI E-utilities) for each question, then the abstract of every cited paper. Open full texts on PMC were read where available (flagged "full text"). Practitioner pages were fetched directly. Where only a search summary or a second-hand account was available, that is flagged. Pre-2016 work is marked **foundational**. Outcomes are labelled **S** (strength), **H** (hypertrophy) and **R** (running). Research numbers stay in the source's units (kg, km, N·m). Lifting loads for this lifter are in pounds.

Tags: **[Evidence]** peer-reviewed; **[Practitioner]** coaches, practitioner sites and books; **[Convention]** widely repeated rules with no traceable source.

## KQ1. Recovery after a long race effort (10 miles to marathon)

### Takeaway

After a half marathon, knee-extensor strength drops about 10-13% at the finish. In most recreational samples it is back within 1-2 days. One small trial found it still down at 72 h. Creatine kinase peaks at 24 h. Jump height at 24 h is only trivially down. Soreness clears by about 48 h, and it clears before function fully returns. A marathon costs about twice as much and recovers over 3-7 days or more. Upper-body strength is barely affected. No study measured squat or deadlift 1RM after a race. For this lifter, 10 miles is more than his weekly mileage. So expect the slower end of the half-marathon range: no heavy lower-body work for 48 h, and an RPE-capped lower session from 72 h.

**Lifting's own recovery, for comparison (already in the repo)**

- `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` KQ1 and addendum: after 5 x 8-10RM squat and leg press to failure, volume load was down at 24 h and first-set volume load at 48 h (Goulart 2021). ### Inferences

- **10 miles sits between the half-marathon data and an easy long run.** The lifter's race (about 60-100 min) is shorter than the samples above (about 100-120 min). But his base (under 10 miles a week) is below theirs (Wang 2025b required ≥20 km a week). A race longer than a whole week of running is unaccustomed eccentric work. So use the conservative half-marathon numbers: strength down for 24-72 h, CK peaking at 24 h, soreness gone by about 48 h.
- **Soreness is a poor all-clear signal.** Macchi 2021 found soreness went before function came back. Use bar speed in warm-ups and the top set's RPE, not how the legs feel walking.
- **Squat is hit harder than deadlift.** Running damage concentrates in the knee extensors and calves (Wang 2025a: concentric quad slowest; Metra 2026; Petersen 2007). Hamstrings recovered within a day in Wang 2025a. So after a race, a deadlift (L2) or volume (L3) slot is a better first lower session than the heavy squat (L1).
- **Upper-body lifting is fine from 48 h, and probably from 24 h.** Non-local strength loss is trivial on average (Behm 2021). Central fatigue after a full marathon had resolved by 24 h (Ross 2007). The one contrary study used 1 h of downhill running and 6 subjects.
- **3-day vs 5-6-day weeks.** In a 5-6-day week, the upper slots (U1-U3) absorb the first 48 h after the race and the rotation continues with no change. In a 3-day week every session is full-body. So the first session after the race either waits until 72 h, or runs its lower half capped at RPE 7 with fewer back-off sets. The cleanest option is to take the upper half of the owed block on its own (see Proposed rules).

### Gaps

- No study measured squat, deadlift or leg-press 1RM, or reps at a fixed load, after a race. Every strength figure is isometric or isokinetic knee torque.
- No study used a 10-mile (16 km) road race. The nearest are 20 km (Macchi 2021) and 21.1 km.
- No study used lifters who run, or runners in an energy deficit. This lifter runs a 300-400 kcal/day deficit. Recovery could be slower; not tested.
- Petersen 2007's MVC values at days 2 and 5 are not in the abstract. Wang 2025b's introduction cites a marathon study as showing knee-extensor MVC recovered in 2 days (secondary; not checked).
- Fu 2021 was a probiotic trial with 8 subjects. Its placebo arm is the only half-marathon data showing a strength deficit at 72 h.

## KQ2. Before a race: lifting in race week

### Takeaway

The endurance taper evidence says cut training volume by 41-60%, keep intensity and frequency, and taper for 1-2 weeks. Strength is kept, or rises slightly, when lifting volume is cut by about 70% for a week, or even stopped for 3-6 days. So a race week costs the lifter nothing in strength. The risk is the other way: muscle damage 24-48 h before a race slows running. Novel eccentric work 48 h before a 30-min time trial cut performance by 4%. Heavy lower-body sessions impaired hard running for at least 6-24 h. In untrained men they impaired submaximal running for 24 h. Familiar exercises cause much less damage than new ones (repeated-bout effect).

### Cited findings

**Taper**

- **[Evidence] Bosquet et al. 2007** (foundational), *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 39(8):1358-1365 ([PubMed 17762369](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/)). Meta-analysis, 27 studies, competitive athletes (swimming, running, cycling). Best taper: 2 weeks (overall effect 0.59 ± 0.33), with volume cut exponentially by 41-60% (0.72 ± 0.36), and **no change in intensity (0.33 ± 0.14) or frequency (0.35 ± 0.17)**. R outcome. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Wang et al. 2023**, *PLoS One* 18(5):e0282838 ([PubMed 37163550](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37163550/)). Meta-analysis, 14 studies, endurance athletes. Taper improved time trials (SMD -0.45) and time to exhaustion (SMD 1.28). It did not change VO2max or economy. Effective: volume cut 41-60%, intensity and frequency kept, tapers of ≤7, 8-14 or 15-21 days, progressive or step. Overload before the taper helped more. R outcome. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Pritchard et al. 2019**, *Int J Sports Physiol Perform* 14(4):458-463 ([PubMed 30204523](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30204523/)). 11 strength-trained men, crossover. 4-week block, then a taper week with about 70% less volume and intensity either up 5.9% or down 8.5%. CMJ rose 39.3 → 40.0 cm across the taper. The higher-intensity taper gave small gains in mid-thigh pull force. S outcome. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Pritchard et al. 2018**, *J Strength Cond Res* 32(2):458-465 ([PubMed 29369954](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29369954/)). 8 resistance-trained men. After 4 weeks of training, 3.5 or 5.5 days of no training. CMJ height and isometric bench force were higher than pre-training (ES 0.30 and 0.21). Nothing fell. S outcome. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Travis et al. 2021**, *J Strength Cond Res* 35(Suppl 2):S26-S35 ([PubMed 34846328](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34846328/)). Survey of 364 raw powerlifters (practice, not a trial). Step taper over 7-10 days with volume cut 41-50%. **Final heavy (>85% 1RM) squat and deadlift 7-10 days before the meet**; last sessions at 75-80% (squat) and 70-75% (deadlift). Last deadlift 5.8 ± 2.5 days out, last squat 4.1 ± 1.9 days out. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Berryman, Mujika & Bosquet 2020**, *Sports* 9(1):1 ([PubMed 33374897](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33374897/)). 8 middle-distance runners. After 8 weeks of added plyometric or weight training, 4 weeks with no strength work. The energy-cost gain was kept (-5.75% after training, -6.31% after the break). 3000 m time improved further (-2.40% → -4.43%). R outcome. Tiny sample; the authors stress caution. Abstract. Also summarised by [Outside, 2021](https://www.outsideonline.com/health/training-performance/strength-training-taper-research) (secondary).
- `research/flexible-frequency-programming.md` (addendum, Izquierdo 2007, foundational): after 16 weeks of training, a 4-week taper (volume cut, intensity kept) gave a further +2% strength. 4 weeks of complete rest cost 6-9%.

### Inferences

- **Strength cost of a race-week cut: none.** One week at half volume with loads kept is a classic strength taper (Pritchard 2018, 2019; Izquierdo 2007). The rotation's progression is not set back.
- **The real constraint is damage within 48 h of the race.** Every running deficit above came from novel or high-volume eccentric work at 24-48 h (Marcora 2007; Braun 2003; Burt 2014; Doma 2015). Heavy sessions also cut hard-running capacity at 6 h (Doma & Deakin 2014). Economy itself had recovered by 24 h after a low-volume session in trained runners (Palmer 2001). No study tested a habitual squat session 3-4 days before a race. The 4-day buffer in the proposed rule is therefore an inference. It rests on the 48 h damage data, a 24 h margin, and the powerlifters' practice of a last squat about 4 days out (Travis 2021).
- **Familiar movements only.** The repeated-bout effect (Doma 2023) means sets of exercises done in each of the last 2-3 weeks cost far less than new ones. Race week is not the week to add lunges, a new RDL variation or jumps.
- **Taper the running too.** Cut running volume by about half in the final 7-10 days, keep the number of runs, and keep a few minutes at race pace (Bosquet 2007; Wang 2023). At under 10 miles a week this is small: the last long run falls 7 or more days out.
- **3-day vs 5-6-day race weeks.** A 3-day race week puts all lower work into two or three full-body sessions. Place the last one at least 4 days before the race (for a Sunday race: Wednesday or earlier). A 5-6-day race week has more lower slots than the taper allows. Run L1 and L2 early in the week. Drop or shorten L3, and fill the last 3 days with upper slots or rest. Either way the week is one pass of the rotation at reduced lower volume.

### Gaps

- No trial tested lifting in the race week of recreational runners who also lift.
- No trial compared a last heavy lower session at 2, 4 or 7 days before a race.
- The taper meta-analyses pooled competitive athletes, not hobby runners under 10 miles a week.
- RP Strength, Stronger By Science and Barbell Medicine pages on race-week lifting were searched for and not found.

## KQ3. Does lifting improve running economy and performance in recreational runners?

### Takeaway

Yes, modestly, and heavy loads matter most. Meta-analyses agree that adding strength training to running improves economy by about 2-8% (pooled about 4%). Heavy loads (≥80% 1RM, best ≥90%) beat submaximal loads and isometrics. Combining heavy lifting with plyometrics did best for performance (ES about -1.0). Plyometrics alone helped economy only at slow speeds (≤12 km/h), and its effect on performance was not significant. Effects grow with longer programmes (10-14 weeks beat 6-8). VO2max does not change. Strength training also improves economy late in a 90-min run, which matters in a 10-mile race. The evidence in recreational runners is mixed: some trials showed faster 10 km times or better economy, one showed neither.

### Cited findings

**Meta-analyses**

**Practitioner**

- **[Practitioner] Alex Viada**, in an interview with BarBend (Nick English; updated 2023; [page](https://barbend.com/alex-viada-hybrid-training-interview/)), page read. ### Inferences

- **The lifter's lifts that do this job.** The squat top set (4 at RPE 8, about 84% 1RM) and the deadlift top set are "high load" (≥80% 1RM, Llanos-Lagos 2024a, b). The back-offs (-7%, about 78% 1RM) and the V exposures (about 75% 1RM) fall in the "submaximal" band that did not improve economy on its own. They still build the strength the top sets express. Calf raises train the plantar flexors, which the race damages. Nothing in the program is near 90% 1RM, the band with the best economy effect in Eihara 2022.
- **Missing piece: plyometrics.** Combined methods beat heavy lifting alone for performance (ES -1.04 vs -0.47). At recreational race speeds (about 10-12 km/h), plyometrics helped economy (Llanos-Lagos 2024a). A small dose of jumps at the start of lower sessions would cover it; dose not established here. Inference only. Do not add it in a race week (KQ2).
- **Dose already met.** The trials used 2-3 lower-body sessions a week for 6-14 weeks. Every week type of the rotation has two lower exposures per main lift. So the lifter already meets the dose in 3-day and 5-6-day weeks alike. More lifting days do not add running benefit.
- **Expect performance more than economy.** In recreational runners the clearer outcome was race time (Damasceno 2015) and late-race economy (Zanini 2025), not fresh economy (Ferrauti 2010; Damasceno 2015).
- **The lifter has room to gain.** A 155 lb x 4 squat at 181 lb bodyweight is about 1.0x bodyweight estimated 1RM. Støren's runners gained 33% in 8 weeks from heavy half squats. The rotation's normal progression is the running-strength programme. No separate "runner's strength" work is needed.

### Gaps

- Most trials used trained or competitive runners. The few recreational trials are small (n = 18-38) and conflict.
- No trial used lifters who run as a hobby, so the direction of transfer (a lifting program helping a hobby runner) is inferred.
- No trial tested strength training while in an energy deficit.
- The plyometric dose for a lifter-runner was not searched for.

## KQ4. Recreational running versus strength and hypertrophy goals

### Takeaway

At 2-4 runs a week, running costs this lifter little hypertrophy and a small amount of lower-body strength. Most of that cost comes from doing both in the same session. In trained lifters, concurrent training cut lower-body 1RM gains (ES -0.35), but only in the same session (-0.66). In separate sessions the effect was negligible (-0.10, n.s.). Whether intervals cost more than easy running is contested: work-matched intervals and steady cycling cost the same strength, but intervals blunted leg lean-mass gain in one trial. Sprint intervals cost nothing in a 2026 meta-analysis. Aerobic work cut leg-press reps for up to 8 h, not at 24 h. A heavy lower session impaired the next day's hard running in runners. The day after, easy running was mostly unaffected in trained runners.

### Cited findings

**Running-specific and new evidence**

- **[Evidence] Petré et al. 2021**, *Sports Med* 51(5):991-1010 ([PubMed 33751469](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33751469/)). 27 studies, lower-body 1RM (S). Concurrent vs lifting only: **trained ES -0.35 (p < 0.01)**; moderately trained -0.20 (p = 0.08); untrained 0.03. In trained people: **same session -0.66 vs different sessions -0.10 (p = 0.55)**. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Sabag et al. 2018**, *J Sports Sci* 36(21):2472-2483 ([PubMed 29658408](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29658408/)). HIIT plus lifting vs lifting alone. Hypertrophy and upper-body strength unaffected. Lower-body strength ES -0.248 (p = 0.049). **Running HIIT -0.176 (p = 0.261) vs cycling HIIT -0.377 (p = 0.074).** Longer rest between modes may help. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Fyfe et al. 2016**, *Front Physiol* 7:487 ([PubMed 27857692](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27857692/)). 23 recreationally active men, 8 weeks, 3x/week, **cycling**. Leg-press 1RM: lifting only +38.5%, plus HIIT +28.7%, plus work-matched steady cycling +27.5%. Lower-body lean mass: lifting only +4.1%, steady +3.6%, **HIIT +1.8%**. Intensity did not change the strength cost. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Ferraro-Farro et al. 2026**, *Int J Sports Med* 47(10):747-759 ([PubMed 41734815](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41734815/)). 9 RCTs, 177 adults. Sprint interval training plus lifting vs lifting alone: lower-body strength SMD 0.01, upper-body -0.06, jump 0.11 (all n.s.). VO2max +0.78. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Feuerbacher et al. 2025**, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 35(2):e70025 ([PubMed 39921365](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39921365/)). 96 recreationally active adults (56 men), 12 weeks. Lower-body HIIT (modality not stated in the abstract) plus lower-body lifting vs lifting only. No impairment of squat 1RM, velocity or vastus lateralis size; the concurrent group's vastus lateralis grew, the lifting-only group's did not. A 2026 follow-up on 32 of the men found squat bar velocity fell in the concurrent group (p = 0.006), with a non-significant group difference (p = 0.083) ([PubMed 42370705](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42370705/)). Abstracts.
- **[Evidence] Jones et al. 2013** (foundational), *J Strength Cond Res* 27(12):3342-3351 ([PubMed 24270456](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24270456/)). 24 men with >2 years of lifting, 6 weeks, isolated-limb model. A 3:1 lifting-to-endurance ratio kept strength gains; 1:1 cut strength and limb-girth gains. "The frequency of endurance training should remain low if the primary focus … is strength and hypertrophy." Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Sale et al. 1990** (foundational), *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 22(3):348-356 ([PubMed 2381303](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2381303/)). 15 young men, 20 weeks, cycling intervals. Leg-press 1RM +13% when lifting and cycling shared a day vs +25% on separate days. Muscle size and fibre area grew equally. Abstract.

**Acute carry-over between running and lifting**

- **[Evidence] Sporer & Wenger 2003** (foundational), *J Strength Cond Res* 17(4):638-644 ([PubMed 14636098](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14636098/)). 16 men. Interval or steady aerobic work, then 4 sets at about 75% 1RM. **Leg-press reps fell at 4 h and 8 h; not at 24 h.** Bench press unaffected (local effect). Intensity did not matter. Mode not given in the abstract. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Panissa et al. 2015** (foundational), *J Strength Cond Res* 29(4):1077-1082 ([PubMed 25259468](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25259468/)). 10 active men. 15 x 1 min intervals on a treadmill or a bike, then half squats at 80% 1RM. Both cut first-set reps; cycling cut them more. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Doma & Deakin 2013** (foundational), *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 38(6):651-656 ([PubMed 23724883](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23724883/)). 14 trained runners; lifting and running 6 h apart, then a test the next day. Lift then run: next-day oxygen cost 0.76 and 0.77 vs 0.72 and 0.70 at baseline (+6% and +10%); **time to exhaustion 237.8 s vs 335.4 s (-29%)**. Run then lift: 275.3 s (-18%). Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Doma & Deakin 2015** (foundational), *Res Q Exerc Sport* 86(4):387-396 ([PubMed 26241612](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26241612/)). 24 moderately trained people. Lifting on alternate days plus running on consecutive days for 6 days: small effects on running cost (ES 0.17-0.41), but moderate to large drops in time to exhaustion and MVC (ES 0.65-2.00), and large rises in soreness. Abstract.
- KQ2 table: Palmer 2001 (economy normal at 24 h); Doma & Deakin 2014 (hard running down at 6 h); Doma 2015, 2017a (untrained: submaximal running down to 24-48 h).

**Practitioner**

- **[Practitioner] Greg Nuckols, Stronger By Science, 2022**, "The interference effect is getting less scary by the day" ([page](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=55703)), read via page fetch. On Schumann 2022: interference "doesn't really exist in any generalizable sense for strength and hypertrophy outcomes", but endurance work "does seem to consistently and significantly affect explosive performance". "A powerlifter may not notice any negative effects from jogging a few times per week."
- **[Practitioner] Nick Occhipinti, Barbell Medicine, updated 25 Nov 2025**, "Concurrent Training and the Interference Effect" ([page](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/concurrent-training-and-the-interference-effect/)), read via page fetch. Same day: conditioning after lifting, at "~60-80% max heart rate". Running and swimming "seem to cause more fatigue" than cycling, rowing or elliptical, stated as experience, not data. For strength athletes before a meet, conditioning tapers from 200 min/week (5 weeks out) to 30 min/week (1 week out).
- **[Practitioner] Alex Viada**, BarBend interview (above). "The biggest mistake … is to do a lot of heavy intervals … it's not going to make you significantly better aerobically, and all it's gonna do is hurt your squatting." Adding endurance costs "10 to 15 percent more training minutes". Keep runs easy: "it is always tempting to up the intensity and to turn a training run into an interval run."

### Inferences

- **At this dose, the main cost is fatigue, not lost adaptation.** Under 10 to about 20 miles a week is 2-4 runs. Several trials above used 2-3 endurance sessions a week (Sale 1990: 2; Fyfe 2016: 3). That is about this lifter's dose. In separate sessions, trained lifters showed no reliable 1RM cost (Petré 2021). Hypertrophy was not reduced in the largest analyses (Schumann 2022; Held 2026). The type I fibre cost of running (Lundberg 2022, repo) is the one running-specific hypertrophy signal.
- **Easy runs are nearly free. Intervals and long runs are not.** The evidence on intensity conflicts (see Conflicts). The practical signal is consistent, though: hard or long running causes muscle damage and next-day deficits, and easy running under about 45 min does not (Palmer 2001; de Carvalho e Silva 2022). So keep most runs easy (Viada; Barbell Medicine) and treat the long run and any quality run as lower-body stress.
- **How running counts against lower-body recovery.** Count a long run (about 60 min or more) or a quality session as one lower-body fatigue event for scheduling. Keep it 48 h before a heavy lower slot and 24 h after one (KQ5). Easy runs of 45 min or less do not count. Do not count running as leg sets for hypertrophy, and do not cut leg sets for it at this mileage.
- **When running should cost leg volume.** If running rises above about 15-20 miles a week or includes two quality sessions, trim leg accessories first. Never trim main-lift top sets (the priority stack, `guide/03` §4.3). No study sets this threshold; it is an inference from Jones 2013 (frequency) and Wilson 2012 (duration).
- **3-day vs 5-6-day weeks.** In a 3-day week every lifting day has heavy lower work and there are four free days. Runs go on free days, so same-session interference is easy to avoid. In a 5-6-day week, runs share days with lifting. Lift first and run 6 h or more later (Robineau 2016, repo). Put runs on upper days (U1-U3). Keep weekly running nearer the low end, because there are fewer 48 h windows before heavy lower slots.
- **10k+ steps a day.** Walking is not counted as running. It was part of baseline in all the studies above.

### Gaps

- No study set a weekly running distance above which interference starts. The meta-analyses verified here do not report a weekly-km moderator.
- Few hypertrophy outcomes come from running specifically. Most HIIT trials used bikes.
- No study tested running the day after a heavy squat in experienced lifters (the Doma trials used untrained men or runners).
- The Feuerbacher 2025 HIIT modality was not confirmed (abstract only).

## KQ5. Scheduling: where the runs go

### Takeaway

The timing rules fall out of KQ1-KQ4. Aerobic work hurts leg-press reps for up to 8 h. A heavy lower session hurts hard running for at least 6 h and often the next day. Same-day sessions are where trained lifters lose 1RM. So: on a lower day, lift before running, 6 h apart where possible; on an upper day, order does not matter. Keep the long run and any quality run at least 48 h before a heavy lower slot (L1, L2, Full A, Full B), and off the day after one unless stacked on the same day. Easy runs can go almost anywhere 3-6 h after lifting, best on upper days. The volume slot (L3, Full C) is the best lower day to sit next to a run. Practitioners say keep runs easy and either group hard days together or keep heavy legs away from hard running. Neither practice has been tested.

### Cited findings

- Evidence constraints, all cited above: Sporer 2003 (aerobic work cut leg-press reps at 4-8 h, not 24 h); Palmer 2001 (economy back at 24 h); Doma & Deakin 2013 (next-day hard running -29% after lifting then running); Doma & Deakin 2014 (hard running down at 6 h after heavy lifting); Petré 2021 and Sale 1990 (same-session or same-day concurrent training costs 1RM); Robineau 2016 (repo: ≥6 h between sessions); Murlasits 2018 (repo: lift first in the same session).
- **[Practitioner] Alex Viada, second-hand.** CoachWeb's summary of his approach: "Perform all the activities that create the same kind of fatigue at the same time": heavy upper-body work with heavy sprint intervals; long runs with slower, bodybuilding-style work ([CoachWeb](https://www.coachweb.com/exercises/4524/how-to-be-fast-and-strong-at-the-same-time); seen as a search excerpt only, not opened). His book *The Hybrid Athlete* (2015) was not read.
- **[Practitioner] Barbell Medicine 2025** (above): conditioning after lifting, at moderate intensity.
- **[Convention] "One easy day per mile raced"** after a race. Attributed to Pete Pfitzinger by a recovery-planner site ([rundida](https://rundida.com/tools/recovery-planner/)) and to other coaches elsewhere. Sources disagree whether the days are rest or easy running. No primary source found. For 10 miles: about 10 days without hard running.

### Inferences: weekly layouts (examples)

Slot types: heavy lower = L1, L2, Full A, Full B. Volume lower = L3, Full C. Upper = U1, U2, U3. "PM" means at least 6 h after that day's lifting. Days are examples; the rules matter more than the weekdays. The rotation pointer still decides which slot comes next (`guide/03` §1.4).

| Week type (`guide/03` §1.3) | Example lifting days | Easy run | Quality run (optional) | Long run | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3-day: Full A, Full B, Full C | Mon, Wed, Fri | Tue | Wed PM, after Full B (stacked on a heavy day so Thu stays easy) | Sat (24 h after Full C, a volume day; 48 h before Mon Full A) | Sun; Thu rest or easy |
| 4-day: Full A, L2, U2, Full C | Mon, Wed, Thu, Fri | Tue; or Thu PM after U2 | Wed PM, after L2 (stacked) | Sat (after Full C; 48 h before Mon Full A) | Sun |
| 5-day: L1, U1, L2, U2, Full C | Mon-Fri | Tue PM, after U1 | Wed PM, after L2 (stacked); or Thu after U2, expecting it to feel harder | Sat (48 h before Mon L1) | Sun |
| 6-day: L1, U1, L2, U2, L3, U3 | Mon-Sat | Tue PM, after U1 | Wed PM, after L2 (stacked); or drop it this week | Sat AM, then U3 later that day (48 h before Mon L1) | Sun |

Rules behind the table (inference):

1. **Same day as a lower slot: lift first, run 6 h later where possible, 3 h at minimum** (Robineau 2016; Murlasits 2018, repo). Never run before a lower slot on the same day (Sporer 2003: leg-press reps down at 4-8 h).
2. **Same day as an upper slot: either order.** In Sporer 2003 the deficit after aerobic work was local to the legs; bench press was unaffected.
3. **Long run and quality run: at least 48 h before a heavy lower slot.** Next to a volume slot (L3, Full C), 24 h is enough.
4. **Avoid the long or quality run on the day after a heavy lower slot**, unless it is stacked on the same day as the lift. A quality run the day after L1 or L2 will feel harder (Doma & Deakin 2013: -29% time to exhaustion). Either accept that or move it. With 5-6 lifting days, stacking after L2 is the only clean slot for a quality run.
5. **Easy runs (45 min or less): anywhere, 3-6 h after lifting.** Upper days first.
6. **Never sacrifice a top set for a run.** If a week cannot fit a run under rules 1-4, drop the quality run first, then shorten the long run. The rotation does not change.
7. **More lifting days, fewer hard runs.** In a 6-day week, the guide's cardio floor (`guide/03` §7) means two runs (one long, one easy) is the default. A 3-day week has room for three or four.

### Gaps

- No trial compared run placements around a lifting rotation.
- "Stack hard days" (Viada, second-hand) and "keep hard running away from heavy legs" (Barbell Medicine; Doma) both appear in practice. They agree only on "lift first". The layouts above use stacking for the quality run only, never for the long run.
- Viada's own text was not read; only an interview and a second-hand summary.

## Proposed rules for the guide (all inference)

These are proposals for `guide/06-recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` §5 and `guide/03-flexible-scheduling.md` §7. Each traces to the KQ above.

### Race-week rule (race day = R)

| Day | Lower body | Upper body | Running |
|---|---|---|---|
| R-7 to R-5 | Normal slots, but back-off and accessory sets about halved; top-set loads unchanged (KQ2: Bosquet 2007; Pritchard 2019) | Normal | Last long run at R-7 or earlier; total volume down 40-60%, same number of runs |
| R-4 | **Last heavy lower exposure** (top set; at most 1-2 back-offs). Familiar exercises only, no new eccentric-heavy accessories (Doma 2023; Marcora 2007) | Normal | Easy |
| R-3 to R-1 | None | Normal through R-2; R-1 rest or a short, light upper session | Short easy runs; a few strides or a few minutes at race pace at R-2 or R-3 |

- The race week is one pass of the rotation at reduced lower volume. It is not a deload by `guide/03` §4.5, and missed lower sets are dropped, not postponed.
- A lower slot owed after R-4 moves past the race and follows the after-race rule.
- 3-day race week: merge so that the last full-body session with lower work falls on or before R-4. 5-6-day race week: L1 and L2 early in the week; drop or shorten L3; upper slots or rest fill R-3 to R-1.
- If a scheduled deload falls within a week of the race, consider moving it to race week. Both cut volume and keep the load.

### After-race rule (10 miles to half marathon, about 60-120 min)

| Day | Lower body | Upper body | Running |
|---|---|---|---|
| R+0 | None | None | No cool-down jog (Wiewelhove 2018: a 15-min jog straight after the race worsened CK and perceived recovery at 24 h) |
| R+1 | None | Optional; rest is fine | Walk only |
| R+2 | **None** (strength still down at 24-48 h, sometimes 72 h; KQ1) | Normal upper slot | Optional easy 20-30 min |
| R+3 | **First lower session allowed.** Prefer a deadlift (L2) or volume (L3, Full C) slot over L1. Top set capped at RPE 7; back-offs 1-2 sets | Normal | Easy only |
| R+4 to R+7 | Normal rotation. First heavy squat (L1) at R+4 or later | Normal | Easy only |
| R+7 to R+10 | Normal | Normal | First quality or long run (convention: one easy day per mile) |

- **Logging.** A post-race top set below plan is a **hold**, not a miss. It does not count toward the stall rule (same logic as merged-session holds, `templates/progression-tracks.md` §3). Use warm-up bar speed and top-set RPE, not soreness, to judge readiness (Macchi 2021).
- **Rotation.** If the pointer names a lower slot within 48 h of the race, take the upper half of that block first. Do the lower half at R+3 or later. This swaps halves within one block; it does not skip ahead.
- **3-day week after the race.** The first full-body session goes at R+2 or later, with its lower half capped at RPE 7 and back-offs cut. Or run the first session as its upper half only.
- **5-6-day week after the race.** Upper slots take R+1 and R+2; the rotation otherwise runs unchanged.
- **Marathon (if ever raced).** Double the windows: no heavy lower for about 4-5 days, capped lower sessions through day 7 (Petersen 2007; Sherman 1984; Bernat-Adell 2021).
- **This week.** The plan written for 9-15 Nov (`data/weekly-plan.md`: Mon rest; Tue U1 at R+2; Wed Full B with the deadlift at RPE 7 if sore at R+3; Fri Full C at R+5; Sun L1 at R+7) already fits these rules.

### Steady-state rules

- Lower day: lift first, run 6 h later where possible (3 h minimum); never run before a lower slot the same day. Upper day: either order.
- Long and quality runs: at least 48 h before L1, L2, Full A or Full B; avoid the day after one, unless stacked on the same day.
- Easy runs (45 min or less): free placement, upper days first.
- Most runs easy; at most one quality run a week; none in 6-day weeks if recovery markers slip.
- The rotation's heavy top sets already are the strength work that helps running. Consider adding a small plyometric block outside race weeks.

| Strength recovery after a half marathon | Back within 1-2 days (Wang 2025a, n = 38; Wang 2025b, n = 36; isokinetic) | Still down at 72 h (Fu 2021 placebo arm, n = 8; isometric) | Plan on the slower figure for an under-trained runner: no heavy lower before 72 h |
| Easy running in the week after a marathon | Helps: 40 min at 48, 96, 144 h did not slow marker recovery and improved squat jump at 96 h (Martínez-Navarro 2021, n = 64) | Hurts: 20-45 min a day (40-45 min by days 5-7) slowed torque recovery through day 7 (Sherman 1984, n = 10, foundational) | Different outcomes (jump and CK vs isokinetic torque). Short easy runs are allowed after R+2; do not expect them to speed strength recovery |
| Running vs cycling | Running costs more: type I fibre growth (Lundberg 2022, repo); strength and hypertrophy (Wilson 2012, repo) | Cycling costs more: lower-body strength after HIIT (Sabag 2018); next-set squat reps (Panissa 2015). No modality effect (Schumann 2022, repo) | Running is the lifter's hobby, so the rules manage running rather than replace it. The guide's "prefer cycling on heavy leg weeks" stays an option, not a requirement |
| Interval vs steady endurance work | Intensity does not change the strength cost; intervals cut leg lean-mass gain (Fyfe 2016, cycling) | Sprint intervals cost nothing (Ferraro-Farro 2026); lower-body HIIT cost nothing over 12 weeks (Feuerbacher 2025). Practitioners say intervals "hurt your squatting" (Viada) | Keep most runs easy; at most one quality run a week |
| Upper body after a long run | Trivial non-local effect (Behm 2021, 52 studies; Jaén-Carrillo 2025) | Elbow-flexor strength -17% at 24 h (Brandenberger 2021, n = 6, downhill) | Upper slots allowed from R+1 or R+2 |
| Does lifting help recreational runners? | Yes: 10 km +2.5% (Damasceno 2015); economy -5 to -6.4% (Li 2021) | No economy change (Ferrauti 2010; Damasceno 2015); no endurance benefit when lifting after runs (Schumann 2015) | Expect race-time and late-race benefits, not fresh-economy changes |
| Plyometrics | Trivial for economy (Eihara 2022); performance n.s. (Llanos-Lagos 2024b) | Helps economy at ≤12 km/h (Llanos-Lagos 2024a); 2-5 km time trial ES 0.88 (Ramirez-Campillo 2021) | Optional small add-on, outside race weeks |
| When to stop heavy lifting before a race | 10-14 days before a half (RunnersConnect, running-coach site) | Powerlifters' last heavy squat and deadlift 7-10 days before a meet, last squat about 4 days out (Travis 2021). The running deficits found came from damage at 24-48 h | Last heavy lower exposure at R-4, at reduced volume. Untested; revisit if race performance suffers |
| Same-day stacking vs separation | Group same-type fatigue on one day (Viada, second-hand) | Lift then run on one day impaired next-day running (Doma & Deakin 2013); same-day training costs 1RM (Petré 2021; Sale 1990) | Separation by default; stacking only for the quality run, with the lift first |
