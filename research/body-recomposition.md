# Body Recomposition, Maintenance Calories, and Population Body-Composition Norms

Last updated: 2026-10-21

Research notes compiled 2026-10-21. Scope: how to estimate maintenance calories, how fast a lifter can lose fat and gain muscle at the same time (recomposition), whether a returning lifter regains muscle faster, and population reference values for height, weight, BMI, body fat and fat-free mass index (FFMI). Method note: only the CDC anthropometric report was read in full (PDF text extracted). Everything else comes from abstracts, repository records and secondary summaries surfaced in search; each item says which. Units: kilograms (kg) and kilocalories (kcal), with pounds (lb) where a source reports them.

This note adds to `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` (KQ4). It does not change that note's conclusions.

## Key Question 1: Estimating maintenance calories

### Takeaway
Use a resting-metabolic-rate (RMR) equation times an activity factor to get a first estimate. Then correct it with 2-3 weeks of bodyweight trend at a known intake. The equations are only a starting point; the scale trend is the measurement.

### Cited Findings
- Mifflin-St Jeor equation, men: RMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age (y) + 5. — Mifflin et al. 1990, Am J Clin Nutr 51(2):241-7 (older foundational; equation quoted from memory of the widely reproduced form, not re-read this round).
- Katch-McArdle equation: RMR = 370 + 21.6 × fat-free mass (kg). Uses lean mass, so it suits people with a body-composition scan. (Practitioner formula; primary source not retrieved.)
- Activity multipliers on RMR, as commonly used: about 1.375 (light), 1.55 (moderate), 1.725 (very active). (Practitioner convention; no primary source retrieved.)
- Rule of thumb for lifters: 30-35 kcal per kg bodyweight per day (about 14-16 kcal/lb). (Practitioner convention; not cited in any note.)
- Running costs roughly 100 kcal per mile for a person of about 80 kg. (Practitioner rule of thumb; not verified this round.)

### Inferences
- When a person gains weight steadily without tracking, intake is above maintenance by roughly 500 kcal/day per lb/week gained (using the approximate 3,500 kcal per lb of tissue). Early gains include water, so this overstates the surplus somewhat.

### Gaps
- No validation study comparing these equations in lean, trained men was retrieved.
- The activity multipliers have no primary source in this repository.

## Key Question 2: How fast can recomposition happen?

### Takeaway
Simultaneous fat loss and muscle gain is documented in trained lifters, not only in beginners and people with obesity. It is fastest in people who are returning to training, carry recent fat gain, eat high protein, and train hard. Well-controlled timelines are scarce; most trials report a single 4-12 week endpoint.

### Cited Findings
- Review: "Body Recomposition: Can Trained Individuals Build Muscle and Lose Fat at the Same Time?" Strength and Conditioning Journal 42(5):7-21, 2020 (Barakat et al.), DOI 10.1519/SSC.0000000000000584. Says a substantial literature shows recomposition in resistance-trained people. Key factors: progressive resistance training and evidence-based nutrition (high protein); also covers sleep and limits of body-composition measurement. Abstract record only; specific protein figures attributed to it by blogs (1.6-2.4 g/kg/day) are unverified. — [Library record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4066252?lng=en)
- Longland et al. 2016, Am J Clin Nutr: 4 weeks, ~40% energy deficit, resistance training plus high-intensity intervals 6 days/week, 40 recreationally active overweight young men. Protein 2.4 g/kg/day: lean body mass +1.2 ± 1.0 kg, fat mass −4.8 ± 1.6 kg. Protein 1.2 g/kg/day: lean mass +0.1 ± 1.0 kg, fat −3.5 ± 1.4 kg. Performance improved similarly in both. Abstract only. Extreme conditions; wide individual variation. — [PubMed 26817506](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01776359](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01776359)
- Murphy & Koehler meta-analysis (in `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md`, KQ4): a deficit of about 500 kcal/day held lean mass flat on average; larger deficits cost lean mass roughly linearly. (Secondary.)

### Muscle memory: regaining previous muscle
- Halonen et al. 2024: 55 untrained adults (age 32 ± 5). Periodic group: 10 weeks training, 10 weeks off, 10 weeks retraining. Continuous group: 10 weeks control, then 20 weeks training. Strength and muscle size fell during the break. In the first 5 weeks of retraining, leg-press 1RM and vastus lateralis and biceps cross-sectional area rose faster than in the continuous group at the same stage (p < 0.01). Both groups ended with similar results. Abstract only. — [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_97584); [PMC12659766](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12659766/)
- Cumming et al. 2024: after 16 weeks of detraining, muscle size fell but myonuclear content stayed about 33% higher than in the untrained arm, mainly in type 2 fibres. Secondary summary only. — [train.fitness summary](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/the-secret-to-strength-comebacks)
- Seaborne et al. 2018 (epigenetic memory in human muscle) and Psilander et al. 2019 (human retraining) were not retrieved this round.
- Competing explanation: faster regain may come from retained motor-unit recruitment rather than myonuclei. (Secondary.)

### Rate-of-gain models (practitioner)
- Alan Aragon's model, monthly muscle gain as a share of bodyweight: beginner 1-1.5%, intermediate 0.5-1%, advanced 0.25-0.5%. Assumes optimal training and a normal body-fat level. Critics call it optimistic. Lyle McDonald's annual model: about 20-25 lb in year 1, 10-12 lb in year 2, 5-6 lb in year 3. Both are secondary write-ups only. — [RNT Fitness](https://www.rntfitness.co.uk/realistic-rates-muscle-gain/); [Built With Science](https://builtwithscience.com/how-to-build-muscle-fast/)

### Inferences
- For a returning lifter with recent fat gain, a deficit of about 300-400 kcal/day with protein at about 2.2 g/kg is a reasonable recomposition setting. It sits inside the deficit that held lean mass on average (Murphy & Koehler) and uses the protein level that separated the groups in Longland.
- The fastest phase is the first 2-3 months, while previous muscle is regained. It slows once previous strength levels return; after about 6 months a deliberate surplus or deficit phase is likely more efficient.
- Bioimpedance scans (InBody) vary by a few percentage points of body fat between sessions, which is the size of a 12-week recomposition. Use several scans plus bodyweight trend, waist and strength, not one scan.

### Gaps
- No study tracking recomposition month by month in trained or returning lifters was retrieved.
- The measurement error of InBody devices was not searched; the "few points" figure is general knowledge, not cited.

## Key Question 3: Population reference values (US adult men)

### Takeaway
A 25-year-old man at 5'11" and 181 lb is around the 75th percentile for height and the median for weight. BMI misclassifies muscular people as overweight. Typical body fat for men aged 20-29 is about 24-25% by DXA. Median FFMI for young men is about 19.

### Cited Findings
- CDC, Anthropometric Reference Data for Children and Adults: United States, 2015-2018 (NCHS Series 3, No. 46). Full PDF read. — [CDC PDF](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/series/sr_03/sr03-046-508.pdf)

| Weight, lb (Table 6) | 188.6 | 152.8 | 179.1 | 216.5 | 242.1 | 255.7 |

| BMI (Table 15) | 27.6 | 22.6 | 26.5 | 31.6 | 34.4 | 36.8 |

- Kelly, Wilson & Heymsfield 2009, PLOS One: DXA reference values from NHANES 1999-2004. "Fat comprises approximately 24% of body weight in males ... at age 25." Percentile tables are in supplementary Tables S2 (%fat) and S5 (lean mass/height²), not retrieved. — [PMC2737140](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2737140)
- NHANES-based analysis (Research Square preprint): men 20-29 mean body fat 25.35% (SD 8.4), n = 573. Not peer reviewed. — [Preprint PDF](https://assets-eu.researchsquare.com/files/rs-2253879/v1/5b44f2300d782b1141d4e363.pdf)
- Schutz et al. 2002: 5,635 healthy Swiss adults. Median FFMI 18.9 kg/m² for men aged 18-34 (15.4 for women). Percentile tables exist but were not retrieved. — [Record](https://sonar.ch/global/documents/165611)
- Kouri et al. 1995: FFMI of about 25 cited as the upper limit for drug-free men. Seen only in secondary sources; the normalization coefficient is printed as 6.3 in the abstract and 6.1 in most calculators. — [Calculator note](https://getfitcraft.com/tools/ffmi-calculator)
- American Council on Exercise body-fat categories, men: fitness 14-17%, average 18-24%. (Practitioner chart; not retrieved this round.)

### Inferences
- Body-fat percentiles above are estimated from means and SDs, not read from a percentile table.
- DXA usually reads higher than bioimpedance in lean, muscular people, so an InBody value should be compared to DXA norms with a few points of allowance.

### Gaps
- Exact %fat and FFMI percentile tables (Kelly 2009 Tables S2/S5; Schutz 2002) were not retrieved.
- InBody's own reference ranges for skeletal muscle mass and ECW/TBW were not sourced.
