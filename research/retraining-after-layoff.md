# Retraining after a long layoff: detraining, muscle memory, connective tissue, and a regain-phase rule

Last updated: 2026-11-06

**Scope.** What happens to strength, muscle and tendon during a break of 3-24 months, how fast they come back, how coaches bring a returning lifter back, and what that means for this program's progression rules. Five questions: detraining (KQ1), retraining and its mechanisms (KQ2), connective tissue and load-ramp injury risk (KQ3), practitioner methods (KQ4), and a proposed "regain phase" rule for this lifter (KQ5). The lifter: male, 25, trained about twice a week in 2024 (43 sessions), very little for about 18 months, restarted mid-October 2026.

**Relation to other notes.** This note extends, and does not change, `research/body-recomposition.md` KQ2 ("Muscle memory", which flagged Psilander 2019 and Seaborne 2018 as not retrieved; both are read here), `research/flexible-frequency-programming.md` (short gaps of 1-6 weeks, addendum 2026-10-21), and `templates/progression-tracks.md` sections 2, 5 and 8. Short-gap studies already covered there (Hortobágyi 1993, Hwang 2017, Ogasawara 2011 and 2013, Kraemer 2002, Izquierdo 2007, McMaster 2013, Bickel 2011, Coleman 2024) are cited by pointer, not re-summarised.

**Method.** PubMed searches through NCBI E-utilities, then the abstract of every cited paper. Full texts were read where open: Staron 1991 (PDF mirror), Seaborne 2018, Räntilä 2025 (the published follow-up of Halonen 2024's cohort), Bohm 2015, Gabbett 2016, and the unloading section of Magnusson & Kjaer 2019. Practitioner pages were fetched and read in full where the site allowed it: Stronger By Science (three articles), Barbell Medicine (article plus forum threads read through the forum's JSON endpoint), Renaissance Periodization (two articles), and a Wendler Q&A page. Starting Strength returned 403 and is reported from search snippets only. Halonen 2024 itself returned 403 (abstract only). Each finding says what was read.

**Labels.** **[Evidence]** peer-reviewed. **[Evidence, foundational]** peer-reviewed and pre-2016. **[Practitioner]** Stronger By Science, MASS, Barbell Medicine, Renaissance Periodization, program authors. **[Convention]** common coaching practice with no traceable source. Strength outcomes and hypertrophy (size) outcomes are reported separately. Units are those of the source; the lifter's numbers are in pounds.

## KQ1. Detraining after long breaks: how much strength and muscle is lost, and how fast

### Takeaway

- In young adults, maximal strength is barely affected for about 4 weeks. After that it declines slowly. Most of the strength gained survives 3-8 months off.
- Muscle size falls faster and further than strength. After 10-20 weeks off, most of the size gained is gone while a third to two thirds of the strength gain remains.
- No controlled study in young adults covers 12-24 months off. In older adults, part of a 2-year strength gain was still present after 3 years off.
- Almost every study trained previously untrained people for 6-24 weeks, then stopped them. That is close to this lifter's 2024 (about 9 months at 2 sessions a week). It is not the situation of a lifter with years of training.

### Cited findings

**Reviews and meta-analyses**

- **[Evidence, foundational] Bosquet, Berryman, Dupuy, Mekary, Arvisais, Bherer & Mujika 2013**, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 23(3):e140-9, meta-analysis of 103 studies. [PubMed 23347054](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347054/). Abstract read.
  - Training cessation reduced maximal force, SMD −0.46 (95% CI −0.54 to −0.37); submaximal strength −0.62 (−0.80 to −0.45); maximal power −0.20 (−0.28 to −0.13).
  - Dose-response with the length of cessation. Larger effects in people over 65 and in inactive people than in recreational athletes.
  - Per Nuckols' reading of the full text (Stronger By Science 2022, below; secondary): maximal strength effects were trivial (g < 0.2) up to 28 days and accelerated after that. Pooled maximal-strength effect g = 0.31 (95% CI 0.21-0.40) in adults under 65 vs 0.76 (0.62-0.90) at 65 and over.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Mujika & Padilla 2000**, *Sports Med* 30(2):79-87 (Part I, < 4 weeks) and 30(3):145-54 (Part II, > 4 weeks). [PubMed 10966148](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10966148/), [PubMed 10999420](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10999420/). Abstracts read.
  - Short term: "training-induced changes in fibre cross-sectional area are reversed, but strength performance declines are limited".
  - Long term: in strength athletes "fibre areas are significantly reduced", while "force production declines slowly, and usually remains above control values for very long periods".
- **[Evidence, foundational] McMaster et al. 2013**, elite rugby and American football players: strength held for up to 3 weeks, then decayed; −14.5% (ES −0.64) over 7.2 ± 5.8 weeks. Already verified in `research/flexible-frequency-programming.md`, addendum 2026-10-21.

### Inferences

- **Strength is stickier than size.** Every study that measured both found size lost faster and further. Räntilä 2025 is the cleanest: after 10 weeks off, about three quarters of the vastus lateralis CSA gained was gone (26.2 → 30.3 → 27.2 cm²), but only about a third of the leg-press gain (2.12 → 2.54 → 2.41 kg/kg). The usual explanation is retained neural adaptation and skill (Häkkinen 1985; Taaffe 1997; Psilander 2019).
- **For an 18-month layoff,** expect most 2024 hypertrophy to be gone and a meaningful share of 2024 strength to remain. The lifter's first three weeks fit this: bench, press and deadlift came back to about 88-100% of their 2024 e1RM (KQ5 table). The squat, at about 62%, does not fit. Possible reasons, none verified: the 2024 squat was logged without RPE and with unknown depth; the early squat exposures were capped by the program's conservative start; or the squat skill detrained more. Staron 1991 found the squat lost the least of three lifts, which argues against the last explanation.
- **3-day vs 5-6-day weeks.** Detraining is about stopping, not about weekly frequency. A 3-day week of this program is far above the maintenance doses in Bickel 2011 and Spiering 2021 (one session a week). Short weeks carry no detraining risk.

### Gaps

- No controlled detraining study in young adults lasts longer than about 32 weeks. The 12-24-month range is covered only by older adults (Smith 2003) and by inference.
- Almost no study detrains people with years of training. Coratella 2016 (trained men, 6 weeks off) is short.
- The Bosquet 2013 duration bins were not read at the primary source; the 28-day line and the age split come from Nuckols' secondary reading.

## KQ2. Retraining: how fast strength and size come back, and why

### Takeaway

- In every retraining study found, strength was back at or above the previous peak within **about 5-8 weeks**, after breaks of 3 to 32 weeks.
- Size also came back. In two studies, regrowth was faster than first-time growth over the same period (Seaborne 2018; Halonen 2024). In two others, previously trained muscle regrew no faster than untrained muscle (Psilander 2019; Cumming 2024).
- Strength returns faster than size because less of it was lost and much of it is neural. No study gives "weeks to previous peak" separately for strength and size in the same people at fine resolution.
- Mechanisms: retained neural adaptation has the clearest human support for strength. Myonuclear retention is strong in rodents and mixed in humans. Epigenetic and proteomic "memory" is shown in human muscle but not yet linked to faster regain in people.

### Cited findings

**Weeks to previous peak**

**Mechanisms**

- **Neural and skill retention.**
  - **[Evidence]** Psilander 2019: strength stayed elevated while size returned to baseline, "supporting previous findings of a long-lasting motor learning effect". Abstract.
  - **[Evidence, foundational]** Taaffe & Marcus 1997: strength came back without fibre-size change, so "much of the retention in strength ... and reacquisition of lost strength with retraining reflects neural adaptation." Abstract.
  - **[Evidence, foundational]** Kubo 2010: neural activation did not fall over 3 months off. Häkkinen 1985 found the opposite (IEMG fell with strength over 12 weeks). See Conflicts.
- **Myonuclear retention ("myonuclear permanence").**
  - **[Evidence, foundational] Bruusgaard et al. 2010**, *PNAS* 107(34):15111-6 ([PubMed 20713720](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20713720/)), mice: myonuclei were added before hypertrophy and kept during long denervation atrophy. Abstract.
  - **[Evidence, foundational] Egner et al. 2013**, *J Physiol* 591(24):6221-30 ([PubMed 24167222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24167222/)), mice: 14 days of testosterone gave +66% myonuclei and +77% fibre CSA. Fibre size returned to control within 3 weeks, myonuclei stayed elevated for at least 3 months. Six days of overload then gave +31% fibre CSA vs no significant growth in controls. Abstract.
  - **[Evidence] Murach et al. 2020**, *J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle* 11(6):1705-22 ([PubMed 32881361](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32881361/)), mice: myonuclei gained were lost with detraining in plantaris and gastrocnemius; full reversal within 6 months. Abstract.
  - **[Evidence] Rahmati, McCarthy & Malakoutinia 2022**, *J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle* 13(5):2276-97 ([PubMed 35961635](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35961635/)), meta-analysis of 147 articles: hypertrophy-acquired myonuclei were retained during atrophy in rodents but **not in humans**; myonuclear content fell after atrophy in humans. Conclusion: "myonuclei are not permanent". Abstract.
  - **[Evidence] Snijders et al. 2020**, *Acta Physiol* 229(3):e13465 ([PubMed 32175681](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32175681/)), review: myonuclear-permanence memory rests mainly on rodent data; human evidence "remains largely ambiguous". Abstract.
  - **[Evidence] Cumming 2024** is the strongest human support: type 2 fibres kept about 33% more myonuclei than the control arm after 16 weeks off. The authors: "the physiological benefit remains to be determined." Abstract.
- **Epigenetic and proteomic memory.**
  - **[Evidence] Seaborne 2018** (human): 18,816 hypomethylated CpG sites after reloading vs 9,153 after first loading. Several genes (for example UBR5, RPL35a, HEG1) kept their methylation change through 7 weeks off and showed the largest expression increase with reloading. Full text.
  - **[Evidence] Wen et al. 2021**, *Function* 2(5):zqab038 ([PubMed 34870208](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34870208/)), mice: methylation changes persisted in myonuclei after 12 weeks off; previously trained mice grew more over 4 weeks of retraining "with no difference in myonuclear number". Abstract.
  - **[Evidence] Hulmi et al. 2025**, *J Physiol* 603(9):2655-73 ([PubMed 40183698](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40183698/)), Halonen cohort: most training-induced proteins reversed with 10 weeks off; some (CBR1, calpain-2) stayed elevated. Abstract.

**Practitioner synthesis**

- **[Practitioner] Nuckols 2022**, Stronger By Science / MASS cover story, ["A Guide to Detraining"](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/detraining/). Full text read.
  - From Ogasawara, Taaffe, Staron, Psilander, Seaborne and Henwood & Taaffe: the time to regain lost strength and muscle is "approximately half as long as the preceding period of training cessation", with a rough range of one third to two thirds. Example: 12 weeks off, 4-8 weeks to regain, 6 the best guess.
  - On size: whole-muscle measures fall back near baseline after 7-20 weeks off, while fibre CSA is "preserved quite well". He thinks "a significant loss of contractile and structural protein occurs within 20-32 weeks".
- **[Practitioner] Nuckols 2020**, ["The Evidence for Myonuclear Muscle Memory May be Murkier Than Commonly Believed"](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/myonuclear-muscle-memory/), MASS review of Snijders 2020. Full text read. "At least some myonuclei can be lost during or following significant muscle atrophy", probably more slowly than fibres shrink.

### Inferences

- **The "half the layoff" rule does not extrapolate to 18 months.** It was fitted to breaks of 3-32 weeks. Applied here it predicts about 9 months to regain. The lifter's bench was at 95-100% of its 2024 e1RM three weeks after restarting. For long layoffs, a better working model is: a fast rebound on retained skill, then a flattening near (or a little below) the old level, where normal progression takes over. Nuckols himself switches to "treat yourself like an untrained lifter" for breaks over a year (KQ4).
- **Strength first, size later.** Expect top-set loads to recover within weeks, while muscle size lags and depends on volume and diet. The lifter is on a small deficit (recomposition). That matters more for size than for strength (`research/body-recomposition.md` KQ2). Do not read a fast strength rebound as proof that 2024 muscle is back.
- **3-day vs 5-6-day weeks.** The retraining studies used 2-3 sessions a week (Halonen 2x whole body; Seaborne and Taaffe 3x). This program gives each main lift one heavy and one volume exposure per week in every 3- to 6-day week, which sits inside that range. Nothing found suggests 5-6 days speeds strength regain. Extra days add accessory volume, which is a hypertrophy lever.

### Gaps

- No retraining study after more than 32 weeks off in young adults, and none in lifters with multi-year histories.
- Retraining studies run the original program unchanged. None compares progression rates during retraining.
- Halonen 2024's 5-week numbers (percent change at week 25) were not retrieved; the full text returned 403.

## KQ3. Connective tissue: tendon adaptation, tendon detraining, and load-ramp injury risk

### Takeaway

- Tendon adapts more slowly than muscle strength and detrains faster. In the two time-course studies, tendon stiffness rose only after 2-3 months of training and was back to baseline within 1-2 months of stopping, while strength held.
- A returning lifter's strength can therefore outrun the tendon. This mismatch is a plausible injury mechanism. No study has shown it causes injuries in adult lifters.
- Heavy loads (above about 70% of maximum) drive tendon stiffness. Light loads barely do.
- "Too much too soon" is supported by team-sport observational data. The acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR) that formalises it is disputed on statistical grounds. Treat it as a loose heuristic, not a threshold.
- Injury rates in weight-training sports are low per hour of training.

### Cited findings

**Tendon adaptation and detraining**

- **[Evidence, foundational] Bohm, Mersmann & Arampatzis 2015**, *Sports Med Open* 1:7, meta-analysis of 27 studies, 37 interventions, 264 participants, Achilles and patellar tendon, interventions of 8 weeks or more ([PMC4532714](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4532714/)). Full text read.
  - Stiffness SMD 0.70 (95% CI 0.51-0.88); Young's modulus 0.69 (0.36-1.03); CSA 0.24 (0.07-0.42).
  - Loading above 70% of MVC or RM: stiffness SMD 0.90 (0.71-1.08), n = 27 interventions. Lower intensity: 0.04 (−0.46 to 0.53), n = 5. Difference p < 0.00001.
  - Duration ≥ 12 weeks: 0.91 (0.71-1.12), n = 23; 8-12 weeks: 0.81 (0.33-1.29), n = 4; p = 0.7. The authors still recommend "over a longer intervention duration (>12 weeks)".
- **[Evidence, foundational] Kubo et al. 2010** (table in KQ1): tendon stiffness and muscle CSA "did not change until 2 months of training". In detraining, CSA was back to baseline at 1 month and tendon stiffness at 2 months, while strength and neural activation did not change. Authors: tendon and muscle-size adaptations to training "are slower than those of muscle function and inversely ... faster" to detraining. Abstract.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Kubo et al. 2012**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 112(7):2679-91 ([PubMed 22105708](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22105708/)), 9 men, 3 months isometric plantar flexion then 3 months off: Achilles stiffness unchanged until 2 months, +50.3% at 3 months, back to pre-training **after 1 month** off. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Magnusson & Kjaer 2019**, *J Physiol* 597(5):1283-98, review ([PMC6395417](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6395417/)). Unloading section read.
  - "Rather short-term (1-2 weeks) limb unloading seems to significantly reduce the stiffness of the tendon", without a change in tendon CSA. CSA falls only "during extremely protracted periods of inactivity".
  - 2-3 weeks of lower-limb immobilisation cut tendon collagen synthesis by about 80% (citing de Boer et al.).
  - Reported tendon collagen synthesis rates range from about 0.2% to 1% per 24 h.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Heinemeier et al. 2013**, *FASEB J* 27(5):2074-9 ([PubMed 23401563](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23401563/)), 28 forensic Achilles samples, bomb-pulse ¹⁴C: the tendon core "is formed during height growth and is essentially not renewed thereafter"; muscle showed continuous turnover. Abstract. Adult tendon adaptation happens in properties and in the periphery, not by replacing the core.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Couppé et al. 2008**, *J Appl Physiol* 105(3):805-10 ([PubMed 18556433](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18556433/)), 7 elite fencers and badminton players: the habitually loaded leg had larger patellar tendon CSA (distal 139 vs 116 mm²) and stiffness (4,766 vs 3,494 N/mm). Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Mersmann, Bohm & Arampatzis 2017**, *Front Physiol* 8:987 ([PubMed 29249987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29249987/)), narrative review: muscle and tendon "differ with regard to both the time course of adaptation"; an imbalance between muscle strength and tendon properties is proposed as a tendinopathy risk in adolescent athletes. Abstract.

**Injury rates and return-to-training spikes**

- **[Evidence] Aasa et al. 2017**, *Br J Sports Med* 51(4):211-9 ([PubMed 27707741](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27707741/)), 9 studies, mostly low quality: powerlifting 1.0-4.4 and weightlifting 2.4-3.3 injuries per 1,000 training hours. Spine, shoulder and knee most common. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Keogh & Winwood 2017**, *Sports Med* 47(3):479-501 ([PubMed 27328853](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27328853/)), 20 studies, mostly retrospective: bodybuilding 0.24-1 injury per 1,000 h; strongman 4.5-6.1. Shoulder, lower back and knee most often injured; strains, tendinitis and sprains most common. Abstract.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Hootman, Dick & Agel 2007**, *J Athl Train* 42(2):311-9 ([PubMed 17710181](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17710181/)), 16 years of NCAA data: preseason practice injuries 6.6 per 1,000 athlete-exposures vs 2.3 in-season and 1.4 postseason. Abstract. Team sports; preseason also differs in intensity and contact, not only in the return from a break.
- **[Evidence, foundational, observational] Myer et al. 2011**, *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 41(10):702-5 ([PubMed 21941038](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21941038/)): after the 2011 NFL lockout removed the usual off-season conditioning, 10 Achilles tendon injuries occurred in the first 12 days of training camp and 2 more in the next 17 days, against about 4 ruptures a year historically. Full text not read; numbers from the [Ohio State press release](https://wexnermedical.osu.edu/mediaroom/pressreleaselisting/did-nfl-lockout-provide-lessons-to-minimize-injury-risk) and secondary citations.

**Acute:chronic workload ratio, for and against**

- **[Evidence] Gabbett 2016**, *Br J Sports Med* 50(5):273-80 ([PMC4789704](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4789704/)). Full text read. Team sports.
  - ACWR 0.8-1.3 described as the "sweet spot"; ≥ 1.5 as the "danger zone".
  - In the team-sport data he reviews, when weekly load rose ≥ 15% over the previous week, injury risk rose to 21-49%, vs < 10% when load stayed within −5% to +10%. In Australian football, 40% of injuries followed a > 10% weekly change (Piggott et al., as cited). Recommendation: limit weekly increases to < 10%.
- **[Evidence] Andrade et al. 2020**, *Sports Med* 50(9):1613-35 ([PubMed 32572824](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32572824/)), systematic review, 20 studies, 2,375 injuries in 1,234 elite male team-sport athletes: most studies suggest higher ACWR means higher time-loss injury risk; 14 different binning methods "limit the strength of recommendations". Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Griffin et al. 2020**, *Sports Med* 50(3):561-80 ([PubMed 31691167](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31691167/)), 22 articles: supports an association with non-contact injury, as one part of wider monitoring; the exponentially weighted version preferred. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Impellizzeri et al. 2020**, *Int J Sports Physiol Perform* 15(6):907-13 ([PubMed 32502973](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32502973/)): "There is no evidence supporting the use of ACWR in training-load-management systems or for training recommendations aimed at reducing injury risk"; no study has estimated causal effects. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Impellizzeri et al. 2021**, *Sports Med* 51(3):581-92 ([PubMed 33332011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33332011/)): dividing acute load by fixed or random "chronic" loads reproduced the original association (OR 1.95, 95% CI 1.18-3.52 with a fixed denominator; 1.16-2.07 with random ones, vs 2.45, 1.28-4.71, for the real ratio). Neither ACWR nor acute load alone predicted injury meaningfully (c-statistic 0.574 and 0.544 vs 0.5). They recommend dismissing ACWR. Abstract.
- **[Evidence] Lolli et al. 2019**, *Br J Sports Med* 53(15):921-2 ([PubMed 29101104](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29101104/)): mathematical coupling causes spurious correlation in the usual ACWR calculation. Title and record only (no abstract).
- **[Practitioner] Eure 2020**, Stronger By Science, ["Risks of Returning to Training"](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/risks-of-returning/) (physical therapist). Full text read. ACWR "does not seem to be panning out" for the weight room, but "the idea that we shouldn't drastically change our training volume is hardly a radical one". In powerlifting and bodybuilding, prior injury is "one of the only variables that is strongly predictive on an individual level".

### Inferences

- **The mismatch window.** If Kubo's time courses apply to dynamic barbell training, a lifter 3-8 weeks into retraining has regained most of his strength, while his tendons have had only that long to respond to a stimulus that takes 2-3 months to change stiffness. This is the main argument for capping how fast loads climb even when RPE says a lift is easy. It is a mechanism argument, not injury data.
- **Keep loads heavy, ramp them steadily.** Tendon stiffness responds to loads above about 70% of maximum (Bohm 2015). The program's top set (4 reps at RPE 8, about 83% 1RM) is in that range. So the regain phase should not stay light for long. It should climb in moderate steps.
- **A weekly ceiling of about 10%** of the top-set load is a reasonable heuristic, borrowed from team-sport data (Gabbett 2016) whose causal status is disputed (Impellizzeri 2020, 2021). At a 155 lb squat, +10 lb is 6.5%, +15 lb is 9.7%, +20 lb is 12.9%.
- **Which tissues detrained most** (inference, not measured): the lifter runs under 10 miles a week, which probably kept some Achilles and patellar tendon loading through the layoff. The spine and hip extensors, hamstrings, shoulders and elbows had little heavy loading for 18 months. These line up with the lower back and shoulder sites that dominate lifting injury surveys (Aasa 2017; Keogh & Winwood 2017). That is the reason for extra deadlift caution in KQ5.
- **3-day vs 5-6-day weeks.** This program keeps weekly main-lift exposure constant across 3- to 6-day weeks (one H and one V per lift per pass). So the week-to-week load on any given tendon does not jump when the day count changes. In a 3-day week each session carries two heavy top sets (merged blocks), so per-session load is higher, with rest days in between. No evidence says which pattern is safer. The real spike risk is a run of 2-day weeks followed by 6-day weeks. A 2-day week is Full A and Full B with main lifts only, a top set plus one back-off (`guide/03-flexible-scheduling.md` §4.6), so it carries well under two thirds of a full pass. The weekly load ratio on the return to 6 days is then 1.5 or more. That would be the "danger zone" if ACWR were valid. The per-lift H/V structure still caps what any single lift sees.

### Gaps

- No study measured tendon properties after a 12-24-month layoff, or how fast they return with retraining.
- No study links a muscle-tendon mismatch to injuries in adult lifters.
- ACWR has never been tested in resistance training.
- The NFL lockout figures are from a press release; the paper was not read.

## KQ4. Practice: how coaches bring a returning lifter back

### Takeaway

- Practitioners agree on three things: start well below the old numbers, let RPE or reps in reserve choose the load, and expect to get back faster than the first time.
- They disagree on how fast to climb. Wendler says to ride out his normal +5 lb per cycle even when reps come easily. RP and Stronger By Science say to climb faster when reps in reserve rise, and RP names +10 lb a week instead of +5.
- For breaks over a year, Stronger By Science and Barbell Medicine both say to run a novice-style progression.
- No controlled study tests progression rates for returning lifters. The retraining studies (KQ2) simply re-ran the original program, and that sufficed to regain the old peak in 5-8 weeks.

**Supporting evidence for an easy first week** (moot for this lifter, now three weeks in):

- **[Evidence] Huang et al. 2019**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 119(5):1055-64 ([PubMed 30778759](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30778759/)), 24 untrained men: 50 eccentric contractions at 10% of MVC two days before a damaging 80% bout reduced peak soreness by 36-54% across nine muscle groups and sped strength recovery. Abstract. This is the "repeated bout" basis for Nuckols' 1/3-load first week.

### Inferences

- **This program already sits between the two camps.** It started at 80% of the recent e1RM (`templates/progression-tracks.md` §2) and has a +10 lb big step at RPE ≤ 7 (§5). That is faster than Wendler and close to RP.
- **The Stronger By Science formula, applied to the squat,** lands on the same number as RP. The 2024-equivalent top set is about 245 lb (KQ5 table). The current track is 155 lb. A deficit of about 90 lb spread over the 8-9 weeks the retraining studies suggest is about 10-11 lb a week.
- **"Treat as untrained" after a year off** fits the deadlift best. Its 2024 sets were all 6-8 reps, never pushed (KQ5). So its 2024 "best" is a soft floor, not a ceiling.
- **RPE accuracy is weakest exactly where the big step lives.** The repo already notes that lifters underpredict reps to failure by about 1 rep, and are less accurate far from failure (`templates/progression-tracks.md` §1: Halperin 2022, Refalo 2024). A rating of "RPE 7 or lower" is the least reliable part of the scale. Reps achieved at a fixed RPE near 8 are a better signal. This shapes the rule in KQ5.

### Gaps

- No controlled trial of progression rates in returning lifters.
- Starting Strength's long-layoff rules were not read; RTS has none that could be found.
- None of the practitioner rules distinguish 3-day from 5-6-day weeks.

## KQ5. Applied: a regain-phase rule for this lifter (inference)

Everything in this section is **inference** from KQ1-KQ4. It proposes a change; it does not make one. The program files are unchanged.

### Takeaway

- Only the squat (62% of its 2024 e1RM) and the row (64%) still need a regain phase. Bench, press and deadlift are within about 10% of 2024 and should run the normal rules.
- Regain-phase squat: top set of 4-6 reps stopping at RPE 8; +10 lb when reps + RIR ≥ 7 with a logged RPE; never more than +10 lb per H exposure.
- The phase ends at 90% of the 2024 e1RM (squat 263 lb), at the first top set rated RPE 8.5 or higher, or on 9 January 2027, whichever comes first.
- Deadlift: no more than one +10 lb step in any two H exposures until 9 January 2027.
- Step sizes are the same in 3-day and 5-6-day weeks. The rule differs only for 2-day weeks (a gap waiver) and for the first week back at 5-6 days after 2-day weeks (normal steps only).

### The lifter's numbers

e1RM uses the repo's convention (Epley with RPE: load × (1 + (reps + 10 − RPE) ÷ 30); unrated sets treated as reps to failure, `tools/analyze_history.py`). The 2024 sets carry no RPE, so their e1RMs assume failure. If they were really RPE 8-9, the true 2024 e1RMs were 3-7% higher. So the thresholds below are slightly conservative. The "top set" columns convert an e1RM into the program's 4 reps at RPE 8 (e1RM ÷ 1.2, nearest 5 lb). The row column converts into the double-progression track load (first set of 8 at RPE 8, e1RM ÷ 1.333).

| Lift | 2024 best set | 2024 e1RM (lb) | 90% of it (lb) | Top set or track at 90% | Top set or track at 100% | Current track | Latest e1RM (lb) | % of 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Squat | 250 x 5 (113.4 kg, 15 Apr 2024) | 292 | 263 | ≈ 220 x 4 | ≈ 245 x 4 | 155 | 181 (155 x 5, 5 Nov, unrated) | **62%** |
| Bench | 105 x 8 (47.63 kg, 19 Aug 2024) | 133 | 120 | ≈ 100 x 4 | ≈ 110 x 4 | 110 | 126 (105 x 6 unrated); 133 with the RPE 8 reported on 27 Oct | 95-100% |
| Overhead press | 70 x 8 (31.75 kg, 12 Aug 2024) | 89 | 80 | ≈ 65 x 4 | ≈ 75 x 4 | 70 | 79-80 (70 x 4 on 4 Nov; 65 x 7 on 17 Oct; unrated) | 89-90% |
| Deadlift | 155 x 6 (70.31 kg, 4 Sep 2024) | 186 | 167 | ≈ 140 x 4 | ≈ 155 x 4 | 150 | 164 (145 x 4, 29 Oct, unrated) | 88% |
| Barbell row | 115 x 7 (52.16 kg, 15 Apr 2024) | 142 | 128 | track ≈ 95 | track ≈ 105 | 70 | 91 (65 x 12, 18 Oct) | **64%** |

Two things stand out. First, only the **squat** and the **row** are far from their 2024 level. Bench, press and deadlift are within about 10%, and their current tracks (110, 70, 150) already equal or pass the 90% line if lifted at RPE 8. Second, the deadlift's 2024 "best" came from sets of 6-8 that were never pushed (135 x 8 for weeks, then 155 x 6 x 3). It understates what the lifter could do then.

### Proposed rule

**Entry (per lift).** A main lift is in the regain phase if it came back after more than 8 weeks without training and its latest e1RM is below 90% of its pre-layoff best. Today that is the **squat** and the **row**. The press leaves at its next top set rated RPE 8 or lower at 70 lb (e1RM ≥ 84). Bench and deadlift are out.
- Basis: Barbell Medicine's "8 weeks or more: dial things way back"; losses accelerate after about 4 weeks (Bosquet 2013, per Nuckols); strength back to the old peak within 5-8 weeks in every retraining study (KQ2).

**How the top set is run during the regain phase.** Take the planned load for **4 to 6 reps, stopping at RPE 8**. Log the reps and the RPE. A big step needs a logged RPE: an unrated set gets the normal step only (the engine already treats unrated sets as RPE 8).
- Basis: ratings near RPE 8 are more accurate than ratings of 7 or lower (KQ4 inference). Reps at a fixed effort are the stronger readiness signal in this repo (`research/coaching-check-ins.md` KQ1). Barbell Medicine keeps returning lifters at or below RPE 8.

**Step sizes at the H exposure.** Compute reps plus reps in reserve (reps + 10 − RPE) at the planned load.

| Top set at the planned load | Squat | Bench, press (if in regain) | Deadlift (all lifts in the first 12 weeks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reps + RIR ≥ 7 (for example 5 @ 8, 6 @ 8, or 4 @ 7) | **+10 lb** | +5 lb, every time (no second-success wait) | +10 lb only with a logged RPE, and **never at two H exposures in a row**; otherwise +5 |
| Reps + RIR = 6 (4 @ 8) | +5 lb | +5 lb, every time | +5 lb |
| 4 reps at RPE 8.5-9 | Hold. **Regain phase ends** | Hold. Regain phase ends | Hold (§5) |
| RPE 9.5-10, or 3 reps or fewer | Miss (§5). Regain phase ends | Miss. Regain phase ends | Miss (§5) |
| No RPE logged | Normal §5 step (+5 lb); no big step | Normal §5 (every second success) | Normal §5 (+5 lb) |

- **Squat cap: +10 lb per H exposure, however easy the set.** No +15 or +20 steps. Reasons: the tendon lag of 2-3 months (Kubo 2010, 2012); team-sport data that put risk above about 10% weekly load jumps, though that evidence is disputed (Gabbett 2016 vs Impellizzeri 2021); RP's ceiling of +10 a week with a warning against +25; and an unverified 2024 squat benchmark. At +10 a step, the squat goes 155 → 165 → 175 → 185 → 195 → 205 → 215 → 225: six or seven qualifying H exposures to reach the 90% line (263 lb e1RM is about 220 x 4 @ 8, or 215 x 5 @ 8). That is about 6-7 weeks in 3- to 6-day weeks, which matches the 5-8-week retraining windows. A +15 cap would save about 2 weeks.
- **Row (double progression).** If every set reaches 8 reps at RPE 7 or lower, or the first set reaches 11 or more reps, add **+10 lb** (two pins) instead of +5 and drop back to 6 reps. Otherwise run §5 as written. Two or three such jumps take the track from 70 to about 90-95 lb.
- **Back-offs and V exposures do not change.** Back-offs stay at −7% and the V exposure at 90% of the new track load. Do not add sets during the regain phase, and do not strip them. The load climbs; the volume stays as the template sets it (Israetel 2020 on volume jumps; Nuckols 2022 on not reconditioning tissues with load alone).

**Gaps between exposures during the regain phase.** For a lift in the regain phase, a gap of **8-14 days does not block the step** if the top set qualifies on the day. The 8-14-day hold is a conservative convention, and the short-gap trials show no measurable loss at 2-3 weeks (`templates/progression-tracks.md` §8; `research/flexible-frequency-programming.md` addendum). The RPE stop protects the day. Gaps of 15-21 days and more than 21 days follow §8 as written.

**Exit (per lift, whichever comes first).** After exit, the lift runs `templates/progression-tracks.md` §5 unchanged, including its +10 lb big step at RPE 7 or lower.
1. The best e1RM from a top set with a logged RPE reaches **90% of the 2024 best**: squat **263 lb** (about 220 x 4 @ 8); row **128 lb** (track about 95 lb); press **80 lb**.
2. The **first top set at RPE 8.5 or higher** at the planned load, or the first miss. This is Nuckols' "can't increase loads week-to-week anymore".
3. **12 weeks after the restart**: 9 January 2027 (restart 17 October 2026). The retraining studies regained old peaks in 5-8 weeks; 12 weeks adds a margin for the much longer layoff.

**Extra caution for the deadlift** (applies until 9 January 2027, whether or not the deadlift is "in regain"):
- At most one +10 lb step in any two consecutive H exposures, and only with a logged RPE of 7 or lower. This overrides §5's "keep taking the big step" for this lift.
- Work-up singles and V sets never count as evidence for a step.
- Any low-back pain during or after the deadlift: hold the load. If it persists past a few days, see a physical therapist or physician. This note is general training guidance, not medical advice.
- Basis: the lower back is among the most injured sites in lifting (Aasa 2017; Keogh & Winwood 2017); posterior-chain tissues had about 18 months without heavy loading while running kept the lower-leg tendons partly loaded (KQ3 inference); the deadlift carries the heaviest loads in the program; and its 2024 benchmark is soft, so "regaining" is the wrong frame for it. It behaves more like a novice lift, and Nuckols' advice for a year-plus layoff (novice linear progression at 5-10 lb a week for lower-body lifts) covers it.

**3-day vs 5-6-day weeks.**
- **Step sizes do not change with day count.** Every 3- to 6-day week is one pass with one H exposure per lift (`guide/03-flexible-scheduling.md` §1.2). So the regain phase advances once a week in both. Six days do not speed it up.
- **3-day weeks (merged sessions).** Only the first heavy lift of a merged session is fresh: squat in Full A, deadlift in Full B. Bench and row (Full A) and press and pulldown (Full B) come second. Fatigue raises their RPE, which makes a false big step less likely, not more. No change needed. Per-session load is higher, so keep the RPE 8 stop strict.
- **5-6-day weeks.** Every H top set is fresh. The squat's H (L1) and its V (L3) are 4 sessions apart, as in a 3-day week. No change needed.
- **2-day weeks** (common lately). A 2-day week is Full A and Full B, main lifts only (`guide/03-flexible-scheduling.md` §4.6), and the next week starts with Full C. Over a run of 2-day weeks a pass takes about 1.5 weeks, so a lift's H exposures land every 7-14 days, about 10 on average. The regain phase stretches in calendar time. This is why the 8-14-day gap waiver matters. The 12-week cap may arrive before the squat reaches 263 lb. If it does, the normal §5 rules still allow +10 at RPE 7 or lower.
- **A jump from 2-day weeks to 6-day weeks** is the largest week-to-week load change this program can produce (KQ3). In the first week back at 5-6 days after two or more 2-day weeks, take normal steps only (no big steps) for that week. This is a heuristic with weak support.

**How this differs from the current rules** (for whoever edits `templates/progression-tracks.md` and `tools/update_tracks.py`):

| Item | Now (§5, §8) | Proposed, regain phase only |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence for the big step | 4 reps at RPE ≤ 7 | Reps + RIR ≥ 7 at the planned load (credits rep overshoot; the 27 Oct bench amendment did this by hand) |
| Top set reps | Exactly 4 | 4-6, stop at RPE 8 |
| Unrated top set | Treated as RPE 8 | Same, and never earns a big step |
| Squat big step | +10 lb, repeatable | +10 lb, repeatable, hard cap |
| Bench and press | +5 every second success; big step +5 | +5 every success |
| Row | +5 when every set reaches 8 | +10 when every set reaches 8 at RPE ≤ 7, or first set ≥ 11 reps |
| Deadlift | +10 at RPE ≤ 7, repeatable | +10 at logged RPE ≤ 7, not on consecutive H exposures, until 9 Jan 2027 |
| Gap 8-14 days | Hold | Step allowed if the day's set qualifies |
| End | — | 90% of 2024 e1RM, first RPE ≥ 8.5 or miss, or 9 Jan 2027 |

### Gaps and uncertainties in the rule

- Every threshold depends on 2024 e1RMs from unrated sets. Squat depth and row strictness in 2024 are unknown.
- The +10 lb squat cap is a judgment. It balances the speed the retraining studies allow against a tendon mechanism that has no injury data in lifters.
- The 90% exit line is arbitrary within about 85-95%. Below it, regain is clearly incomplete. Above it, the normal rules already move the lift at 5-10 lb a week.
- Unlogged RPE has been the norm since 29 October (`data/track-log.md`). Without RPE the rule cannot tell a big step from a normal one, and it falls back to +5.
