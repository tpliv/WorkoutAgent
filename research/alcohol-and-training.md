# Alcohol and training: protein synthesis, recovery, sleep, hormones, and body composition

Last updated: 2026-11-06

Scope: what alcohol does to a lifter who also runs and drinks at weekends (amount not yet recorded). Five questions: acute alcohol after training and muscle protein synthesis (KQ1); recovery of strength and power, next-day performance, sleep and hormones (KQ2); chronic moderate drinking and training adaptations (KQ3); alcohol in an energy deficit (KQ4); applied rules for this lifter (KQ5). Method: a PubMed search for each question, then the abstract through NCBI E-utilities; full text where it was open (PMC, Europe PMC) or where a copy of the published PDF could be fetched. Each item says what was read. No practitioner article on alcohol from Stronger By Science, Renaissance Periodization, Barbell Medicine or MASS could be found and checked in this round, so the [Practitioner] tag does not appear; see Gaps at the end. Tags: [Evidence] peer-reviewed paper or official position stand; [Convention] definitions and rules of thumb. "Foundational" marks pre-2016 work. S = strength or power outcome; H = hypertrophy or lean-mass outcome. This note does not change any other note's conclusions; alcohol was not covered anywhere in the repo before.

## KQ1. Acute alcohol after resistance or concurrent exercise, and muscle protein synthesis

### Takeaway

Only one human study has measured muscle protein synthesis (MPS) after training with alcohol. A very large dose (1.5 g/kg; about 9 US drinks for this lifter), drunk over 3 h starting 1 h after exercise, lowered myofibrillar MPS by 24% when taken with protein and by 37% when taken with carbohydrate, both compared with protein alone. About 5 US drinks blunted anabolic signalling for about 3 h in men, not in women. No human study has measured MPS after 1-3 drinks. No newer human MPS study was found up to November 2026. All of this is acute mechanism (H), not measured hypertrophy.

### Cited findings

- **[Evidence] Parr et al. 2014**, *PLoS One* 9(2):e88384, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0088384, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3922864/. Full text read.
  - 8 physically active men (21.4 ± 4.8 y, 79.3 ± 11.9 kg), randomised crossover, three trials.
  - Exercise: leg extension 8 × 5 at 80% 1RM, then 30 min cycling at 63% of peak power and 10 × 30 s at 110%.
  - Drinks: 25 g whey immediately and 4 h after exercise (PRO); alcohol 1.5 g/kg with the same protein (ALC-PRO); or alcohol with 25 g maltodextrin (ALC-CHO). Everyone had a 1.5 g/kg carbohydrate meal at 2 h.
  - Alcohol started 1 h after exercise: 6 equal drinks (about 60 mL vodka with 240 mL orange juice), one every 30 min for 3 h. Peak blood alcohol about 0.056-0.059 g/100 mL at 4 h.
  - Myofibrillar FSR (%/h): rest 0.025; ALC-CHO 0.032; ALC-PRO 0.039; PRO 0.052. Versus PRO: −24% (ALC-PRO) and −37% (ALC-CHO), both P < 0.05. MPS still rose above rest in every condition (about 29-109%).
  - No alcohol-free carbohydrate arm. So the 37% mixes the alcohol effect with the missing protein. One dose only; biopsies at 2 and 8 h.
- **[Evidence] Duplanty et al. 2017**, *J Strength Cond Res* 31(1):54-61, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27135475/. Abstract read; dose and time course from the Levitt 2022 review (secondary).
  - 10 resistance-trained men and 9 women; 6 sets of Smith-machine squats; alcohol or placebo afterwards.
  - Dose 1.09 g/kg FFM over 10 min; peak blood alcohol about 0.11 g/dL (Levitt 2022).
  - Men: mTOR and S6K1 phosphorylation lower with alcohol at +3 h, no longer different at +5 h (Levitt 2022). Women: no alcohol effect. 4E-BP1: no effect in either sex.
  - Signalling only; MPS was not measured.
- **[Evidence] Levitt, Luk & Vingren 2022**, *Biomolecules* 13(1):2, narrative review, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9855961/. Abstract and full-text passages read. Acute alcohol impairs and resistance exercise activates mTORC1 signalling. Anabolic stimuli such as feeding "may partially rescue" the alcohol-mediated inhibition. Effects vary with sex, feeding, training status and dose.
- **[Evidence] Lakićević 2019**, *J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 4(3):41, systematic review of 12 studies, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33467356/. Abstract read. After resistance exercise, alcohol lowered testosterone, plasma amino acids and MPS, and raised cortisol. Force, power, muscular endurance, soreness and RPE were "unmodified". Single author; no pooled analysis.
- **[Evidence, animal] Steiner & Lang 2015**, *Physiol Rep* 3, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25780086/. Abstract read. Mice fed about 20 g/kg/day alcohol for 14 days. Synergist overload raised plantaris mass by 90% and protein synthesis by 125% in both the alcohol and the control groups. Moderate chronic alcohol did not block overload growth in mice. The dose and the model do not transfer to people.

### Inferences

- The measured MPS penalty comes from about 9 drinks started within an hour of training. It says little about 2-3 drinks taken several hours after a session.
- Protein taken with the alcohol cut the penalty from 37% to 24% (Parr 2014). If drinking follows a session, the post-training protein meal comes first.
- 3-day weeks: four non-lifting days, so a drinking night can almost always fall on a day with no session. 5-6-day weeks: most evenings follow a session, so the dose matters more than the choice of night.

### Gaps

- No human MPS data below 1.5 g/kg, or with drinking starting more than about 1 h after training.
- No study in trained lifters doing a normal hypertrophy session followed by a normal mixed meal.
- No study links an acute MPS drop after alcohol to long-term hypertrophy.
- Smiles et al. 2016 (*Am J Physiol Endocrinol Metab* 311:E836-E849; protein with alcohol after exercise and markers of apoptosis and autophagy) was found by title only and not read.

## KQ2. Recovery of strength and power, next-day performance, sleep, and hormones

### Takeaway

After a novel, very damaging eccentric bout, 1 g/kg (about 6 US drinks) made the loss of force at 36-60 h much larger, while 0.5 g/kg (about 3) did not. In trained people doing familiar sessions, about 4-8 drinks did not slow recovery of force or power. The morning after about 5 drinks, time to exhaustion in severe-intensity cycling fell 11%, while strength and jump tests did not change. Sleep: REM sleep falls even at ≤0.5 g/kg and gets worse with dose. Testosterone: low-to-moderate acute doses did not lower it (one study showed a rise); large doses raised cortisol, and chronic intake lowered testosterone slightly.

Effects depend on timing, the recovery time needed, injury status and dose. "A dose of approximately 0.5 g/kg body weight is unlikely to impact most aspects of recovery."

**Next-day performance (hangover)**

- **[Evidence] Shaw et al. 2022**, *Int J Sports Physiol Perform* 17(1):44-49, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34225252/. Abstract read. 12 recreationally active people; 1.09 g/kg FFM in the evening (about 5 US drinks for this lifter) vs water. Next morning: constant-power cycling to exhaustion 181 vs 203 s (−11%, d = 0.61, P = 0.03). Vertical jump, isometric mid-thigh pull and biceps curls did not differ.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Prentice, Stannard & Barnes 2015**, *J Sci Med Sport* 18(3):268-271, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24820258/. Abstract read. 19 male club rugby players, drinking in a real setting: 6 to more than 20 "standard drinks" (drink size not given in the abstract), with 1-3 h of sleep. Next morning: countermovement jump −1.8 ± 1.5 cm. Maximal lower-body strength and repeated sprints unchanged. Full recovery by 2 days. Mean AUDIT 18.2 (hazardous drinking).
- **[Evidence, foundational] Prentice et al. 2014**, *J Sci Med Sport* 17(2):244-248, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23684981/. Abstract read. 26 rugby players after a match, split by team: usual post-game behaviour (large alcohol intake, less sleep) vs controlled behaviour. No performance difference in the following days. Prospective cohort, not randomised.

**Sleep**

- **[Evidence] Gardiner et al. 2025**, *Sleep Med Rev* 80:102030, systematic review and meta-analysis of 27 studies, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39631226/. Abstract read.
  - REM onset delayed and REM duration reduced.
  - Dose-response: REM disruption already at ≤0.50 g/kg ("approximately two standard drinks"; about 3 US drinks for this lifter), worse with more.
  - Shorter time to fall asleep and to reach deep (N3) sleep only at ≥0.85 g/kg (about 5 US drinks).
  - Total sleep time, sleep efficiency and wake after sleep onset: too uncertain to judge.
- **[Evidence] Pietilä et al. 2018**, *JMIR Ment Health* 5(1):e23, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29549064/. Abstract read.
  - Observational; 4,098 Finnish employees; each person's days with vs without alcohol; first 3 h of sleep.
  - An HRV-derived "recovery state" fell by 9.3, 24.0 and 39.2 percentage units after ≤0.25, 0.25-0.75 and >0.75 g/kg (about ≤1.5, 1.5-4.4 and >4.4 US drinks here).
  - Regular physical activity did not protect. Effects were stronger in younger people.
  - Two authors were employed by Firstbeat, which makes HRV devices.
- Repo link: `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` (KQ3 and its addendum) records that one night without sleep lowered MPS 18% (Lamon 2021) and 5 nights of 4 h lowered myofibrillar protein synthesis (Saner 2020). Alcohol was not tested in those studies.

**Hormones**

- **[Evidence, foundational] Vingren et al. 2013**, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 45(9):1825-1832, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23470309/. Abstract read. 8 resistance-trained men (25.3 y, 87.7 kg). Heavy resistance exercise, then 1.09 g/kg lean mass of ethanol (peak 0.088 g/dL at 60-90 min) or none. At 140-300 min, total and free testosterone were **higher** with ethanol: total 22.5 vs 13.9 nmol/L; free 40.5 vs 22.7 pmol/L. Cortisol, SHBG and estradiol did not differ.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Haugvad et al. 2014** (table above): the high dose (1.4 g/kg in men) raised cortisol and lowered the free testosterone/cortisol ratio.
- **[Evidence] Smith, Lopresti & Fairchild 2023**, *Expert Rev Endocrinol Metab* 18(2):155-166, review, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36880700/. Abstract read. In men, acute low-to-moderate alcohol raises testosterone (through liver enzyme activity); a large volume lowers it; chronic excess impairs production (HPA-axis activity, inflammation, oxidative stress). The abstract gives no dose thresholds.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Sierksma et al. 2004**, *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 28(5):780-785, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15166654/. Abstract read. Diet-controlled crossover; 10 middle-aged men drank 40 g/day of alcohol as beer (about 2.9 US drinks a day) for 3 weeks. Plasma testosterone fell 6.8% (95% CI −1.0 to −12.5).
- **[Evidence, low quality] Moosazadeh et al. 2024**, *Int J Prev Med* 15:75, meta-analysis of 17 observational studies, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39867254/. Abstract read. Testosterone, drinkers vs non-drinkers: SMD 0.03 (95% CI −0.11 to 0.16), no difference. Low (0.19) and moderate (0.22) intake groups were slightly higher. The authors still call alcohol use disorder harmful to the axis. The search ran only from June 2020 to June 2022.

### Inferences

- The finding that alcohol enlarges strength loss comes from a novel, very damaging bout (300 maximal eccentric contractions) in healthy men who were not described as trained. In trained people doing familiar sessions, about 4-8 drinks did not slow recovery of force or power (Haugvad 2014, Levitt 2020). The risk is highest after the most damaging sessions: the first session after a long gap, a new exercise, a long or downhill run, and the 10-mile race.
- Sleep is the most consistent cost, and it starts at low doses. Even about 3 drinks reduce REM sleep.
- For a session the next morning, the clearest measured cost is in high-intensity endurance (−11% at about 5 drinks). Maximal strength the next morning held up in every study found.
- Testosterone: the acute data do not support the idea that one night of drinking crashes testosterone. About 3 drinks a day for 3 weeks lowered it by about 7% in middle-aged men. Whether a change that size matters for training is unknown.
- 3-day weeks: a drinking night can sit 48 h or more before the next session. 5-6-day weeks: the next session is often 12-24 h away, so choose the night before a rest day or an upper-body day, and keep the dose low.

### Gaps

- No dose between 0.5 and 1.0 g/kg was tested in the eccentric-damage model, so the threshold for this lifter (somewhere between 3 and 6 drinks) is unknown.
- No study of alcohol after a long run or race on running performance the next day.
- No study of two drinking nights in a row on the next week's training.
- Barnes 2014 full text (paywalled) was not read, so any dose thresholds it gives for hormones were not checked.

## KQ3. Chronic moderate drinking and lean mass, strength and training adaptations

### Takeaway

Two reports from one trial in young adults found that about 2 US drinks a day, 5 days a week, for 10 weeks did not reduce the gains from HIIT in VO2max, grip strength, fat loss or lean mass. No trial has tested drinking alongside resistance training. Large observational data link high intakes, far above 2-3 drinks per occasion, with lower muscle mass in middle-aged and older adults.

### Cited findings

- **[Evidence] Molina-Hidalgo et al. 2019** (body composition), *Nutrients* 11(4):909, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31018614/, and **2020** (fitness), *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 17:29, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32460793/. The BEER-HIIT study. Abstracts read.
  - 72-73 young adults (24 ± 6 y; 35 women). 10 weeks of HIIT, 2 days a week. Groups: beer (5.4%), the same ethanol in sparkling water, alcohol-free beer, sparkling water, and a non-training control.
  - Men drank 330 mL at lunch and 330 mL at dinner, Monday to Friday: about 28 g/day, 2.0 US drinks a day, 10 a week. Women drank 330 mL at dinner.
  - H: all training groups lost fat mass and gained lean mass (DXA), unaffected by beer or ethanol.
  - S and fitness: VO2max and test duration improved in all training groups; grip strength improved "clinically"; vertical jumps did not change in any group. Beer or ethanol changed none of these.
  - Limits: participants chose alcohol or no alcohol (only the type was randomised); HIIT, not resistance training; groups of about 13-16. One author is a member of the Scientific Advisory Board of the Spanish Beer and Health Information Center.
- **[Evidence] Skinner et al. 2023**, *Calcif Tissue Int* 113(2):143-156, UK Biobank, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37227465/. Abstract read. 196,561 middle-aged and older adults. Cross-sectionally, muscle-mass measures peaked at medium intake and fell steeply above it. Modelled difference from 0 to 160 g/day: 3.6-6.1% for FFM%. Grip strength rose with alcohol intake. In the 4-year longitudinal subset (n = 12,298) there was no association.
- **[Evidence] Sokolowski et al. 2026**, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* (online October 2026), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42868342/. Abstract read. 48 young women in 4 groups of 12. Resistance-trained binge drinkers vs resistance-trained low drinkers: no differences in bone density, muscle quality or physical performance. Fasting glucose was higher in the binge group (103.2 vs 88.9 mg/dL). Cross-sectional; women only.
- **[Evidence] Smith 2023 and Sierksma 2004** (KQ2): chronic excess lowers testosterone; 40 g/day for 3 weeks lowered it 6.8%.

### Inferences

- For this lifter, 2-3 drinks per occasion at weekends is unlikely to limit hypertrophy or strength gains by an amount any study could detect. The likelier routes to a cost are the acute ones: sleep, the next session, and calories (KQ4).
- High weekly intakes are a health question before they are a training question.

### Gaps

- No randomised trial of alcohol with resistance training on hypertrophy or strength.
- No study of a weekend-only pattern versus the same weekly total spread across the week.

## KQ4. Alcohol in an energy deficit

### Takeaway

Alcohol carries about 7 kcal/g. People do not eat less food to make room for it, and on average they eat a little more. While alcohol is being metabolised, the body burns much less fat. In a deficit the energy still counts the same way: the cost is the extra calories, not a separate fat-storing effect. After endurance exercise, alcohol slows glycogen refilling mainly when it replaces carbohydrate.

### Cited findings

- **[Evidence, position stand] Thomas, Erdman & Burke 2016**, Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada and ACSM joint position, *J Acad Nutr Diet* 116(3):501-528, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26920240/ (same text in *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 48(3):543-568, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891166/). Full text read from a copy of the published PDF; the publisher pages returned HTTP 402.
  - "Besides the calorie load of alcohol (7 kcal/g), alcohol suppresses lipid oxidation, increases unplanned food consumption, and may compromise the achievement of body composition goals."
  - Warns against "significant amounts of alcohol during the pre-exercise period and during training".
  - After exercise, alcohol "may interfere with recovery by impairing glycogen storage, slowing rates of rehydration ... and impairing the MPS desired for adaptation and repair".
- **[Convention] 7.1 vs 7 kcal/g.** 7.1 kcal/g is the heat of combustion of ethanol (about 1,367 kJ/mol ÷ 46.07 g/mol = 29.7 kJ/g = 7.09 kcal/g; arithmetic from the standard thermochemical value, not from a nutrition paper). The position stand and labels use 7. The 1.5% difference does not matter in practice.
- **[Evidence] Kwok et al. 2019**, *Br J Nutr* 121(5):481-495, systematic review and meta-analysis, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30630543/. Abstract read.
  - 22 crossover or randomised studies, 701 participants aged 18-37.
  - No compensation: food intake did not fall to make room for alcohol energy.
  - Meta-analysis of 12 studies, alcohol vs a non-alcoholic comparator: food energy +343 kJ (about 82 kcal; 95% CI about 38-125 kcal); total energy +1,072 kJ (about 256 kcal; 95% CI about 196-316 kcal).
  - Substantial heterogeneity in some outcomes, and signs of small-study effects.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Suter, Schutz & Jéquier 1992**, *N Engl J Med* 326(15):983-987, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1545851/. Abstract read. 8 men in a respiration chamber. Adding ethanol at 25% of energy needs (96 ± 4 g/day; about 6.9 US drinks) cut 24-h fat oxidation by 49 g/day (36%). Swapping the same energy of fat and carbohydrate for ethanol cut it 31%. 24-h energy expenditure rose 7% and 4%. Carbohydrate and protein oxidation did not change.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Siler, Neese & Hellerstein 1999**, *Am J Clin Nutr* 70(5):928-936, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10539756/. Abstract read. 8 healthy men; 24 g of alcohol (about 1.7 US drinks). Release of fatty acids from fat tissue fell 53%, and whole-body fat oxidation fell 73%. New fat made from the alcohol (de novo lipogenesis) was less than 5% of the dose. About 77% of the alcohol became acetate, which the body burned in place of fat.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Traversy & Chaput 2015**, *Curr Obes Rep* 4(1):122-130, review, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4338356/. Abstract read. In prospective studies, light-to-moderate intake is not associated with gains in adiposity; heavy drinking more consistently is.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Burke et al. 2003**, *J Appl Physiol* 95(3):983-990, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12740311/. Abstract read. Well-trained cyclists after glycogen-depleting exercise.
  - Alcohol (1.5 g/kg) **replacing** carbohydrate energy: glycogen lower at 8 h (24.4 vs 44.6 mmol/kg wet weight) and at 24 h (68 vs 82).
  - Alcohol **added** to a full carbohydrate diet: a trend at 8 h (36.2, P = 0.1); no difference at 24 h (85).
  - Conclusion: the main effect is indirect, by displacing carbohydrate.

### Inferences

- For this lifter, 3 US drinks are about 300 kcal from alcohol alone, and 6 are about 600. The planned deficit averages about 300-400 kcal a day, or 2,100-2,800 kcal a week (`data/body-composition.md` §5). Six drinks over a weekend, plus Kwok's average extra food (about 80 kcal per occasion, two occasions), come to about 760 kcal before mixers. That is roughly a quarter to a third of the weekly deficit.
- The drop in fat oxidation means fat from food eaten alongside alcohol is more likely to be stored that evening. Over the week, fat balance still follows total energy, so counting alcohol calories is enough. No extra penalty factor is needed.
- After a long run or race, alcohol that replaces the carbohydrate meal slows glycogen refilling (Burke 2003). The same alcohol after a full carbohydrate meal made no difference at 24 h.

### Gaps

- No study of alcohol intake in lean lifters during a planned deficit.
- Kwok's meta-analysis covers single laboratory sessions. Late-night meals and appetite the day after drinking were not measured.

## KQ5. Applied: dose, timing and logging rules for this lifter (inference)

Everything in this section is inference from KQ1-KQ4. No trial tested these rules.

### Dose

| Level | US drinks (14 g) | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Default ceiling per occasion | ≤3 (≤0.5 g/kg, ≤41 g) | No measured effect on recovery of force after damaging exercise (Barnes 2011); the dose a review calls "unlikely to impact most aspects of recovery" (Barnes 2014). REM sleep is still reduced at this dose (Gardiner 2025) |
| Avoid within 48 h after a hard or novel session, and the night before a race or key session | ≥6 (≥1 g/kg) | Much larger force loss after damaging exercise (Barnes 2010a, 2010b, 2012); −11% severe-intensity endurance the next morning at about 5 (Shaw 2022); REM and HRV recovery worse with dose |
| Health thresholds | Binge: 5 or more in about 2 h (blood alcohol 0.08%). "High-intensity drinking": 10 or more on one occasion | [Convention] NIAAA binge-drinking fact sheet, https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/publications/brochures-and-fact-sheets/binge-drinking (read) |

### Timing

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| After lifting | Eat the post-training protein meal first: at least about 0.3 g/kg, so 25-40 g (the AND/DC/ACSM stand gives 0.25-0.3 g/kg after exercise). Start drinking several hours after the session, not within the first hour as in Parr 2014 |
| Night before a lifting session | ≤3 drinks. After more, make the next session the planned upper-body or lighter day. Next-morning maximal strength held up in the studies; sleep and high-intensity work did not |
| Night before a long run or race | None. For the 10-mile race on Sun 8 Nov 2026: no alcohol on Sat 7 Nov |
| After a race or long run | Fluids, carbohydrate and protein first; then ≤3 drinks. A race on a low running base is the most damaging leg session of the month, which is the Barnes model |
| 3-day weeks | Put drinking nights before rest days. After more than 3 drinks, leave 48 h before the next lower-body session |
| 5-6-day weeks | Few rest days. Hold to ≤3 per occasion. Never drink heavily on the evening of the hardest lower-body session (the Barnes effect needs fresh muscle damage) |

### Logging

- MyFitnessPal: log each drink as it is drunk (the specific beverage, size and count). It counts toward the daily and weekly calorie totals, as `data/body-composition.md` §5 already says.
- Weekly check-in: add one item, "drinks this week: number, and which nights". A count is more useful than a 1-5 score.
- Hevy session notes: after a drinking night, note "drinks last night: n". Later check-ins can then compare RPE at known loads on those days, which is the repo's strongest readiness signal (`research/coaching-check-ins.md`).

### What the evidence does not show

- That 2-3 drinks at weekends limit muscle growth or strength gains.
- Any human MPS effect below about 9 drinks, or with drinking that starts hours after training.
- Any effect of about 5 drinks on next-morning maximal strength.
- Where the recovery threshold lies between 3 and 6 drinks.
- Any difference between beer and spirits beyond the alcohol and the calories.

### When to see a professional

- See a doctor if drinking often goes past 5 drinks on one occasion, if cutting down is hard, or if drinking affects work, sleep or mood. A doctor can screen for alcohol-use problems; questionnaires such as the AUDIT are screening tools, not diagnoses.
- Do not mix alcohol with sleep medication or other sedatives. Check any regular medication with a pharmacist.
- This note is general training guidance, not medical advice.

## Conflicts

- **Recovery of force: Barnes vs Haugvad and Levitt.** Barnes found that 1 g/kg made the force loss after 300 maximal eccentric contractions much larger. Haugvad (0.7 and 1.4 g/kg) and Levitt (1.09 g/kg FFM after a non-novel eccentric bout) found no effect in trained people. In Haugvad, MVC was even recovered at 24 h after alcohol but not in the control trial. Lakićević's review concluded that force and power were "unmodified". Both sides are kept. The likely difference is how damaging and how novel the bout was.
- **Testosterone up or down.** Vingren 2013: higher after 1.09 g/kg lean mass. Haugvad 2014: lower free testosterone/cortisol ratio after 1.4 g/kg. Smith 2023: low-moderate raises it, large amounts lower it. Sierksma 2004: 40 g/day for 3 weeks lowered it 6.8%. Moosazadeh 2024: no overall difference in observational data. Read as dose- and time-dependent.
- **Lean mass: BEER-HIIT vs UK Biobank.** About 2 drinks a day did not blunt lean-mass gains with HIIT; UK Biobank links high intakes with lower muscle mass. Not a true conflict: different doses, ages and designs.
- **"Alcohol helps sleep".** High doses shorten the time to fall asleep (Gardiner 2025), but REM sleep falls at every dose, and HRV-based recovery falls with dose (Pietilä 2018). Both kept; the net effect on recovery looks negative.
- **"Alcohol cuts MPS by 37%".** Often quoted this way. The 37% compares alcohol plus carbohydrate with protein alone. With protein matched, the difference was 24% (Parr 2014).

## Gaps across the note

- The US Dietary Guidelines' drink limits were not checked against the current edition and are not used here.
