# Sauna and heat exposure: muscle, recovery, heat acclimation for running, hormones, health, and safety

Last updated: 2026-11-06

Scope: what a gym sauna can and cannot do for this lifter: a 25-year-old man, 181 lb (82 kg), about 16.5% body fat (InBody), in a 300-400 kcal/day recomposition deficit. He lifts 3-6 days a week, runs under 10 miles a week, races 10 miles on Sun 8 Nov 2026, drinks on weekends, and takes creatine. The note does not record whether he has sauna access or uses one. Seven questions: muscle growth and strength (KQ1), recovery and sleep (KQ2), endurance and heat acclimation (KQ3), hormones (KQ4), health, mortality and fertility (KQ5), safety and practicalities (KQ6), and an applied protocol (KQ7, inference). Method: broad web search, then the primary source for each claim. The PubMed abstract was read for every cited paper, through NCBI E-utilities. Full texts were read where open: PMC, the JYX repository, and the SportRxiv preprint. Each citation says which was read. Cold-water immersion is not redone here; see `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` KQ6 and its 2026-10-21 addendum (Piñero 2024). Alcohol itself, race-week lifting and InBody methods are in the parallel notes `research/alcohol-and-training.md`, `research/running-and-lifting.md` and `research/body-composition-measurement.md`. This note covers only how the sauna interacts with them.

Tags: **[Evidence]** peer-reviewed; **[Evidence, foundational]** peer-reviewed and pre-2016; **[Practitioner]** coaches, evidence-review sites and popular science podcasts; **[Convention]** manufacturer or customary guidance. S = strength, H = hypertrophy, E = endurance, R = recovery, Hl = health outcome. RCT = randomised controlled trial.

## Heat types used in the studies

The physiology differs by method, so results do not transfer one-to-one between them. Water conducts heat far better than air. A 40 °C bath can load the body about as much as an 80 °C sauna.

| Method | Typical conditions in the cited studies | Core-temperature load | Studies below |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional Finnish (dry) sauna | 70-105 °C, 5-25% relative humidity (RH); water thrown on stones raises humidity briefly | High | Laukkanen 2015 (mean 78.9 °C, 14.2 min), Scoon 2007, Stanley 2015, Kirby 2021, Rissanen 2020, Bartolomé 2025, Garolla 2013, Lee 2022 |
| Infrared sauna | 35-60 °C air, 20-35% RH; radiant heat warms the body directly | Low to moderate | Mero 2015, Ahokas 2023, Ahokas 2025 |
| Steam room / humid sauna | 40-50 °C, near 100% RH | Moderate to high (sweat cannot evaporate) | None directly; Kukkonen-Harjula 1989 "80 °C becoming humid" was the most straining condition |
| Hot-water immersion (HWI) | 38-46 °C, to sternum or neck, 10-60 min | High per minute | Zurawlew 2016/2019, Brunt 2016, Fuchs 2020, 3 of 6 trials in Hopper 2026 |
| Local heat (pads, garments, diathermy) | Muscle temperature +4-5 °C for 20 min to 8 h | Local only | Goto 2011, Kim 2020, Labidi 2021, Hafen 2019, Stadnyk 2018 |

## Key numbers at a glance

| Question | Best current answer | Evidence type |
|---|---|---|
| Does heat after lifting add muscle? | Maybe a little: SMD 0.12 (95% CrI -0.09 to 0.31), 6 small trials, none of traditional sauna in trained lifters | Bayesian meta-analysis, fair-to-poor trials |
| Does it hurt muscle growth like a cold plunge? | No signal of harm. Cold: cSMD -0.22 (Piñero 2024) | RCT meta-analyses |
| Does it change post-lift muscle protein synthesis? | No: 0.050 vs 0.049 %/h over 5 h (46 °C leg immersion) | One tracer RCT, n = 12 |

| Endurance (temperate) | Mixed. +0.27 L/min VO2max in runners after 3 wk, 3x/wk, ~28 min at 101-108 °C (non-randomised); no VO2peak effect in 2 small randomised sauna trials with cycling training | Small trials |
| Growth hormone | Up to 16-fold, but only with 2 h/day at 80 °C for 7 days (n = 10). Acute hormone rises do not predict growth | Old small study + RCTs on hormones |

## KQ1. Heat and muscle: hypertrophy and strength

### Takeaway

Passive local heat can add a little strength, and sometimes size, in untrained or immobilised muscle. In active people and lifters, the signal is small and uncertain. The only meta-analysis of heat after resistance training finds a small, uncertain hypertrophy benefit (SMD 0.12) and no strength benefit. No trial has tested a traditional Finnish sauna after lifting in trained lifters with muscle imaging. Heat after lifting does not raise muscle protein synthesis. There is no sign that it blunts growth the way cold-water immersion may.

### Cited findings

**Passive heat without training**

- **[Evidence, foundational] Goto et al. 2011**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 111(1):17-27, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20803152/. Abstract read. 8 healthy men. A heat-and-steam sheet on one quadriceps, 8 h/day, 4 days/week, for 10 weeks, with **no exercise training**. Isometric knee extension force rose about 5.8%. Vastus lateralis cross-sectional area (CSA) rose about 2.7%, rectus femoris about 6.1%, and fibre CSA 8.3% (all P < 0.05). This is often cited as "heat plus low-load training". It is not: there was no training.
- **[Evidence] Kim et al. 2020**, *J Appl Physiol* 128(3):483-492, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31971474/. Abstract read. 12 young adults (23.6 y). One thigh wore a garment perfused with ~52 °C water, 90 min/day, 5 days/week, for 8 weeks; the other thigh was the control. Peak isokinetic torque gain was larger in the heated thigh (week 8: +7.8 ± 10.2 vs +1.8 ± 9.7 Nm; P = 0.007). Small heat shock proteins HSPB1 and HSPB5 rose. **Fibre CSA and mitochondrial content did not differ** between thighs.
- **[Evidence] Labidi et al. 2021**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 121(2):573-582, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33159573/. Abstract read. 15 active adults (8 men; 35 y; 87 min of training a week). Heat pads on one calf, 8 h/day, 5 days/week, for 6 weeks; muscle temperature +4.6 °C. **No change** in gastrocnemius CSA (MRI), pennation, twitch, isometric or isokinetic torque.
- **[Evidence] Hafen et al. 2019**, *J Appl Physiol* 127(1):47-57, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31046520/. Abstract read. 23 adults (12 men) with 10 days of leg immobilisation. Pulsed shortwave diathermy for 2 h/day raised muscle temperature 4.2 °C. HSP70 rose 25% and HSP90 20%. Whole-muscle CSA fell -4.5% with heat vs -7.6% with sham (P = 0.037). Fibre CSA fell -5.8% vs -10.8%. Mitochondrial respiration was preserved. This is local deep heating, not sauna.
- **[Evidence] Rodrigues et al. 2020**, *J Therm Biol* 93:102684, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33077110/. Abstract read. A "preliminary" review: 10 articles, 4 in the meta-analysis. The pooled muscle-mass gain was in **animals**. Human evidence was described only as "preliminary".

**Heat combined with resistance training**

- **[Evidence] Hopper, Coleman, Piñero, … Swinton & Schoenfeld 2026**, "Feeling the heat", Bayesian systematic review and meta-analysis of heat applied shortly after resistance training. Preprint: SportRxiv, posted 9 May 2026, https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/866. **Full text of the preprint read.** Published version: *Sport Sci Health*, online 20 Aug 2026, doi:10.1007/s11332-026-01903-6 (paywalled; only Crossref metadata read).
  - 6 trials. Two used sauna: Ahokas 2025 (infrared, women) and Bartolomé 2025 (100 °C, untrained men). Three used HWI: Horgan 2023, McGorm 2019 (a thesis) and Wilson 2023 (no journal listed). One used an electric pad: Stadnyk 2018. Lengths were 4-12 weeks; participants were young (mean 19-25 y). The three trials in resistance-trained people all used HWI. No trial trained to failure.
  - **H:** SMD 0.12 (95% CrI -0.09 to 0.31); posterior probability >0 = 89.7%; probability of at least a "small" effect 58.7%. All trials measured fat-free mass, five by DXA and one by BIA. The authors note that water shifts could bias these.
  - **S:** SMD -0.05 (95% CrI -0.28 to 0.19).
  - Quality: "fair-to-poor".
  - FoundMyFitness summarises the published version as "6 interventions, 125 young adults, five of six rated poor quality", and says heat "did not clearly improve muscle growth or strength" ([Practitioner] secondary summary; https://www.foundmyfitness.com/topics/sauna). That wording is consistent with the preprint's numbers. The published numbers were not checked.
- **[Evidence] Stadnyk et al. 2018**, *Temperature* 5(2):175-183, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30393753/. Abstract read. 10 untrained adults (5 women), contralateral-limb design. 30 sessions over 12 weeks of 4 × 8 knee extensions at 70% 1RM. One thigh was heated (muscle >38 °C) during and for 20 min after each session. Quadriceps lean mass rose +15 ± 7% (heated) vs +15 ± 6% (control), P = 0.94. 3RM rose +75% vs +71%.
- **[Evidence] Ahokas, Hanstock, Kyröläinen & Ihalainen 2025**, *Front Sports Act Living* 7:1462901, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40104529/. Abstract read. 40 female team-sport athletes, pair-matched into two groups of 20. The abstract does not say whether allocation was random; Hopper 2026 lists it as random assignment. 6 weeks of strength and power training, 2-3 sessions/week. **Infrared sauna, 10 min at 50 °C, after training 3x/week.** Both groups grew (DXA, vastus lateralis ultrasound), with **no group × time interaction for any hypertrophy measure**. Loaded jump height and peak power (CMJ with +15% load) improved more with sauna.
- **[Evidence] Bartolomé et al. 2025**, *Applied Sciences* 15(19):10762, doi:10.3390/app151910762. Abstract read via Crossref (the publisher page refused the fetch); design details from Hopper 2026 Table 1.
  - 30 young men, untrained per Hopper, randomised to sauna (n = 14) or control (n = 15). Same lower-body training in both: 2 exercises, 4 × 10 at 75% 1RM, 2 days/week, 4 weeks.
  - The sauna group also did **4 × 10 min at 100 ± 2 °C, 24% RH**, 2 days/week, immediately after training.
  - Only the sauna group gained body mass and "muscle mass" (BIA) and lost skinfold thickness. Both groups improved squat 1RM. Only the sauna group kept improving squat and leg-press 1RM over a 4-week detraining period.
  - Caveats: baseline body mass differed (median 76.3 vs 61.7 kg). The abstract reports within-group tests only. Muscle mass was by BIA. 40 min at 100 °C is far beyond normal use.
- **[Evidence] Nakamura et al. 2019**, *BMC Musculoskelet Disord* 20:603, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31830963/. Abstract read. RCT, 30 young men. A 20-min hot pack on the triceps **before** 3 × 8 at 30% 1RM, 6 weeks. 1RM and triceps thickness rose only in the heat group; the control group did not change at all. The load was very light and not taken to failure, so this does not apply to heavy training.

**Mechanism: protein synthesis, signalling, heat shock proteins**

- **[Evidence] Fuchs et al. 2020**, *J Appl Physiol* 128(4):1012-1022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32191599/. Abstract read. 12 young men lifted, then immersed one leg in 46 °C water and the other in 30 °C water for 20 min, then drank 20 g labelled milk protein. Muscle temperature was 37.5 vs 35.2 °C. Myofibrillar protein synthesis over 5 h did not differ: 0.050 ± 0.005 vs 0.049 ± 0.002 %/h (P = 0.815). Dietary amino-acid incorporation was also the same.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Kakigi et al. 2011**, *J Physiol Sci* 61(2):131-140, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21222186/. Abstract read. 8 young men, randomised crossover; microwave heating of the vastus lateralis before and during 4 × 6 maximal knee extensions. Phosphorylation of Akt, mTOR and ribosomal protein S6 rose at 1 h. This is signalling only, not growth.
- **[Evidence] Iguchi et al. 2012**, *J Athl Train* 47(2):184-190, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22488284/. Abstract read. 25 young adults sat 30 min in a 73 °C chamber. Rectal temperature rose 0.8 °C. Plasma HSP72 rose 48.7 ± 53.9% (n = 13). This is circulating HSP, not muscle.
- **[Evidence] McGorm, Roberts, Coombes & Peake 2018**, *Sports Med* 48(6):1311-1328, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29470824/ (narrative review; abstract read), and **Pryor et al. 2024**, *J Strength Cond Res* 38(7):1350-1357, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38775794/ (narrative review; abstract read). Both say the mechanistic case (mTOR, HSPs, calcium, blood flow) comes mostly from cell and rodent models. Human training trials are few.

### Inferences

- For a trained lifter, sauna is not a hypertrophy tool. The best estimate is a small benefit that may be zero, measured mostly by fat-free mass, which water can inflate. Nothing suggests it harms growth. That makes it neutral-to-slightly-positive, unlike post-lift cold-water immersion (`research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` KQ6).
- Using the sauna straight after a lifting session is acceptable for muscle. Fuchs 2020 shows no protein-synthesis penalty, and the training trials put heat within 0-15 min of lifting.
- Disuse is the one place where heat has a clear human signal (Hafen 2019), but that used 2 h/day of deep diathermy. Do not assume a 15-min whole-body sauna does the same during an injury layoff.
- 3-day vs 5-6-day weeks: no difference for muscle. The per-session question is the same. The weekly sauna count is limited by fatigue and hydration (KQ2, KQ6), not by any muscle effect.

### Gaps

- No trial of **traditional dry sauna after lifting in resistance-trained men** with direct muscle imaging (MRI or ultrasound) was found. Bartolomé 2025 (untrained, BIA) and Rissanen 2020 (acute) are the closest.
- The Bartolomé full text, between-group statistics and exact randomisation were not read.
- The published (*Sport Sci Health*) version of Hopper 2026 was not read; whether its numbers differ from the preprint is unknown.
- The often-quoted "Goto 2011 heat plus low-load training" does not match the PubMed record. The 2011 paper is heat alone. A separate Japanese-language Goto study of heat plus low-intensity exercise may exist; it was not found.

## KQ2. Recovery: soreness, strength the next day, perceived recovery, sleep, timing

### Takeaway

Low-temperature infrared sauna after lifting reduced soreness and helped jump recovery in one small crossover trial. It did not help maximal strength. A long, hot traditional sauna (30 min at 70 °C) is itself a fatiguing load: leg strength was still down 24 h later. A traditional sauna after a swim session made next-morning sprints slower. The 2025 systematic review calls the acute recovery evidence "uncertain". Sauna did not change sleep in the one lifting study that measured it. Warm baths 1-2 h before bed do shorten sleep onset.

### Cited findings

- **[Evidence] Ahokas, Ihalainen, Hanstock, Savolainen & Kyröläinen 2023**, *Biol Sport* 40(3):681-689, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37398966/. **Full text read (PMC10286597).**
  - Randomised crossover. 16 male basketball players (18.9 y, 83.4 kg).
  - Session: squat 3 × 3 at 90-95% 1RM, Nordic curls, leg press 3 × 3 at 90-95%, plus jumps.
  - Then either **20 min of infrared sauna at 43 ± 5 °C** or 20 min of passive rest.
  - **R:** at 14 h the drop in CMJ was smaller after sauna (P = 0.009, ES 0.76). Isometric leg press and 20-m sprint did not differ. The change in soreness was smaller (P = 0.032, ES 0.58), and perceived recovery was higher.
  - **Sleep:** diary sleep duration (P = 0.377) and quality (P = 0.632) did not differ. Nocturnal heart rate and HRV did not differ (n = 11 analysed).
- **[Evidence, foundational] Mero et al. 2015**, *SpringerPlus* 4:321, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26180741/. Abstract read; protocol details from the Ahokas 2025 review table.
  - 10 active men. Either a 60-min hypertrophy session (5 × 10RM leg press and bench press) or a 34-40-min maximal treadmill run, then 30 min of far-infrared sauna at 35-50 °C.
  - After lifting: no difference from no sauna in isometric bench, leg press or CMJ.
  - After running: CMJ was higher at 30 min (0.34 vs 0.32 m).
  - The "traditional" comparison sauna in this study was also 35-50 °C (60-70% RH), far cooler than a real Finnish sauna.
- **[Evidence] Rissanen, Häkkinen, Laukkanen, Kraemer & Häkkinen 2020**, *J Strength Cond Res* 34(2):313-322, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31490429/. **Accepted manuscript read** (JYX repository, https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/93916).
  - 27 recreationally active men who were used to saunas.
  - Sauna: 3 × 10 min at **70.2 °C, 18% RH**, with 1-min breaks (32 min total), starting 15 min after exercise.
  - Strength session: bench press then leg press, 4 × 10 at 75-80% and 85-95% 1RM, with final reps assisted.
  - **S:** every exercise-plus-sauna condition cut isometric leg press force by 9-15% and rate of force development by 20-26% right after the sauna.
  - After strength + sauna, leg press force, rate of force development and jump were still lower at 24 h, and bench force was still lower at 24 h.
  - **Sauna alone** lowered leg press and bench force, and leg press was still -4.1% at 24 h.
  - Authors: sauna at this temperature and duration "might not be recommended … too close before an intensive strength training session".
  - Limits: there was no exercise-only arm, so the sauna's share of the 24-h fatigue after lifting cannot be separated. The sauna-only condition was always done first, not randomised. Body-mass loss stayed under 0.9% because water was drunk freely.
- **[Evidence] Skorski et al. 2020**, *Int J Sports Physiol Perform* 15(7):934-940, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31869820/. Details from the Ahokas 2025 review table (secondary to the primary). Crossover, 20 young swimmers and triathletes (17.3 y) who were not used to saunas. After a 5,000-m swim session: traditional sauna 80-85 °C, 3 × 8 min, vs a massage-oil placebo. Next-morning 4 × 50 m time got 1.69% slower after sauna vs 0.66% faster after placebo (interaction P = 0.02). CMJ did not differ.
- **[Evidence] Ahokas, Hennessy, Hanstock, Kyröläinen & Ihalainen 2025**, *Sports Med Open* 11:106, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41032138/. **Full text read (PMC12488549).**
  - Systematic review: 14 randomised or crossover trials of whole-body post-exercise heat (≥36 °C), 194 participants.
  - Acute recovery (9 trials): 4 found no effect, 4 found a benefit and 1 found harm (Skorski).
  - Maximal isometric strength improved in only 1 of 5 trials: Vaile 2008, HWI repeated four times over 72 h after heavy eccentric work.
  - Quality was low to moderate, and no meta-analysis was possible.
  - Funding: Urheiluopistosäätiö and **The Finnish Sauna Society**.
- **[Evidence] Ahokas, Kyröläinen, Ihalainen & Hanstock 2025**, *Temperature* 12(3):281-295, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40926789/. Abstract read. Same 40 women as above. After the first 10-min, 50 °C infrared session, salivary cortisol was +5.1 ± 8.6 nmol/L the next morning (no rise in controls). By week 6 the response had adapted (-1.8 nmol/L). The first exposures are an extra stressor; regular use blunts that.
- **[Evidence] Wang et al. 2022**, *J Rehabil Med* 54:jrm00258, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34636405/. Abstract read. Network meta-analysis of 59 RCTs (1,367 people) on soreness (DOMS). A **hot pack** ranked first for pain relief within 24 h and within 48 h; a newer cryotherapy method ranked first after 48 h. Study quality was limited. This is local heat, not sauna.
- **[Evidence] Haghayegh et al. 2019**, *Sleep Med Rev* 46:124-135, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31102877/. Abstract read. 17 studies (13 pooled). A warm shower or bath at 40-42.5 °C was associated with better self-rated sleep quality and sleep efficiency. Taken 1-2 h before bed for as little as 10 min, it shortened sleep-onset latency. Water, not sauna.
- **[Evidence] Hussain, Greaves & Cohen 2019**, Global Sauna Survey, *Complement Ther Med* 44:223-234, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31126560/. Abstract read. Online survey, 482 self-selected regular bathers: 83.5% reported sleep benefits. This is self-report from enthusiasts, not evidence of an effect.

### Inferences

- Pick the heat type for the purpose. A **short, mild** session (infrared ~45-50 °C for 10-20 min, or a traditional sauna for 10-15 min) is the version tied to less soreness and better perceived recovery. A **long, hot** session (≥30 min at ≥70 °C) is a training stress of its own.
- Timing rule: **after** lifting, not before (KQ6, Hedley 2002). Avoid a long or hot sauna in the **~24 h before** the heaviest lower-body session or a race (Rissanen 2020, Skorski 2020).
- 5-6-day weeks have a session most days, so "24 h before a hard day" rules out many evenings. Keep sauna short (≤15 min) and put it after upper-body or easy-run days. 3-day weeks have rest days between sessions, so sauna after each session is compatible with the rule.
- Sleep: no reason to expect harm. An evening session ending 1-2 h before bed matches the warm-bath data. Treat any sleep benefit as unproven for sauna.
- Do not count on sauna to speed recovery of maximal strength. The only positive strength-recovery trial used repeated hot-water immersion after severe eccentric work.

### Gaps

- No trial measured traditional-sauna recovery after lifting with an exercise-only control.
- No trial measured objective sleep (polysomnography or actigraphy) after evening sauna in lifters.
- Skorski 2020 was read only through the review's table.

## KQ3. Endurance and heat acclimation for a recreational runner

### Takeaway

About 3 weeks of post-run sauna (3-4 sessions a week, ~30 min at 87-108 °C) produces heat acclimation: lower core temperature and heart rate in the heat, and larger plasma volume. Benefits in the heat are consistent. Benefits in **cool or temperate** conditions are contested. Two small trials in runners showed gains: Scoon 2007 had no pre-test, and Kirby 2021 was not randomised. Two small randomised sauna trials with cycling training showed none, and neither did two of three trials of exercise in the heat. For a recreational runner racing in November, the expected gain is small. Two days out from the 8 Nov race, there is nothing to gain and a fatigue and dehydration cost.

### Cited findings

- **[Evidence, foundational] Scoon, Hopkins, Mayhew & Cotter 2007**, *J Sci Med Sport* 10(4):259-262, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16877041/. Abstract read.
  - Crossover, 6 male distance runners: 3 weeks of post-training sauna vs 3 weeks of control, with a 3-week washout.
  - Sauna: humid, 89.9 ± 2.0 °C, 31 ± 5 min, immediately after training, on 12.7 ± 2.1 occasions.
  - **E:** run time to exhaustion +32% (90% CL 21-43%), which the authors equate to about +1.9% (1.3-2.4%) in a time trial.
  - Plasma volume +7.1%; red-cell volume +3.5% (unclear).
  - The Ahokas 2025 review notes there were **no pre-tests** before either period.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Stanley et al. 2015**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 115(4):785-794, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25432420/. Abstract read. 7 well-trained male cyclists; 30 min at 87 °C (11% RH) after training for 10 days. Plasma volume peaked at +17.8% (90% CL 7.4-29.2) after **4 exposures**. Waking heart rate fell 10.2%. No performance test.
- **[Evidence] Kirby, Lucas, Armstrong, Weaver & Lucas 2021a**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 121(2):621-635, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33211153/. **Full text read (PMC7862510).**
  - 20 trained middle-distance runners (13 women; VO2max 56.1 ml/kg/min). **Participants self-allocated** to sauna (n = 12) or control (n = 8).
  - Sauna: 28 ± 2 min at **101-108 °C, 5-10% RH**, after easy or long runs, 3 ± 1 times/week for 3 weeks, drinking freely. Done October-March in the UK.
  - **Heat tolerance (40 °C):** peak rectal temperature -0.2 °C and heart rate -11 bpm more than control.
  - **E (18 °C):** VO2max +0.27 L/min (P = 0.02) and speed at 4 mmol/L lactate +0.6 km/h (P = 0.01), both more than control.
  - Continuing to 7 weeks (n = 6) only lowered peak rectal temperature a further 0.1 °C.
- **[Evidence] Kirby et al. 2021b**, *Sports Med Open* 7:51, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34297227/. Abstract read. 26 runners (16 women), same protocol. VO2max +5% (P = 0.02) and speed at 4 mmol/L +0.4 km/h in both sexes. The abstract reports no control group for this analysis.
- **[Evidence] Zurawlew, Walsh, Fortes & Potter 2016**, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 26(7):745-754, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26661992/. Abstract read. 17 men. 6 days of a 40-min run at 65% VO2max (18 °C), then 40 min of HWI at 40 °C (n = 10) or 34 °C (n = 7). Resting rectal temperature fell 0.27 °C. 5-km time trial **in 33 °C improved 4.9%; in 18 °C it did not change**.
- **[Evidence] Zurawlew, Mee & Walsh 2019**, *Front Physiol* 10:1080, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31555140/. Abstract read. The same 6-day HWI protocol. Adaptations (resting rectal temperature -0.36 °C, exercise heart rate -14 bpm) were **retained for at least 2 weeks**. Haemoglobin mass, blood volume and plasma volume changes were not significant.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Lorenzo, Halliwill, Sawka & Minson 2010**, *J Appl Physiol* 109(4):1140-1147, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20724560/. Abstract read. 12 trained cyclists; 10 days of exercise heat acclimation (~50% VO2max in 40 °C) vs 8 controls training in 13 °C. In **cool (13 °C)** conditions: VO2max +5% and time trial +6%; plasma volume +6.5%. This is active acclimation, not sauna.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Keiser et al. 2015**, *Am J Physiol Heart Circ Physiol* 309(5):H750-H761, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26150574/. Abstract read. 7 men, counterbalanced crossover: 10 days of 90-min training at 38 vs 18 °C. VO2max (+9.6%) and time trial (+10.4%) improved in the heat; **both were unchanged at 18 °C**. Plasma volume +6%.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Karlsen et al. 2015**, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 25(Suppl 1):269-276, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25943678/. Abstract read. 18 trained cyclists, 2 weeks of outdoor training in ~35 °C vs cool. Time trial in the heat +16%. **No change** in VO2max or a 43.4-km outdoor time trial at 5-13 °C.
- **[Evidence] Sitkowski et al. 2022**, *Res Q Exerc Sport* 93(4):795-803, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34727008/. Abstract read. Randomised crossover, 13 male PE students. 4 weeks of 60-min cycling 3x/week with or without 30 min of sauna at 89 °C, **with no fluid allowed**. No added effect on haemoglobin mass, blood volumes or VO2peak.
- **[Evidence] Karolkiewicz et al. 2022**, RCT (8 sauna vs 7 control). Sauna ~90 °C, 3 × 10 min (weeks 1-2) then 2 × 15 min (weeks 3-4), 3x/week after cycling. VO2peak was not affected. Read only through the Ahokas 2025 review table.
- **[Evidence] Ahokas et al. 2025 review** (above): post-exercise heat "may improve running performance, at least in hot conditions"; "repeated heat exposures had no effect on cycling performance or VO2max".
- **[Practitioner] Stronger By Science podcast episodes 93 and 94** (2022; Eric Trexler and Greg Nuckols), https://www.strongerbyscience.com/podcast-episode-93/ and https://www.strongerbyscience.com/podcast-episode-94/. The show pages list Laukkanen 2015, Rissanen 2020, Skorski 2020, Scoon 2007 and Kirby 2021 as sources. Only the reference lists were read; their conclusions are in the audio and not recorded here.

### Inferences

- **This race (Sun 8 Nov 2026, cool weather):** do not start sauna now. Acclimation takes at least 4 exposures (Stanley) and more like 3 weeks for performance markers (Kirby). Any new heat stress in the last 48 h risks fatigue (Rissanen) and dehydration. After the race, wait until the next day, when fluids and food are back to normal (KQ6).
- **A future race in warm weather:** the tested dose is 3 weeks × 3 sessions/week × ~30 min, right after easy or long runs (Kirby). Finish the block in the last 1-2 weeks before the race; HWI adaptations lasted at least 2 weeks (Zurawlew 2019). Treat the first 1-2 weeks as extra stress: cut lifting volume a little in a 5-6-day week, or keep the 3-day template.
- **Cool-weather races:** expect little or nothing. The positive temperate findings come from a 6-man trial without pre-tests and a self-allocated trial. The randomised cycling trials and the active-acclimation trials at 13-18 °C mostly found no change.
- 3-day vs 5-6-day weeks: in a 3-day lifting week there is more room for 3 post-run sessions. In a 5-6-day week, a heat block competes with recovery; run it only if a hot race is the priority.

### Gaps

- No randomised trial of post-exercise sauna in recreational runners at this lifter's volume (<10 miles/week) was found.
- No study tested whether sauna acclimation transfers to a 10-mile race in cool weather.
- Haemoglobin mass changes with sauna alone are unresolved (Sitkowski 2022 restricted fluid; others did not measure it).

## KQ4. Hormones: growth hormone, testosterone, cortisol, and whether spikes matter

### Takeaway

The famous growth-hormone numbers come from small, old studies with extreme doses. "16-fold" is Leppäluoto 1986: 10 men, 1 h at 80 °C twice a day for 7 days, with the effect fading after day 3. The "2-fold" and "5-fold" protocols could not be traced to a primary paper. Testosterone changes are small and inconsistent. After lifting, a sauna did not add to the exercise hormone response. Most importantly, acute rises in GH and testosterone within the normal range do not predict muscle growth or strength. Even injected GH did not add muscle in young men who lift.

### Cited findings

- **[Evidence, foundational] Leppäluoto et al. 1986**, *Acta Physiol Scand* 128(3):467-470, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3788622/. Abstract read.
  - 10 men and 7 women (women for prolactin only). Finnish sauna at 80 °C, **1 h twice a day for 7 days**.
  - Men: serum GH rose **16-fold** and prolactin 2.3-fold (P < 0.01).
  - Testosterone, LH, FSH and thyroid hormones did not change. Cortisol and ACTH fell slightly.
  - **The GH response declined after day 3.** The authors link the rises partly to dehydration.
  - Five of the seven women had transient amenorrhoea.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Kukkonen-Harjula et al. 1989**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 58(5):543-550, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2759081/. Abstract read.
  - 8 young men in three sessions, each until discomfort or exhaustion: 80 °C dry, 100 °C dry, and 80 °C dry becoming humid.
  - Oral temperature rose 1.1, 1.9 and 3.2 °C; heart rate rose about 60%, 90% and 130%. Prolactin rose 2-, 7- and 10-fold.
  - GH rose at 100 °C. Testosterone rose only in the humid condition. Cortisol fell at 80 and 100 °C dry.
  - **The abstract gives no GH fold-change.**
- **[Evidence] Rissanen et al. 2020** (KQ2; manuscript read). Sauna alone raised testosterone 15% immediately afterwards. Exercise raised GH (22 kD), testosterone and cortisol, and "a sauna bath after the loadings did not further change the hormonal responses".
- **[Evidence] Ahokas 2023** (KQ2; full text). After the infrared session, next-morning cortisol was higher than after passive recovery, but the pre-to-post change did not differ. Other hormones did not differ.
- **[Evidence] West et al. 2010**, *J Appl Physiol* 108(1):60-67, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19910330/. Abstract read. 12 young men trained each arm for 15 weeks, one arm with a large exercise-induced rise in GH, IGF-1 and testosterone (leg work added) and one without. Arm CSA rose 12% (low hormone) vs 10% (high hormone), with no difference; strength gains were also the same.
- **[Evidence] West & Phillips 2012**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 112(7):2693-2702, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22105707/. Abstract read. 56 young men, 12 weeks. Acute GH, free testosterone and IGF-1 responses did not correlate with gains in lean mass or strength. The GH response correlated weakly with fibre-area gain (r = 0.28-0.36). Cortisol correlated with lean-mass gain (r = 0.29).
- **[Evidence] Morton et al. 2016**, *J Appl Physiol* 121(1):129-138, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27174923/. Abstract read. 49 **resistance-trained** men, 12 weeks: "No significant correlations between the acute postexercise rise in any purported anabolic hormone and the change in strength or hypertrophy."
- **[Evidence, foundational] Yarasheski et al. 1992**, *Am J Physiol* 262(3):E261-E267, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1550219/. Abstract read. 16 young men, 12 weeks of training with recombinant GH (40 µg/kg/day) or placebo. Fat-free mass rose more with GH, but quadriceps protein synthesis, limb size and strength did not. The extra fat-free mass was "probably" non-muscle lean tissue.
- **[Practitioner] Popular claims.**
  - Huberman Lab newsletter, "Deliberate Heat Exposure Protocols", https://hubermanlab.com/newsletter/deliberate-heat-exposure-protocols-for-health-and-performance (page read). For GH: sauna once a week or less, four 30-min sessions in one day, semi-fasted, "up to 16-fold in one study". For general health: about 1 h/week in 2-3 sessions at 80-100 °C.
  - FoundMyFitness sauna topic page, https://www.foundmyfitness.com/topics/sauna (page read). "Two 20-minute sessions at 80 °C … two-fold" and "two 15-minute sessions at 100 °C … five-fold", both cited to Hannuksela 2001 (a review) and Kukkonen-Harjula 1989.
  - The 16-fold figure matches Leppäluoto 1986: 2 h/day for 7 days in 10 men. The 2- and 5-fold figures are not in the 1989 abstract.

### Inferences

- Ignore GH and testosterone as a reason to use a sauna. Acute hormone spikes, even large ones, did not change hypertrophy or strength in three lifting trials (West 2010, West 2012, Morton 2016). Supra-physiological injected GH did not add muscle in trained young men (Yarasheski 1992).
- The "GH protocol" (4 × 30 min in one day, fasted) is the most dehydrating and hypotension-prone way to use a sauna, for no proven muscle benefit. Do not use it, especially in a calorie deficit.
- No change between 3-day and 5-6-day weeks.

### Gaps

- The primary source for "2-fold at 2 × 20 min, 80 °C" and "5-fold at 2 × 15 min, 100 °C" was not found. The full texts of Kukkonen-Harjula 1989 and Hannuksela 2001 were not read.
- No study links sauna-induced hormone changes to training adaptations.

## KQ5. Health: cardiovascular disease, mortality, blood pressure, and male fertility

### Takeaway

In one Finnish cohort of middle-aged men, more frequent sauna use went with lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality: all-cause HR 0.60 for 4-7 vs 1 session/week. Associations are similar for hypertension and stroke, and for tub bathing in Japan. The data are observational. Everyone in the reference group used a sauna weekly, the 4-7/week group was small, and healthy-user bias is likely. Randomised evidence is short-term but points the same way: sauna added to exercise lowered systolic BP by 8 mmHg in 8 weeks, and hot-water immersion improved vascular function. For a 25-year-old the absolute mortality effect is tiny and untested. The cost relevant to his age is **reversible loss of sperm count and motility**.

### Cited findings

**Observational (KIHD cohort, eastern Finland, traditional sauna)**

- **[Evidence] Laukkanen, Khan, Zaccardi & Laukkanen 2015**, *JAMA Intern Med* 175(4):542-548, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25705824/. Abstract read; the hazard-ratio table and covariates were taken from the publisher full text through a summarising fetch tool (https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2130724).
  - 2,315 men aged 42-60, baseline 1984-1989, median follow-up 20.7 years.
  - Groups: 601 men at 1 session/week, 1,513 at 2-3, and 201 at 4-7.
  - Mean sauna 78.9 °C, mean session 14.2 min.
  - Adjusted for age, BMI, systolic BP, LDL, smoking, alcohol, prior MI, type 2 diabetes, cardiorespiratory fitness, resting HR, physical activity and socioeconomic status.

  | Outcome (vs 1/week) | 2-3/week HR (95% CI) | 4-7/week HR (95% CI) |
  |---|---|---|
  | Sudden cardiac death | 0.78 (0.57-1.07) | 0.37 (0.18-0.75) |
  | Fatal coronary heart disease | 0.77 (0.60-0.99) | 0.52 (0.31-0.88) |
  | Fatal cardiovascular disease | 0.73 (0.59-0.89) | 0.50 (0.33-0.77) |
  | All-cause mortality | 0.76 (0.66-0.88) | 0.60 (0.46-0.80) |

  - Duration: sessions >19 min vs <11 min, sudden cardiac death HR 0.48 (0.31-0.75). There was no duration association for all-cause death.
  - Authors' limits: a single baseline questionnaire, men only, traditional hot dry sauna only, and possible residual confounding.
  - The 4-7/week group had only 10 sudden cardiac deaths.
- **[Evidence] Zaccardi et al. 2017**, *Am J Hypertens* 30(11):1120-1125, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28633297/. Abstract read. 1,621 normotensive men, median 24.7 years, 251 new cases of hypertension. Fully adjusted (including fitness): 2-3/week HR 0.83 (0.59-1.18); 4-7/week HR 0.53 (0.28-0.98).
- **[Evidence] Kunutsor, Khan, Laukkanen & Laukkanen 2018**, *Ann Med* 50(2):139-146, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28972808/. Abstract read. 2,277 men, median 26.1 years. Sauna 3-7 vs ≤2/week: CVD death HR 0.74 (0.59-0.94), all-cause HR 0.84 (0.72-0.97). High vs low fitness: 0.51 and 0.65. High fitness plus frequent sauna: CVD death HR 0.42 (0.28-0.62). **Fitness was the stronger association.**
- **[Evidence] Kunutsor et al. 2018**, *Neurology* 90(22):e1937-e1944, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29720543/. Abstract read. 1,628 men and women aged 53-74, median 14.9 years. Stroke HR for 4-7/week was 0.38 (0.18-0.81) after adjustment, including physical activity and socioeconomic status.
- **[Evidence] Ukai et al. 2020**, *Heart* 106(10):732-737, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32209614/. Abstract read. Japanese cohort, 30,076 adults aged 40-59, **hot-tub bathing**. Almost daily vs 0-2/week: total CVD HR 0.72 (0.62-0.84), stroke 0.74 (0.62-0.87). No association with sudden cardiac death.

**Randomised and controlled trials**

- **[Evidence] Lee et al. 2022**, *Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol* 323(3):R289-R299, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35785965/. **Full text read (PMC9394774).**
  - 47 inactive adults (49 ± 9 y) with at least one CVD risk factor; 8 weeks.
  - Arms: guideline exercise 3x/week (~50 min) plus **15 min of sauna starting at 65 °C and rising 5 °C every fortnight (10-20% RH)**; exercise alone; or control.
  - Exercise + sauna vs exercise alone: systolic BP -8.0 mmHg (95% CI -14.6 to -1.4), fitness +2.7 ml/kg/min (+0.2 to +5.3), and lower total cholesterol.
  - Only one dropout.
- **[Evidence] Brunt et al. 2016**, *J Physiol* 594(18):5329-5342, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27270841/. Abstract read. 20 young sedentary adults. 8 weeks of **hot-water immersion** at 40.5 °C, 4-5x/week, holding rectal temperature ≥38.5 °C for 60 min, vs a thermoneutral sham. Flow-mediated dilation rose from 5.6 to 10.9%. Aortic pulse-wave velocity fell from 7.1 to 6.1 m/s. Mean arterial pressure fell from 83 to 78 mmHg. Carotid intima-media thickness fell from 0.43 to 0.37 mm.
- **[Evidence] Laukkanen, Laukkanen & Kunutsor 2018**, *Mayo Clin Proc* 93(8):1111-1121, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30077204/ (narrative review; abstract read), and **Hussain & Cohen 2018**, *Evid Based Complement Alternat Med* 2018:1857413, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29849692/ (systematic review; abstract read). The second found 40 studies (3,855 participants), only 13 of them RCTs, mostly n < 40. The only adverse health outcome it found was the reversible sperm effect (Garolla 2013).

**Male fertility (scrotal heat)**

- **[Evidence] Garolla et al. 2013**, *Hum Reprod* 28(4):877-885, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23411620/. Abstract read (full text paywalled).
  - 10 normozoospermic men; Finnish sauna **2x/week for 3 months, 80-90 °C, 15 min each**. No control group.
  - At the end, sperm count and motility were strongly impaired (P < 0.001). Sex hormones did not change.
  - The share of sperm with normal histone-protamine substitution fell from 78.7 to 69.0%, and with normal mitochondrial function from 76.8 to 54.0%.
  - **All effects were "completely reversed" 6 months after stopping.** The 3-month post-stop values were not in the abstract.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Brown-Woodman et al. 1984**, *Arch Androl* 12(1):9-15, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6476971/. Abstract read. A **single** sauna, 85 °C for 20 min: sperm numbers fell within 1 week and returned to normal over 5 weeks. The sample size is not in the abstract.
- **[Evidence] Rao et al. 2015**, *Asian J Androl* 17(4):668-675, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25652627/. Abstract read. 20 men, scrotal water bath at 43 °C for 30 min, 10 times (daily, or every 3 days). Concentration and motility fell reversibly over the 16-week follow-up. Intermittent exposure suppressed concentration more.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Shefi et al. 2007**, *Int Braz J Urol* 33(1):50-56, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17335598/. Abstract read. 11 infertile men exposed to hot tubs or baths. After stopping, 5 (45%) improved; their total motile count rose 491%, and motility went from 12% to 34%. Uncontrolled.

### Inferences

- **Cardiovascular:** reasonable as a pleasant habit that may help blood pressure and vascular function. Do not use it as a substitute for fitness. In KIHD, fitness had the stronger association, and the combination was best. The best-supported pattern is 2-3 or more sessions a week of ~15-20 min at ~80 °C (the KIHD means). No trial has tested mortality.
- **For a 25-year-old,** the mortality data (men aged 42-60) have little direct relevance. The blood-pressure RCTs (middle-aged people with risk factors, and sedentary young adults with hot water) are closer, but neither is a trained young lifter.
- **Fertility:** regular sauna lowers sperm count and motility. It recovers, but full recovery was confirmed only at 6 months. If he plans to conceive within the next ~6 months, skip regular sauna or hot tubs, or see a professional. Otherwise this is a reversible trade-off, not a reason to avoid sauna.
- Frequency does not depend on the lifting week type. 2-3 sessions/week fits both 3-day and 5-6-day weeks (KQ7).

### Gaps

- No randomised trial of sauna and hard outcomes (events or mortality) exists. No cohort covers young men or regular exercisers specifically.
- The Garolla full text (3-month post-stop values, individual variability) was not read. Whether infrared or 10-min sessions affect sperm less was not tested.
- KIHD has no non-user group, so "sauna vs none" cannot be estimated from it.

## KQ6. Safety and practicalities

### Takeaway

For healthy people, sauna is low-risk. The serious risks cluster around **alcohol**: half of Finnish sauna deaths involved it, and alcohol plus sauna dropped systolic BP by 23 mmHg. Expect to sweat about 0.5-0.8 kg (1-2 lb) in 20 min, more at higher heat. Sauna **before** lifting cut leg-press 1RM and muscular endurance. A sauna or any dehydration before an InBody scan reads water loss as fat loss. Contraindications include unstable angina, recent heart attack and severe aortic stenosis. BP medicines raise the risk of fainting on standing.

### Cited findings

**Deaths and alcohol**

- **[Evidence] Kenttämies & Karkola 2008**, *J Forensic Sci* 53(3):724-729, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18471223/. Abstract read. Finland, 1990-2002. Deaths in a sauna occurred at **under 2 per 100,000 inhabitants per year**. 51% were natural deaths and heat was the cause in 25%. **50% of those who died were under the influence of alcohol**, and alcohol's role had grown. Advice: drink less, and do not leave a drunk bather alone.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Kortelainen 1991**, *Am J Forensic Med Pathol* 12(2):115-118, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1882775/. Abstract read. 228 hyperthermia deaths in Finland, 1970-86. Most occurred in a sauna, most victims were men aged 42-62, and most had acute alcohol on board.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Ylikahri, Heikkonen & Soukas 1988**, *Ann Clin Res* 20(4):287-291, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3218903/. Abstract read; narrative. Alcohol contributed to an estimated 20-25 sauna-related deaths a year in Finland (population 4.8 million). Risks named: fainting on standing, burns, falls, drowning, and arrhythmia, especially in the **hangover phase**.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Roine et al. 1992**, *J Intern Med* 231(4):333-338, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1588256/. Abstract read. 10 healthy men. Sauna alone left systolic BP unchanged; sauna after heavy drinking dropped it from 136 ± 4 to 113 ± 3 mmHg (P < 0.01). Premature ventricular beats did not increase.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Hannuksela & Ellahham 2001**, *Am J Med* 110(2):118-126, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11165553/. Abstract read. Review. Contraindications: unstable angina, recent myocardial infarction and severe aortic stenosis. Alcohol during sauna "increases the risk of hypotension, arrhythmia, and sudden death, and should be avoided".
- **[Evidence, foundational] Kukkonen-Harjula & Kauppinen 2006**, *Int J Circumpolar Health* 65(3):195-205, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16871826/. Abstract read. Review. Sauna was well tolerated by healthy people. **Antihypertensive medication may predispose to orthostatic hypotension after bathing.** Most data came from uncontrolled studies in lifelong bathers. Typical experimental exposure: 80-90 °C, 5-20 min, repeated 1-3 times.

**Cardiovascular load during the session**

- **[Evidence] Ketelhut & Ketelhut 2019**, *Complement Ther Med* 44:218-222, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31126559/. Abstract read. 19 adults (46 y), 25 min at 93 °C. BP and heart rate **rose** progressively during the sauna, equivalent to cycling at about 60-100 W. BP then fell **below** baseline afterwards.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Hedley, Climstein & Hansen 2002** (below): 30 min at 65-75 °C took BP from 124/78 to 148/60 mmHg and heart rate from 64 to 122 bpm.
- **[Evidence] Iguchi 2012** (KQ1): 30 min in a 73 °C chamber lowered systolic BP by 16 mmHg and diastolic by 5 mmHg; heart rate reached 131 bpm.

**Sweat loss and hydration**

- **[Evidence] Podstawski et al. 2014**, *ScientificWorldJournal* 2014:307421, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25614882/. **Full text read (PMC4295591).** 674 sedentary students (348 men). 2 × 10 min supine at 90 °C, 35% RH, with a 5-min break including a 30-s cold plunge. Body-mass loss ranged from **0.24 kg (underweight women) to 0.82 kg (men with BMI ≥25)**. Heavier people lost more. The paper also cites "average total secretion of 0.5 kg" per session (secondary).
- **[Evidence] Rissanen 2020** (manuscript read): with water allowed freely, body-mass loss stayed **<0.9%** across a 30-min, 70 °C sauna after exercise.
- **[Evidence] Savoie et al. 2015**, *Sports Med* 45(8):1207-1227, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26178327/. Abstract read. Meta-analysis of 28 papers. Hypohydration cut muscle endurance by 8.3%, strength by 5.5% and anaerobic power by 5.8%. Losses were smaller when dehydration was passive, as in a sauna, and in trained people.

**Heat before lifting**

- **[Evidence, foundational] Hedley, Climstein & Hansen 2002**, *J Strength Cond Res* 16(3):353-358, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12173948/. Abstract read. 10 weight-trained men (23 y), randomised to 30 min at 65-75 °C (15% RH) or at 22.5 °C, then tested while euhydrated. Oral temperature rose 2.48 °C.
  - **S:** bench 1RM was unchanged, but **leg press 1RM fell** (P < 0.05).
  - **Muscular endurance fell 29.2% (leg press) and 15.8% (bench).**
  - Vertical jump rose 3.1%.
- **[Evidence] Rissanen 2020:** sauna alone lowered leg and bench force at 30 min, and leg force was still -4.1% at 24 h.

**Body-composition scans**

- **[Evidence, foundational] Saunders, Blevins & Broeder 1998**, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 30(6):885-892, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9624647/. Abstract read. 15 athletes. At 3% exercise hypohydration, BIA body fat fell from 14.4% to 12.3%; after rehydration it read 15.5%. **82% of the water lost was read as fat lost.** This was an older BIA device.
- **[Evidence] Aburto-Corona et al. 2024**, *Nutrients* 16(14):2202, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39064645/. Abstract read; device confirmed in the full text (PMC11279509). 25 active men; **InBody 770**. Five hours of passive dehydration (26.6 °C, 72% RH) cost 0.76 ± 0.34 kg of body mass, and **BIA fat mass fell 0.90 ± 0.87 kg**. The authors call this clinically negligible. At this lifter's size, a 0.9 kg fat swing is about 1 percentage point of body fat, the size of a real month's change in a recomposition.
- **[Convention] InBody 970 user manual**, measurement precautions: "Avoid using the sauna or bath before measuring". Read only as a search snippet of a manual copy (https://manualslib.mx/manual/694248/Inbody-970.html?page=23). The InBody pre-test rules are covered in `research/body-composition-measurement.md` KQ1.

**Popular hydration and safety advice**

- **[Practitioner] Huberman Lab newsletter** (above): drink "at least 16 ounces of water for every 10 minutes" in the sauna. It also says not to sauna if pregnant or under 16, to start at cooler temperatures, and that hyperthermia and dehydration are possible. It does not mention alcohol or heart conditions.

### Inferences

- **Alcohol:** never sauna while drinking or within the hangover window, and never alone after drinking. For this lifter, that means no sauna on weekend drinking nights and none the morning after. If he drinks Friday and Saturday, his sauna days are Sunday evening at the earliest, more safely Monday-Thursday. This is the single largest risk factor in the death data.
- **Sweat estimate:** at 181 lb, plan on about 1-2 lb (0.5-0.8 kg) per 20 min at 80-90 °C. Weigh before and after a few times to learn your own rate. Replace the loss with water over the next 1-2 h, and get sodium from the next meal. The Huberman figure (16 oz per 10 min, about 1.4 L per 30 min) is 1.5-3 times the measured loss in Podstawski. It does no harm in one session, but it is not a measured requirement.
- **Order with lifting:** lift first, then sauna. No sauna before a session. A warm-up does the job of raising muscle temperature without the strength-endurance cost (Hedley: -16% to -29% reps).
- **InBody:** scan first, sauna afterwards. No sauna, hot bath or hot tub on scan morning. Avoid a long or hot session the evening before as well, and keep the pre-scan routine identical each time (`research/body-composition-measurement.md`). If a sauna happened, note it on the scan and do not read a single-scan fat drop as real.
- **Weigh-ins:** in a deficit, a post-sauna bodyweight is water. Take daily weigh-ins before any sauna, in the usual morning routine.
- **Safe dose for a healthy beginner [Convention, from the cited ranges]:** traditional 70-90 °C, 5-10 min for the first sessions, building to 10-20 min, in 1-2 rounds with a cool-down between. Stand up slowly. Leave if dizzy, nauseated, or if your chest hurts or your heart races.
- **Who should consult a doctor first:** anyone with heart disease, chest pain, arrhythmia, fainting, a heart valve problem, high or low BP, or kidney disease; anyone on BP medicines, diuretics or other drugs that affect heart rate or fluids; anyone with a recent illness or fever; and men trying to conceive.

### Gaps

- No data on creatine plus sauna (water shifts, cramping, performance). There is no known interaction, but no study either.
- Sweat sodium concentration in a sauna was not found, so electrolyte replacement beyond normal meals is an inference.
- The InBody manual text was read only as a search snippet.
- Roine 1992's alcohol dose and the sample size of Brown-Woodman 1984 are not in the abstracts.

## KQ7. Applied protocol for this lifter (inference)

Everything in this section is **inference** from KQ1-KQ6. Sauna is optional. It is not part of the program, and nothing in the templates depends on it.

### Should he use one at all?

| Goal | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Muscle and strength | Neutral to slightly positive; not a reason to go | Hopper 2026 H SMD 0.12 (uncertain), S -0.05; Fuchs 2020 no change in protein synthesis |
| Soreness and feeling recovered | Plausible small benefit from short, mild sessions | Ahokas 2023 (n = 16); Wang 2022 (local heat) |
| Next-day strength | Neutral if short; a cost if long and hot | Rissanen 2020 (-4.1% at 24 h after 32 min at 70 °C) |
| Running in cool weather | Little expected | Keiser 2015, Karlsen 2015, Sitkowski 2022 vs Scoon 2007, Kirby 2021 |
| Running in heat | Yes, with a 3-week block | Kirby 2021, Zurawlew 2016 |
| Long-term cardiovascular health | Plausible; observational plus short RCTs | Laukkanen 2015, Lee 2022, Brunt 2016 |
| Fertility in the next 6 months | Avoid regular use | Garolla 2013 |

### Default protocol (if he has access and likes it)

| Item | Setting |
|---|---|
| Type | Traditional dry sauna 80-90 °C (176-194 °F), or infrared 45-60 °C (113-140 °F) |
| Time | Traditional: 10-15 min, 1-2 rounds, ≤20 min total. Infrared: 15-20 min. First 2-3 sessions: 5-10 min |
| Frequency | 2-3 sessions/week. More is fine for health but adds hydration load and fertility exposure |
| When | After lifting (after the cool-down and a drink), or after an easy run. Evening is fine; finish 1-2 h before bed |
| Never | Before a lifting session; while drinking or hungover; alone after alcohol; on InBody scan morning |
| Not within ~24 h before | The heaviest lower-body day, a race, or a 1RM or top-set test (applies to long or hot sessions; a 10-min mild session is unlikely to matter) |
| Hydration | Weigh before and after a few times. Replace the loss (typically 16-28 oz, 0.5-0.8 L) over 1-2 h, with a normal salted meal |
| Bodyweight log | Weigh in before any sauna, in the usual morning routine |

### By week type

| | 3-day lifting week | 5-6-day lifting week |
|---|---|---|
| Sessions/week | 2-3, after any lifting session (rest days follow, so the 24-h rule rarely binds) | 2, at most 3, after upper-body or easy-run days |
| Avoid | The evening before a scan | The evening before the heaviest lower day; back-to-back long sessions |

### Race week and race weekend (now: race Sun 8 Nov 2026)

- **Not a regular user:** no sauna this week. Two days out it cannot acclimate him (KQ3), and it adds fatigue and dehydration risk.
- **Already a regular user:** sessions earlier in the week do not matter. From today (Fri 6 Nov) until the day after the race: none.
- **After the race:** wait until the next day, once fluids and food are back to normal and any celebration drinks are out of his system. Post-race dehydration, alcohol and heat combine the three biggest risk factors in KQ6.
- Race-week lifting rules are in `research/running-and-lifting.md` KQ2; this note does not change them.

### If a warm-weather race comes up later

- Do 3 weeks of 3 sessions/week, ~25-30 min after easy or long runs, at whatever temperature the gym runs (the trials used 87-108 °C). Build up from 10-15 min.
- Finish within 1-2 weeks of the race.
- During the block, prefer 3-day lifting weeks, or cut accessory volume in 5-6-day weeks.

## Conflicts

1. **Does heat build muscle?**
   - Yes, a little: Goto 2011 (passive heat, untrained), Nakamura 2019 (30% 1RM), Bartolomé 2025 (untrained, BIA), Hopper 2026 (SMD 0.12, 89.7% probability >0).
   - No: Labidi 2021 (active adults), Stadnyk 2018 (+15% vs +15%), Ahokas 2025 (no interaction), Kim 2020 (strength but not fibre size), Fuchs 2020 (no change in protein synthesis).
   - Reading: positive results come from untrained, very-low-load, local-heat or BIA-measured designs. In trained or active people the effect is small or absent. Both are kept.
2. **Heat acclimation and cool-weather performance.**
   - Gains: Lorenzo 2010 (+5% VO2max, +6% time trial at 13 °C), Scoon 2007 (+32% time to exhaustion, no pre-test), Kirby 2021 (self-allocated).
   - No gain: Keiser 2015, Karlsen 2015, Zurawlew 2016 (18 °C), Sitkowski 2022 and Karolkiewicz 2022 (randomised).
   - Not resolved. The better-controlled studies lean towards little or no temperate benefit.
3. **Post-exercise heat and next-day performance.**
   - Mild infrared helped jump recovery and soreness (Ahokas 2023; Mero 2015 after running).
   - Hot traditional sauna was a fatigue load (Rissanen 2020) and slowed next-morning sprints (Skorski 2020).
   - Temperature, duration and familiarity probably explain the split. The review (Ahokas 2025) calls the evidence uncertain.
4. **Fertility.**
   - Hannuksela & Ellahham 2001 (review): sauna "does not influence fertility".
   - Garolla 2013, Brown-Woodman 1984, Rao 2015: reversible impairment.
   - The later, direct measurements are preferred. Recovery time also conflicts: the Huberman newsletter says "~45-60 days", Brown-Woodman 5 weeks after a single session, and Garolla full reversal confirmed only at 6 months after 3 months of use. Use the longer figure when planning.
5. **Blood pressure during the session.**
   - Rose: Ketelhut 2019 (93 °C), Hedley 2002 (systolic 124 → 148).
   - Fell: Iguchi 2012 (73 °C chamber, systolic -16 mmHg).
   - All agree BP falls **after** the session. That, plus alcohol (Roine 1992) and BP drugs (Kukkonen-Harjula 2006), is the fainting risk.
6. **Hydration advice.** The Huberman newsletter's 16 oz per 10 min vs the measured 0.24-0.82 kg per 20 min (Podstawski 2014). This note uses the measured loss.
7. **Testosterone.** No change (Leppäluoto 1986); a rise only in humid exhaustion (Kukkonen-Harjula 1989); +15% after sauna alone (Rissanen 2020). These are all acute and irrelevant to growth (KQ4).
8. **What "traditional" means.** Mero 2015's "traditional" sauna was 35-50 °C. Results from that arm do not describe an 80-90 °C Finnish sauna.
