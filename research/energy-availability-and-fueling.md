## KQ1. Energy availability: definitions, thresholds, and the evidence in men

### Takeaway

Energy availability (EA) is energy intake minus exercise energy expenditure, per kg of FFM per day. The well-known 30 and 45 kcal/kg FFM cut-offs come from short laboratory studies in sedentary women, and the IOC says they were meant as a guide, not a diagnosis. In men, the IOC 2023 consensus says the level at which problems appear "appears to be lower (eg, ~9 to 25 kcal/kg FFM/day)". In short male laboratory studies, 15 kcal/kg FFM for 4-5 days lowered leptin and insulin and shifted bone turnover toward breakdown; testosterone and T3 held in some studies and fell in others. Over 14-day steps in trained endurance men, explosive power fell first (at an EA of about 22), then endurance. Calculated EA is error-prone. Hormones, symptoms and injury history are better markers.

### Cited findings

**Definitions and thresholds**

- **[Evidence, consensus] Mountjoy et al. 2023**, IOC consensus statement on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs), *Br J Sports Med* 57(17):1073-1097, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/. Full text read (copy of the published PDF).
  - EA = (EI − EEE) / FFM, in kcal/kg FFM/day.
  - Low energy availability (LEA) is "any mismatch between dietary energy intake and energy expended in exercise that leaves the body's total energy needs unmet".
  - LEA runs on a continuum. **Adaptable LEA** has "benign effects, including mild and quickly reversible changes" and "is typically a short-term experience". **Problematic LEA** brings "greater and potentially persistent disruption ... often presenting with signs and/or symptoms". REDs is caused by problematic (prolonged and/or severe) LEA.
  - Zones from research in sedentary women: ≥45 for mass gain; about 45 for weight maintenance; 30-45 "reduced EA for body mass/fat loss"; ≤30 "LEA causing health implications". The threshold "was intended as a guide, rather than a diagnostic end-point". The panel warns against setting "a definitive clinical threshold".
  - Men: "only 20% of original studies from 2018 to 2022 include male athletes". The male threshold "is even less understood ... but appears to be lower (eg, ~9 to 25 kcal/kg FFM/day)". "Most males can sustain a lower EA before physiological and psychological disturbances manifest." Problematic LEA in men still affects the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, metabolic hormones, immunity, bone, performance and lean-mass accrual.
  - Low carbohydrate availability (LCA) adds to LEA. LEA interventions usually cut carbohydrate by 25-60%, and real-world dieters who prioritise protein may cut more. In short (≤6 day) studies of male endurance athletes, diets under 3 g/kg/day of carbohydrate raised bone-resorption markers, sometimes without LEA. Six studies since 2019 show an "energy-independent and/or magnifying" effect of LCA.
  - Measurement: EA in free-living athletes is "challenged by the errors associated with accurately measuring EI, EEE and other contributing components (eg, FFM, resting metabolic rate)".
  - Time course: short-term LEA is "a few days to weeks", medium-term "weeks to months", long-term "months to years"; these cut-offs still need validation.
- **[Evidence] Areta, Taylor & Koehler 2021**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 121(1):1-21, review, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7815551/. About the first 60% of the full text read.
  - EEE should be **net**: resting expenditure (and non-exercise activity) removed from the gross cost of exercise.
  - Definitions have shifted. In the paper's worked example (75 kg, 15% fat, 1 h at 700 kcal/h), the same data give 40, 47 and 50 kcal/kg LBM/day under three definitions.
  - Non-exercise activity (NEAT) is excluded from EEE and is hard to measure.
  - The 30 kcal/kg threshold comes from young, lean, sedentary women. For males, whether thresholds exist is "unknown". "Men's physiology is more resilient to LEA", framed as tentative.
  - Prospective studies that manipulate EA mostly last ≤5 days.
- **[Evidence, position stand] Thomas, Erdman & Burke 2016**, AND/DC/ACSM, *J Acad Nutr Diet* 116(3):501-528, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26920240/. Full text read. In women, 45 kcal/kg FFM/day was associated with energy balance and optimal health; a chronic reduction "particularly below 30" impaired body functions.

**Observational data in men, and lifters**

- **[Evidence] Torstveit et al. 2018**, *Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab* 28(4):419-427, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29405793/. Abstract read. 31 male cyclists, triathletes and runners; 20 had suppressed resting metabolic rate (measured/predicted <0.90). 24-h energy balance and EA did not differ between the groups. The suppressed group spent more hours in a deficit of more than 400 kcal (20.9 vs 10.8 h). Larger single-hour deficits went with higher cortisol and a lower testosterone:cortisol ratio.
- **[Evidence] Heikura et al. 2018**, *Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab* 28(4):403-411, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29252050/. Abstract read. National- and world-class distance runners. Men with testosterone in the lowest quartile (n = 10; 15.1 ± 3.0 nmol/L, 40% lower) vs normal (n = 14): lower T3, and bone injuries about 4.5-fold more prevalent (effect size 0.52). The authors judge reproductive hormones a more accurate marker of adequate energy than calculated EA.
- **[Evidence] Gallant et al. 2025**, *Sports Med* 55(2):325-339, systematic review and meta-analysis, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39485653/. Abstract read. 59 studies. LEA in 49.4% of male athletes across 46 studies (definitions varied). LEA went with poorer running and endurance performance, training response, explosive power and agility, and more days lost to illness. Most studies found worse bone health and more bone stress injuries.
- **[Evidence] Fagerberg 2018**, *Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab* 28(4):385-402, review, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28530498/. Abstract read. Natural male bodybuilders in contest preparation. Prolonged EA under 25 is linked with muscle loss, hormonal imbalance, psychological problems and cardiovascular effects near 4-5% body fat. In case studies, EA under 20 went with muscle loss above 40% of total weight lost. No controlled study exists in this group.

**Symptoms and markers: the IOC REDs Clinical Assessment Tool, version 2 (CAT2)**

From Mountjoy 2023, Table 4 (read). Male-relevant items only.

| Level | Indicators |
|---|---|
| Severe primary (counts as two) | Clinically low free or total testosterone (below the reference range) |
| Primary | Testosterone in the lowest quartile of the reference range; T3 in or below the lowest quartile; 1 or more high-risk (femoral neck, sacrum, pelvis) or 2 or more low-risk bone stress injuries in 2 years; BMD Z-score below −1 (men under 50); EDE-Q global score above 1.68 (males) or a diagnosed eating disorder |
| Secondary | 1 low-risk bone stress injury in 2 years; raised total or LDL cholesterol; diagnosed depression or anxiety |
| Potential (not scored, emerging) | Low IGF-1, glucose or insulin; falling iron studies or haemoglobin; raised morning cortisol; GI symptoms; RMR below 30 kcal/kg FFM/day or RMR ratio below 0.90; low libido and fewer morning erections; dizziness on standing; resting heart rate below 40; blood pressure below 90/60; sleep problems; psychological symptoms; exercise dependence; low BMI |

The IOC says the tool "should not be used in isolation nor solely for diagnosis" and is not a substitute for a physician-led team. Each indicator needs other causes ruled out.

### Inferences

- For a man, the evidence puts concern in the low 20s and below, sustained over weeks. An EA in the 30s in a man is in the IOC's "reduced EA for body mass/fat loss" band, which was defined in women and is the expected state of a planned fat-loss phase.
- One low day is adaptable LEA. A pattern of low days, or low days plus symptoms, is the concern.
- Carbohydrate matters on its own. Keeping carbohydrate at 3 g/kg or more, and higher around runs, protects against the "LCA magnifies LEA" effect.
- Lifters and runners combined: the male data come mostly from endurance athletes and bodybuilders in contest preparation. Nobody has studied a recreational lifter who runs under 10 miles a week.

### Gaps

- No male threshold has been validated; 9-25 is a range from one 14-day trial plus small studies.
- No study lasting longer than 14 days manipulated EA in men.
- The remaining 40% of Areta 2021 was not read.
- The male-specific CAT2 validation paper (Stellingwerff et al. 2023, *Br J Sports Med* 57:1109-1118) was not read.

## KQ2. This lifter's estimated energy availability

### Takeaway

On the current plan, with the run add-back eaten, this lifter's EA is about 29-39 kcal/kg FFM/day on most days, and the weekly average is about 34-36 in both week types. That is above the 9-25 range of concern for men. The lowest regular days are lifting days in 3-day weeks (about 29-33) and lifting-plus-running days (about 27-33). The risk days are runs where the add-back is skipped: race day without extra food lands at about 18-21, and a 5-mile run without it at about 28. A 300-400 kcal/day deficit plus under 10 miles a week of running is not, on these numbers, a REDs risk, provided the run food is eaten and carbohydrate stays up.

### Uncertainty

| Input | Effect on EA |
|---|---|
| Lifting MET (3.5 vs 6) | About 3 units on a lifting day |
| Net vs gross EEE | About 1-2 units on a lifting day; about 2 on race day |
| Run cost (100 vs 130 kcal/mile) | About 0.4 units per mile, so about 4 units on race day |
| Intake error | Each 100 kcal logged but not eaten (or eaten but not logged) moves EA about 1.5 units. The size of this lifter's logging error is unknown |
| FFM (InBody) | ±2 kg of FFM moves an EA of 33 by about ±1 unit. InBody error was not quantified in this repo |
| Steps (NEAT) | Excluded from EEE by definition. If the 10,000+ steps were counted as exercise, EA would fall about 5-6 units. Rough assumption: 10,000 steps ≈ 4.5-5 miles of walking at about 80 kcal/mile net (Wilkin 2012 walking, scaled to 82.1 kg, minus resting cost). The step-to-mile conversion was not verified |

### Inferences

- EA and energy balance tell different stories here. Energy balance is about −350 to −500 kcal/day in 3-day weeks, because maintenance (2,850-3,000) includes the steps. EA stays in the 30s because steps are not counted as exercise.
- 3-day vs 5-6-day weeks: the 5-6-day week has about twice the lifting EEE, but the 200 kcal/day extra intake more than covers it. Mean weekly EA is about the same (34-36).
- The add-back rule is what keeps run days safe. Without it, each mile lowers that day's EA by about 1.6-1.7 units (108-116 kcal net ÷ 68.5). With it, the gap is about 0.1-0.2 units per mile.
- Is a 300-400 kcal/day deficit plus running a REDs risk? On these estimates, no: the lowest planned days (27-33) are above the male range of concern (9-25), and the only day that falls into it is a long run or race without the extra food. Risk would rise if the deficit grew, mileage rose (for example, half-marathon training), carbohydrate fell below about 3 g/kg, weight loss ran faster than about 1% of bodyweight a week, or CAT2 symptoms appeared.

### Gaps

- No measured EEE for this lifter (no heart-rate or wearable data in the repo).
- The 100 kcal/mile figure is now checked against MET and laboratory data (it runs 10-25% low), but those data are population averages.

## KQ3. Carbohydrate for runners who lift

### Takeaway

The AND/DC/ACSM stand sets daily carbohydrate by training load: 3-5 g/kg for light training, 5-7 for about 1 h a day, 6-10 for 1-3 h a day. For events under 90 min it advises 7-12 g/kg in the 24 h before, 1-4 g/kg 1-4 h before exercise longer than 60 min, small amounts or a mouth rinse for 45-75 min of hard exercise, and 30-60 g/h for 1-2.5 h. Fast refuelling (1-1.2 g/kg/h) is only needed when the next hard session is under 8 h away. For a 60-90 min race, raising glycogen above normal does not help; starting with normal stores does. For lifting, carbohydrate makes little difference in a fed state at up to about 10 sets per muscle, but it improved session volume after an overnight fast (8 h or more) and in sessions longer than 45 min. There is no direct evidence that carbohydrate, at matched energy and protein and outside ketogenic diets, changes hypertrophy.

  - Protein after key sessions: 0.25-0.3 g/kg (about 21-25 g here); protein needs 1.2-2.0 g/kg/day, possibly higher during energy restriction.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Burke, Hawley, Wong & Jeukendrup 2011**, *J Sports Sci* 29(Suppl 1):S17-S27, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21660838/. Abstract read. In sustained high-intensity exercise of about 1 h, small amounts of carbohydrate, even mouth-rinsing, help through effects on the brain. 30-60 g/h for longer events; up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5 h.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Jeukendrup 2014**, *Sports Med* 44(Suppl 1):S25-S33, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24791914/. Abstract read. About 1 h: mouth rinse or small amounts can help. Up to about 60 g/h for 2-3 h; about 90 g/h for ultra-endurance, from mixed sugars. Lower when absolute intensity is low. The advice does not scale with body weight.
- **[Evidence] Burke, Jeukendrup, Jones & Mooses 2019**, *Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab* 29(2):117-129, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30747558/. Abstract read. For distance running: carbohydrate-rich eating in the hours or days before a race, to store enough glycogen for the event; small amounts or a mouth rinse in shorter events, up to 75-90 g/h in the longest. A race plan should be personal and practised, balancing benefit against gut discomfort.
- **[Evidence, foundational] Hawley, Schabort, Noakes & Dennis 1997**, *Sports Med* 24(2):73-81, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9291549/. Abstract read. No benefit from raising starting muscle glycogen above normal for moderate-intensity running or cycling of 60-90 min; substantial glycogen remains at the end. Above 90 min, supercompensation delays fatigue by about 20%; in time trials, high-carbohydrate diets improved performance 2-3%.

**Carbohydrate and lifting (S, H)**

- **[Evidence] Henselmans, Bjørnsen, Hedderman & Vårvik 2022**, *Nutrients* 14(4):856, systematic review of 49 studies, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35215506/. Abstract read.
  - Acute: more carbohydrate did not improve performance in 13 studies and improved it in 6, mainly with fasted control groups and workouts of more than 10 sets per muscle group. No dose-response.
  - After glycogen depletion: benefit in 3 studies vs placebo (especially with two sessions a day), none against isocaloric controls.
  - Short term (2-7 days): no benefit in any of 7 studies.
  - Long term: strength and performance changes were not affected by carbohydrate in 15 studies; one favoured more and one favoured less carbohydrate.
  - Conclusion: carbohydrate "is unlikely to" affect strength training performance in a fed state at up to 10 sets per muscle group.
- **[Evidence] King, Helms, Zinn & Jukic 2022**, *Sports Med* 52(11):2691-2712, systematic review and meta-analysis, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35809162/. Abstract read. 21 crossover studies, 226 participants. Carbohydrate vs placebo or water: total session volume SMD 0.61. Subgroups: sessions longer than 45 min SMD 1.02; after a fast of 8 h or more SMD 0.39. The number of maximal-effort sets moderated the effect; carbohydrate dose and load did not.
- **[Evidence] Escobar, VanDusseldorp & Kerksick 2016**, *Br J Nutr* 116(12):2053-2065, review, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27993175/. Abstract read. Usual advice for resistance training is 3-7 g/kg/day, but true glycogen depletion from lifting is unlikely, and mTORC1 signalling appears unaffected by carbohydrate restriction or low glycogen. The authors question whether that intake is needed.
- **[Evidence, position stand] Aragon et al. 2026**, ISSN position stand: diets for body weight reduction, *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 23(1):2740266, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42867412/. Abstract read; full text searched. The carbohydrate-fat split "can be individualized" for weight loss, since ketogenic to low-fat diets perform similarly. Ketogenic diets "tend to decrease fat-free mass to a greater degree than higher-carbohydrate comparators", partly through glycogen and water shifts. Protein of 1.9-3.2 g/kg "can maximize lean mass retention in lean, resistance-trained individuals" in a deficit. Several authors report industry consulting.
- Repo: `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` addendum records Iraki 2019's ≥3-5 g/kg/day for bodybuilders (narrative review).

### Inferences

- The plan's carbohydrate is about 290 g in 3-day weeks (3.5 g/kg) and 325 g in 5-6-day weeks (4.0 g/kg). That sits in the "light" band and below the "moderate, about 1 h a day" band. For lifting, the Henselmans and Escobar evidence says this is enough. For the runs that matter (the race, any long or hard run), it is low, and the IOC's carbohydrate-availability point argues for raising it on those days.
- The lifter's sessions are at 7:30 am, about 60-66 min long (`data/weekly-plan.md`). That is the King 2022 case: after a fast of 8 h or more and sessions over 45 min. Eating some carbohydrate before these sessions is the one lifting-specific carbohydrate rule with evidence behind it. Dose did not moderate the effect, so a small amount works.
- A 10-mile race at the lifter's likely pace takes about 60-90 min. Hawley 1997 says normal glycogen is enough; the stand's 7-12 g/kg in the 24 h before is its route to normal stores for daily fuel needs. After weeks at 3.5 g/kg in a deficit, a day at maintenance with high carbohydrate is the practical way to start at normal.
- During the race: under about 75 min, a mouth rinse or a few sips of a sports drink; if the finish is expected beyond 75 min, 30-60 g/h. Only use products already tried in training (Burke 2019).
- After the race: the next hard session is more than 8 h away, so speedy refuelling is not needed. A normal meal with 25-40 g of protein and plenty of carbohydrate covers it.

### Gaps

- No trial isolates carbohydrate intake (outside ketogenic diets, at matched energy and protein) on directly measured hypertrophy.
- The doses used in the King 2022 studies were not extracted, so "a small amount" is not quantified from the source.
- No study of race fueling in recreational runners who also lift.

## KQ4. Dieting, performance, and whether the deficit should pause in race weeks

### Takeaway

In lifters, deficits impaired lean-mass gains but strength was not significantly different (repo: Murphy & Koehler 2022). In endurance-trained men, 14 days at an EA of about 22 lowered explosive power, then endurance. Diet-break trials show that pausing a deficit at maintenance for 1-2 weeks does not slow fat loss over the full diet: in resistance-trained adults fat loss and FFM were similar, with less hunger (ICECAP); in men with obesity, 2-week breaks gave more fat loss (MATADOR). A 1-week break at maintenance improved leg muscle endurance and mood, not strength. So eating at maintenance the day before and the day after a race (with the full add-back on race day) gives up about 700-1,000 kcal of planned deficit and has no measured downside.

### Cited findings

- **[Evidence] Murphy & Koehler 2022**, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 32(1):125-137, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/. From `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` addendum (abstract level). Lean mass ES −0.57 (p = 0.02); strength ES −0.31 (p = 0.28), not significantly different. About 500 kcal/day "prevented gains in LM".
- **[Evidence] Jurov 2022** (KQ1): explosive power fell from stage 1 (EA 22.4), endurance power next.
- **[Evidence] Gallant 2025** (KQ1): LEA associated with poorer run and endurance performance (mostly cross-sectional data).
- **[Evidence] Byrne et al. 2018**, MATADOR, *Int J Obes* 42(2):129-138, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925405/. Abstract read. 51 men with obesity (about 110 kg) randomised. 16 weeks of restriction at 67% of maintenance: continuous, or 8 × 2-week blocks alternating with 7 × 2-week blocks at energy balance (30 weeks in total). Per protocol (19 vs 17): weight loss 14.1 ± 5.6 kg intermittent vs 9.1 ± 2.9 kg continuous; fat loss 12.3 vs 8.0 kg; FFM loss similar. REE adjusted for body composition fell less with intermittent (−360 vs −749 kJ/day). One author has commercial diet-book and industry ties.
- **[Evidence] Peos et al. 2021**, ICECAP, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 53(8):1685-1698, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587549/. Abstract read. 61 resistance-trained adults (32 women; 28.7 y; 77.2 kg; 25.5% fat). 12 weeks of moderate restriction: continuous, or 4 × 3 weeks with 3 × 1-week breaks at energy balance (15 weeks in total). End fat mass 15.3 vs 18.0 kg (P = 0.321); FFM 56.7 vs 56.7 kg (P = 0.309). No differences in strength, muscular endurance, REE, leptin, testosterone, IGF-1, free T3 or ghrelin. The intermittent group reported less hunger and more satisfaction.
- **[Evidence] Peos et al. 2021b**, *PLoS One* 16(2):e0247292, pre-specified secondary analysis of ICECAP, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33630880/. Abstract read. 26 athletes, a 1-week break at predicted maintenance (extra energy as carbohydrate). Fat mass unchanged; body weight +0.6 kg; FFM +0.7 kg; REE 7,000 to 7,200 kJ/day. Leg muscle endurance rose (quadriceps work in a 25-rep set 2,530 to 2,660 J; hamstrings 1,280 to 1,380 J); arm endurance and strength did not change. Less hunger and irritability; more alertness. Pre-post design without a control group; the FFM gain likely includes glycogen and water.
- **[Evidence] Campbell et al. 2020**, *J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 5(1):19, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33467235/. Abstract read. 27 resistance-trained men and women, 7 weeks at about a 25% deficit: continuous, or 5 days of restriction plus 2 high-carbohydrate refeed days each week. Fat loss similar. FFM change −0.4 vs −1.3 kg and RMR change −38 vs −78 kcal, favouring the refeed group. One author advises a supplement company.
- **[Evidence] Peos, Brown, Vorland, Allison & Sainsbury 2020**, comment, *J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 5(4):85, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33467300/. Title and abstract stub read. On reanalysis of Campbell's data, "only dry fat-free mass was different between groups". The full argument was not read.
- **[Evidence] Poon et al. 2025**, *Nutr Rev* 83(1):59-71, systematic review and meta-analysis, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38193357/. Abstract read; numbers below from the ISSN 2026 stand's summary (secondary). 12 RCTs, 881 participants, 4-52 weeks, breaks of 1 day to 6 weeks. Body composition improved with both approaches, with no between-group difference. RMR fell less with breaks: mean difference −47.3 kcal/day overall, −73 in people with overweight or obesity and −10.8 in resistance-trained people.
- **[Practitioner] Ernat 2024**, "All about diet breaks & refeeds", Renaissance Periodization, https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/all-about-diet-breaks-refeeds (read). Diet break: 1-2 weeks at or near maintenance during long fat-loss phases. Refeed: 1-3 days of extra calories, mostly carbohydrate, within the deficit, for key workouts or in moderate-to-severe deficits. Claims breaks restore leptin, thyroid hormones and testosterone, and that MATADOR showed better muscle retention; see Conflicts.

### Inferences

- Should the deficit pause in race weeks? Yes, briefly. The cost is small: 2 days at maintenance (2,850-3,000 instead of 2,500) give up about 700-1,000 kcal of planned deficit, about 0.2-0.3 lb of fat at the repo's 3,500 kcal/lb rule. The diet-break trials found no penalty to final fat loss from far longer pauses. The benefits that have some support are starting the race with normal glycogen (Hawley 1997), better leg muscle endurance and mood after a maintenance week (Peos 2021b), and avoiding the low-EA, low-carbohydrate combination on the most demanding days (IOC 2023).
- High-mileage weeks: the lifter runs under 10 miles a week, so this does not arise yet. If weekly mileage rises well past that, run add-backs grow, and the 5-6-day week's 2,700 kcal plus add-back is the floor.
- Planned diet breaks: optional. A 1-2-week maintenance phase after 8-12 weeks of deficit, or when hunger threatens adherence, fits the ICECAP data. Its main measured benefit is appetite and adherence, not extra fat loss or hormones.
- Week types: a natural place for a maintenance week is a 5-6-day block, where the plan is already closest to maintenance and training demand is highest. In 3-day weeks the deficit can run as planned.

### Gaps

- No trial of race-week deficit pauses in recreational runners.
- No diet-break trial in lean men who lift and run.
- Peos 2020's reanalysis of Campbell 2020 was not read in full.

### Monitoring and when to see a professional

- Weekly: bodyweight trend (loss above about 1% of bodyweight a week is a flag), lifts, and the check-in items.
- Watch for the CAT2 potential indicators that a person can notice: lower libido, fewer morning erections, unusual fatigue, frequent illness, dizziness on standing, sleep problems, mood changes, falling performance at the same RPE, and bone pain that worsens with running.
- If two or more persist for more than 2-4 weeks, see a doctor. The IOC tool depends on blood tests (morning testosterone, T3) and a clinician ruling out other causes. Bone pain on running should be checked promptly, since bone stress injuries are a primary REDs indicator.
- Anyone with a history of disordered eating should plan a deficit with a registered dietitian (`guide/06` §8 already says this).
- This note is general training guidance, not medical advice.

## Conflicts

- **Male thresholds: 30 vs 9-25.** The 30 kcal/kg FFM figure still appears in the 2016 stand and in many tools. The IOC 2023 consensus says the male level "appears to be lower (eg, ~9 to 25)". Jurov 2022 proposes 9-25 (mean about 17). Both are kept: 30 is the female-derived reference, 9-25 the male estimate.
- **Testosterone under LEA.** Koehler 2016: unchanged at 15 for 4 days. Kojima 2020 (secondary): fell at about 19 for 3 days. Jurov 2022: lower quartile over 14-day stages. Different populations, durations and carbohydrate intakes.
- **Bone markers in men.** Papageorgiou 2017 (secondary): unchanged at 15 for 5 days. Murphy 2021: formation down, resorption up at 15 for 5 days. Not resolved; Murphy's participants also exercised to set EEE, which may matter.
- **Campbell 2020 refeeds.** The paper reports better FFM retention with refeeds. Peos et al.'s reanalysis says only dry FFM differed. The 2026 ISSN stand repeats the original FFM numbers without the reanalysis. Both kept; treat the FFM benefit as unconfirmed.
- **Diet breaks and fat loss.** MATADOR (men with obesity, 2-week blocks): more fat loss with breaks. ICECAP (resistance-trained, 1-week breaks): no difference. Poon 2025: no difference in body composition across 12 RCTs, with a small RMR benefit. For this lifter, ICECAP and Poon are the closer evidence.
- **Practitioner claims vs trials.** The RP article says breaks restore leptin, thyroid hormones and testosterone, and that MATADOR showed better muscle retention. ICECAP found no hormone differences between groups, and MATADOR's abstract reports similar FFM loss. The trials are preferred.
- **Pre-race carbohydrate: 7-12 g/kg vs "normal is enough".** The 2016 stand advises 7-12 g/kg in the 24 h before events under 90 min. Hawley 1997 found no benefit of raising glycogen above normal for 60-90 min running. The rule above takes the middle (5-6 g/kg at maintenance) because the lifter starts from weeks of 3.5 g/kg in a deficit.
- **Carbohydrate and lifting: King vs Henselmans.** King 2022 pooled a benefit (SMD 0.61); Henselmans 2022 counted mostly null studies. They agree on where the benefit sits (fasted, longer and higher-volume sessions), so both support the same rule.
