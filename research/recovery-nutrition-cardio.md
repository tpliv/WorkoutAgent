# Recovery, Fatigue Management, Cardio Integration, and Nutrition Fundamentals for a Resistance-Training Program

Research notes compiled 2026-10-21. Scope: the factors outside lifting sessions that determine whether a program works, for a lifter alternating between 3-day and 5-6-day training weeks. Method note: full-text fetches of primary papers were blocked in this environment (DNS/proxy failures for PMC, PubMed, Springer, Wiley, JISSN, Stronger By Science), so most figures below come from abstracts, repository records, and reputable secondary summaries (Examine, Stronger By Science, Biolayne REPS, MySportScience) surfaced in search. Where only a secondary source could be found, that is flagged. The web-search budget was exhausted before every sub-question could be run down; unanswered items are listed under Gaps.

## Key Question 1: Recovery time between sessions for the same muscle group (24-72 h), and training the same muscle on consecutive days in a 6-day week

### Takeaway
Direct recovery studies suggest performance in a trained muscle is typically still depressed at 24 h, mostly restored by 48 h, and fully restored (sometimes supercompensated) by 72 h after a hard, to-failure session; training short of failure markedly shortens that window. Frequency meta-analyses show that when weekly volume is equated, how you split it across the week (including 3 vs 6 sessions) does not meaningfully change hypertrophy or strength, so a 6-day week should be built by spreading the same weekly volume thinner per session, not by adding full-fatigue sessions on consecutive days.

### Cited Findings
- In 14 trained males doing sets of 8-10RM to concentric failure, volume load decreased when the next session came 24 h later; first-set volume load remained decreased at 48 h; jump height and isometric strength were increased at 72 h. The authors concluded at least 48 h was needed to re-establish performance. — [PubMed 32594858 via nufind record](https://nufind.nu.edu.sa/EdsRecord/cmedm,32594858)
- Pareja-Blanco et al. (2017), 10 trained men: training to failure on every set, even with total volume matched, produced a significantly greater acute decline in countermovement jump and bar velocity, and recovery from non-failure protocols was significantly faster between 24 and 48 h. Conclusion: "RT leading to failure considerably increases the time needed for the recovery of neuromuscular function and metabolic and hormonal homeostasis." — [Pareja-Blanco et al. 2017, Eur J Appl Physiol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/)
- In well-trained males performing sets to failure at 80% 1RM, the time course of recovery (measured out to 96 h) was similar for back squat, bench press, and deadlift. — [Belcher et al. 2019, PubMed 30779596](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30779596/)
- Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger (2019) meta-analysis (25 studies, 836 participants, 6-30 weeks): "resistance training frequency does not significantly or meaningfully impact muscle hypertrophy when volume is equated"; a significant effect favoring higher frequency appeared only when volume was not equated. — [Menno Henselmans summary of Schoenfeld et al. 2019](https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/); [Frontiers in Physiology 2021 review citing it](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.789403/pdf)
- A volume-equated study in resistance-trained men found training 3 vs 6 times per week produced similar strength gains over 6 weeks, with comparable hypertrophy expected. — [Grgic et al. 2018, ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jozo-Grgic/publication/327435932_Resistance_Training_Frequencies_of_3_and_6_Times_Per_Week_Produce_Similar_Muscular_Adaptations_in_Resistance-Trained_Men/links/5bd26d6892851c6b278f311d/Resistance-Training-Frequencies-of-3-and-6-Times-Per-Week-Produce-Similar-Muscular-Adaptations-in-Resistance-Trained-Men.pdf)
- A Grgic, Schoenfeld & Latella review of frequency found that studies using direct site-specific hypertrophy measures generally showed once-weekly training produced similar hypertrophy to 2-3 sessions per week when volume was matched. — [ECU repository record](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/5665)
- A 2024 preprint (Pelland et al., not peer reviewed) meta-regressed weekly volume and frequency across 67 studies; a Schoenfeld-coauthored analysis found hypertrophy increasing up to 10+ weekly sets per muscle group. — [Biolayne REPS summary](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-31/the-king-of-volume-metas/); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908)

### Inferences
- For the 6-day week: keep weekly volume per muscle roughly constant with the 3-day week and distribute it across 2-3 touches per muscle; avoid loading the same muscle to failure on back-to-back days, because the 24 h data show volume load is still depressed then. An upper/lower or push/pull/legs split run twice gives each muscle ~48-72 h between hard sessions, which matches the recovery data.
- If the same muscle must be trained on consecutive days (e.g., a 6-day full-body or high-frequency scheme), the failure-vs-non-failure data imply stopping 2-4 reps short of failure and lowering per-session volume so that each session is sub-maximal in fatigue cost.
- Recovery time is similar across the big three lifts, so there is no evidence basis for giving deadlifts a longer standalone recovery window than squats when loads and proximity to failure are similar; the practical driver is total systemic volume and proximity to failure.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis pooling the recovery time course by volume or exercise type was found; the figures above come from small primary studies (n = 10-14).
- A direct study of consecutive-day versus non-consecutive-day training for the same muscle (e.g., Yang et al. 2018) could not be searched before the budget was exhausted.
- Recovery time course in lifters over 40 is addressed under Key Question 7 and is itself thin.

## Key Question 2: Fatigue management - signs of under-recovery, monitoring, and overreaching vs overtraining

### Takeaway
In resistance training, short deliberate overloads produce functional overreaching (FOR) that resolves within about a week, chronic high volume/intensity can produce non-functional overreaching, and there is minimal evidence that true overtraining syndrome occurs in strength sports. The practical, evidence-supported monitors are performance (bar velocity, reps at a given load, RIR-based RPE drift), daily subjective questionnaires (mood, perceived recovery, sleep quality), and objective sleep efficiency; HRV is popular but poorly validated for resistance training.

### Cited Findings
- Bell et al. (2020) scoping review (47 studies): "short-term periods of OR achieved with either high-volume or high-intensity RT can elicit functional OR (FOR), but there is also evidence that chronic high-volume and/or intensity RT can lead to non-functional overreaching (NFOR). There is minimal evidence to suggest that true OTS has occurred in strength sports or RT." — [Bell et al. 2020, J Sports Sci, PubMed 32602418](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32602418/); [SHU repository](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/26176)
- Grandou et al. (2020) systematic review: a lack of standardisation in follow-up performance testing and diagnostic criteria prevents consistent determination of FOR, NFOR and OTS in resistance training. — [Grandou et al. 2020, Sports Med, PubMed 31820373](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31820373/)
- In weight-trained men during a high-power squat overreaching block, daily questionnaires captured changes in perceived recovery, mood and sleep; bar velocity at 70% 1RM dropped post-overreaching and recovered after 1 week. — [IJES abstract, WKU](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijesab/vol11/iss2/20)
- Mood-state questionnaires and subjective sleep complaints "are consistently described as an early marker of maladaptation to training"; sleep measures could serve as an indicator of overreaching/overtraining whether reduced sleep is cause or effect. — [Kölling et al. 2018, Can Sleep Be Used as an Indicator of Overreaching and Overtraining](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5928142/)
- 2024 PLoS ONE meta-analysis (endurance athletes): overreaching reduced objective sleep efficiency (mean difference -2.0%, 95% CI -3.2 to -0.8%) versus baseline, while subjective sleep quality showed no significant change; none of the included studies were low risk of bias. — [DOAJ record, PLoS ONE 2024](https://doaj.org/article/b30f0bc956c84d55b6b2d07e8bcb1bc7)
- Greig et al. (2020) review of 90 studies on regulating and monitoring resistance training: barbell velocity and sessional, RIR-based, and post-set Borg RPE were strongly associated with training performance; velocity- and RIR-RPE-based intensity/volume modifications are "practical, reliable and show preliminary utility," while many physiological measures are lab-restricted. — [Greig et al. 2020, J Hum Kinet](https://johk.pl/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/10078-74-2020-v74-2020-03.pdf); [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/b67c0527-5e33-45d9-886c-746d3a659ea0/full)
- HRV-guided training meta-analysis (2021, Flatt coauthor): HRV-guided training was superior for enhancing vagal HRV indices but effects on aerobic capacity and endurance performance were "consistently small but non-significant." — [Georgia Southern record](https://scholars.georgiasouthern.edu/en/publications/heart-rate-variability-guided-training-for-enhancing-cardiac-vaga/)
- A 2024 review states there is "a lack of research connecting the effective utilization of HRV to guide training, specifically with strength training"; a dissertation supervised by Esco notes HRV "has not been well-established" as an internal-load marker for resistance exercise. — [PMC11204851](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11204851); [Univ. of Alabama dissertation](https://ir.ua.edu/items/a7faddd0-c7da-487e-8612-30f22eaecdf0/full)
- Bell et al. (2022) coaches' study defines FOR as improved capability in the days and weeks after short-term increased training demand. — [SHU repository PDF](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/30288/2/fspor-04-893581.pdf)

### Inferences
- Practical red flags, in order of evidence: (1) rep performance or bar speed at a fixed load falls across two or more sessions; (2) RPE drifts up for the same prescribed load/reps; (3) worsening daily mood/perceived-recovery scores; (4) objectively worse sleep efficiency. Any two together for a week suggests the heavy (5-6 day) week has tipped past FOR and the next week should be a 3-day or deload week.
- Because resistance-training FOR resolved within about a week in the squat overreaching study, alternating a 5-6-day heavy week with a lighter 3-day week is a defensible structure, provided the heavy week's drop in performance is small and reverses in the light week.
- HRV can be logged, but decisions should be driven by performance and subjective readiness, not HRV alone.

### Gaps
- No resistance-specific numbers were found for how large a performance drop (e.g., % of 1RM or % velocity loss) distinguishes FOR from NFOR; the literature itself says diagnostic criteria are unstandardised.
- Deload-week trials (e.g., Coleman et al. 2024) and the Bell deload survey could not be searched before the budget ran out.
- No validated RPE-drift threshold was found.

## Key Question 3: Sleep - effects on strength, hypertrophy, and injury risk

### Takeaway
Sleep loss acutely reduces maximal strength only slightly (about 3%) but strength endurance substantially (about 10%), one night of total sleep deprivation cuts muscle protein synthesis by 18% and testosterone by 24%, and chronic sleep under 8 h is associated with roughly 1.7x injury risk in adolescent athletes; adult injury evidence is weaker. No study directly measures sleep dose against long-term hypertrophy.

### Cited Findings
- Craven et al. (2022) meta-analysis (77 studies): after sleep loss, "strength performance only decreased by 2.85%, on average, whereas strength endurance performance decreased by 9.85%"; the strength-endurance estimate came from only five studies. — [Stronger By Science review of the meta-analysis](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=57132); [2025 systematic review PMC12263768](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12263768/)
- 2025 systematic review (13 studies): both acute and chronic sleep deprivation significantly reduced some measures of muscle strength, power output and muscular endurance; results were mixed across studies after a single night. — [Implications of sleep loss on muscle strength, LJMU](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/27054/)
- Knowles et al. (2018), J Sci Med Sport, systematic review on sleep deprivation and restriction in resistance exercise; Deakin/Gentil summaries note alterations in muscle protein synthesis, hormonal, inflammatory and pain responses. — [Knowles et al. 2018 PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Inadequate%20sleep%20and%20muscle%20strength%20-%20Implications%20for%20resistance%20training.pdf)
- Lamon et al. (2021), Physiological Reports, randomized crossover in 13 young adults: one night of total sleep deprivation reduced muscle protein synthesis by 18% (0.072 vs 0.059 %/h, p = .040), increased plasma cortisol 21% and decreased testosterone 24%; "a single night of total sleep deprivation is sufficient to induce anabolic resistance and a procatabolic environment." — [Lamon et al. 2021, PubMed 33400856](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33400856/); [Examine summary](https://examine.com/summaries/study/y1wDA0/)
- Milewski et al. (2014): adolescent athletes chronically sleeping under 8 h/night had an adjusted risk ratio of 1.7 (95% CI 1.0-3.0) for injury (unadjusted 2.0); all data were self-reported. — [Systematic review table, PMC6446394](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6446394)
- A review concluded current evidence does not establish insufficient sleep as an independent risk factor for musculoskeletal injury in adults; associations are more consistent in adolescents. — [Quality in Sport review](https://apcz.umk.pl/QS/article/view/75752)

### Inferences
- The 7-9 h adult sleep target cannot be derived from these studies directly, but the 18% MPS drop after one bad night and the strength-endurance decrement argue for protecting sleep most on and after the heaviest sessions of a 5-6-day week.
- Because maximal strength is relatively robust to one night of poor sleep, a heavy low-rep session is a better choice than a high-rep volume session after a short night.

### Gaps
- No trial of sleep extension on hypertrophy outcomes was found (a search for Jåbekk 2020 was not run).
- No study linking habitual sleep duration to long-term strength or hypertrophy gains in trained adults was found.
- Adult lifter injury-risk data by sleep hours are essentially absent; consumer-site percentage tables (e.g., d1training.com) are unsourced and should not be used.

## Key Question 4: Nutrition for lifters - protein targets, distribution, surplus/deficit, creatine, caffeine, timing

### Takeaway
Daily protein of roughly 1.6 g/kg (upper plausible bound ~2.2 g/kg) spread over at least four feedings of ~0.4 g/kg captures nearly all the hypertrophy benefit; a 10-20% surplus (~0.25-0.5% bodyweight gain/week) supports muscle gain, while a deficit around 500 kcal/day on average holds lean mass flat and larger deficits cost lean mass but not strength. Creatine monohydrate at 3-5 g/day and caffeine at 3-6 mg/kg are the two best-supported ergogenic aids; protein timing around training matters far less than total intake.

### Cited Findings
- Morton et al. (2018), BJSM, 49 studies, 1,863 participants: protein supplementation significantly increased strength, fat-free mass and muscle size with resistance training; supplementation beyond a total intake of 1.62 g/kg/day produced no further FFM gain; the effect was smaller with age and larger in trained individuals. — [Nutrition-evidence record of Morton et al. 2018](https://nutrition-evidence.com/article/280995/a-systematic-review-meta-analysis-and-meta-regression-of-the-effect-of-protein-supplementation-on-resistance-training-induced-gains-in-muscle-mass-and-strength-in-healthy-adults)
- The 1.62 g/kg breakpoint was not itself statistically significant (p = 0.079) and its confidence interval spanned 1.03-2.20 g/kg, so it is an imprecise estimate rather than a hard ceiling. — [Stronger By Science critique](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=61316)
- Nunes et al. (2022), J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle (62 studies for LBM): overall LBM effect SMD 0.22 (95% CI 0.14-0.30, moderate evidence); effect on LBM was significant in adults <65 doing resistance exercise at >=1.6 g/kg/day and in adults >=65 at 1.2-1.59 g/kg/day. — [PMC8978023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8978023); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/7109fcc11d7d49cdbcd3be7f793e1c2b)
- Tagawa et al. (2020), Nutrition Reviews, 105 articles, 5,411 subjects: LBM improved with about 5 g/day of supplementary protein, increasing further above 50 g/day supplementary protein. — [medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.02.21.20026252v1)
- Schoenfeld & Aragon (2018), JISSN: "to maximize anabolism one should consume protein at a target intake of 0.4 g/kg/meal across a minimum of four meals" to reach at least 1.6 g/kg/day; the upper 2.2 g/kg/day over four meals implies up to 0.55 g/kg/meal. — [PMC5828430](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430/)
- ISSN nutrient-timing position stand (2017): casein ~30-40 g before sleep acutely increases overnight MPS and metabolic rate without affecting lipolysis; timing of caffeine, creatine and bicarbonate may affect acute and chronic responses. — [Kerksick et al. 2017, PMC5596471](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596471)
- Schoenfeld, Aragon & Krieger (2013) protein-timing meta-analysis: total protein intake was the strongest predictor of gains regardless of timing; a small, significant hypertrophy effect for protein within 1 h post-exercise vs delaying >=2 h appeared before adjustment for total protein. The group's follow-up RCT found pre- vs post-exercise protein produced similar adaptations. — [PMC3879660](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3879660); [Schoenfeld et al. 2017, PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/2825); [Schoenfeld blog commentary](https://www.lookgreatnaked.com/blog/our-meta-analysis-of-protein-timing-thoughts-and-perspectives/)
- The anabolic window may extend as wide as 5-6 h after exercise depending on pre-workout meal timing. — [Aragon & Schoenfeld 2013, PMC3577439 via PeerJ citation](https://peerj.com/articles/2825)
- Iraki et al. (2019), Sports (MDPI), off-season natural bodybuilding review: "A hyper-energetic diet (~10-20%) should be consumed with a target weight gain of ~0.25-0.5% of bodyweight/week for novice/intermediate bodybuilders." — [PMC6680710](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/); Examine adds advanced lifters should aim for the lower end of that range — [Examine FAQ](https://examine.com/faq/should-the-magnitude-of-the-energy-surplus-depend-on-the-training-status-of-the-individual)
- Slater et al. (2019), Frontiers in Nutrition, call a surplus of 1,500-2,000 kJ/day (~358-478 kcal) conservative; data on trained individuals suggest a deficit would most likely impair gains, with recomposition most likely in beginners or overweight trainees. — [Slater et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/pdf); [BarBend summary](https://barbend.com/how-many-calories-to-eat-bulking-season)
- Murphy & Koehler (2021/2022), Scand J Med Sci Sports meta-analysis: energy deficiency impaired lean-mass gains but not strength; the deficit scaled linearly with lean-mass change, and on average a 500 kcal/day deficit produced neither loss nor gain of lean mass. — [MySportScience author write-up](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/can-i-gain-muscle-and-lose-weight-at-the-same-time); [Examine FAQ](https://examine.com/conditions/muscle-size-and-strength/faq/how-does-an-energy-deficit-affect-muscle-size-and-strength); [Biolayne REPS](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-7/gains-during-a-calorie-deficit-fact-or-fiction/)
- ISSN caffeine position stand (Guest et al. 2021): caffeine "has consistently been shown to improve exercise performance when consumed in doses of 3-6 mg/kg body mass." — [PMC7777221](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7777221/); [JISSN](https://jissn.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4)
- ISSN creatine position stand (Kreider et al. 2017, JISSN 14:18) states creatine is safe at up to 30 g/day for 5 years; secondary sources summarise its dosing as 3-5 g/day maintenance, or 20 g/day split in four doses for 5-7 days to load, with 3-5 g/day reaching saturation in about 3-4 weeks. — [Exercise & Sport Nutrition Lab summary of the stand](https://exerciseandsportnutritionlab.com/new-issn-position-stand-on-creatine-supplementation/); [MD Saude dosing summary](https://www.mdsaude.com/en/nutrition/creatine-monohydrate/)

### Inferences
- A working target for this lifter: 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day protein (higher end in a deficit or on the heavy week), 4+ feedings of ~0.4 g/kg each, with the pre- and post-training meals within roughly 3-4 h of each other covering the "window."
- During the 5-6-day weeks, hold calories at maintenance or a small surplus (10-20%); run deficits during the lighter 3-day weeks if fat loss is the goal, keeping the deficit near or under ~500 kcal/day to protect lean mass.
- Creatine 3-5 g/day, taken daily regardless of training, and caffeine 3-6 mg/kg before the heaviest sessions are the only supplements with position-stand-level support found here.

### Gaps
- The primary text of the ISSN creatine stand could not be fetched, so the 3-5 g/day figure is cited to secondary summaries.
- Caffeine strength/power effect sizes (Grgic et al. 2018) and pre-exercise timing (~60 min) could not be searched.
- Protein needs in a deficit for lean, trained lifters (Helms et al. 2014, 2.3-3.1 g/kg FFM) could not be searched and should be verified.
- Daily carbohydrate targets by training volume from the ISSN nutrient-timing stand were not retrievable from the abstract-level content available.

## Key Question 5: Concurrent training - adding cardio without hurting gains, weekly doses, and scheduling in 3-day vs 6-day weeks

### Takeaway
The 2022 Schumann et al. meta-analysis found no interference with maximal strength or whole-muscle hypertrophy, with a small-to-moderate decrement only in explosive strength (SMD -0.28) that was worse when cardio and lifting were in the same session; interference grows with cardio frequency and duration, running appears more costly than cycling at the fibre level, and lifting before cardio in the same session preserves lower-body strength (+3.96 kg 1RM). Health guidelines call for 150-300 min moderate or 75-150 min vigorous aerobic activity per week.

### Cited Findings
- Schumann et al. (2022), Sports Medicine 52:601-612: concurrent aerobic and strength training "does not compromise muscle hypertrophy and maximal strength development"; explosive strength pooled SMD -0.28 (95% CI -0.48 to -0.08, p = 0.007); attenuation was more pronounced when both were performed in the same session (p = 0.043) than when separated by at least 3 h (p > 0.05); training frequency (>5 vs <5 weekly sessions) did not moderate effects; results held regardless of cycling vs running. — [Examine summary of Schumann et al. 2022](https://examine.com/research-feed/study/1PQaW0); [JCU repository full text](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/37)
- Secondary blog summaries report 43 studies pooled with SMD -0.06 for maximal strength and -0.01 for hypertrophy; these counts were not verified against the paper. — [train.fitness summary](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/mix-strength-and-cardio-without-losing)
- Lundberg et al. (2022), Sports Medicine, 15 studies, fibre-level: concurrent training had a small negative effect on fibre hypertrophy (SMD -0.23, 95% CI -0.46 to -0.00, p = 0.050); type I fibre hypertrophy was impaired when aerobic training was running but not cycling (SMD -0.81, 95% CI -1.26 to -0.36); frequency, training status, and same-session order showed no differences. — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4077385?lng=en); [Biolayne REPS](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-8/concurrent-training-cardio-will-probably-not-kill-your-gains/)
- Wilson et al. (2012), JSCR, 21 studies, 422 effect sizes: significant negative correlations between endurance-training frequency (r about -0.26 to -0.35) and duration (r about -0.29 to -0.75) and gains in hypertrophy, strength and power; running but not cycling produced significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength; interference "is a factor of the modality, frequency, and duration of the endurance training selected." — [Wilson et al. 2012 via Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/103997237/Concurrent_Training); [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/34457)
- Murlasits, Kneffel & Thalib (2018), J Sports Sci 36:1212-1219: lower-body 1RM gains were higher when strength preceded endurance in the same session, pooled difference 3.96 kg (95% CI 0.81-7.10 kg); VO2max was unaffected by order (0.39 ml/kg/min, 95% CI -1.03 to 1.81). — [PubMed 28783467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/); [Griffith repository](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/1b3fa0b8-0d07-4b68-8038-4c37635a5ea1)
- A 2018 JSSM review notes that studies comparing opposite intra-session orders showed no difference in maximal strength or hypertrophy after 12 and 24 weeks when aerobic training was moderate intensity, and frames order as a tool to maintain acute strength-session volume. — [Methenitis 2018, JSSM 17:623](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-17-623.xml-Fulltext)
- WHO 2020 guidelines: all adults should do 150-300 min of moderate-intensity, or 75-150 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity per week, or an equivalent combination, plus regular muscle-strengthening activity; reducing sedentary time is recommended but no threshold could be quantified. — [Bull et al. 2020, BJSM, PMC7719906](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7719906/)

### Inferences
- Health dose for this lifter: 150 min/week moderate (e.g., 3 x 50 min or 5 x 30 min) or 75 min/week vigorous, which the lifting already partly complements on the strength side.
- 3-day lifting week: there are 4 non-lifting days, so put cardio on separate days (24 h separation), preferring cycling, incline walking or rowing over running if hypertrophy of the legs is a priority; this is also the week to accumulate most of the weekly cardio minutes.
- 5-6-day lifting week: there is little room for separate cardio days, so (a) cut cardio to the minimum health floor (~75 min vigorous or ~150 min moderate, or a mix), (b) do it after lifting, never before, on upper-body days, or at least 3 h apart from a lower-body session, and (c) keep sessions short (20-30 min) because duration correlates most strongly with interference (r up to -0.75).
- Power/explosiveness is the only outcome with a documented cost; a lifter who does not train for power can largely ignore the interference effect at these doses.

### Gaps
- The WHO "muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days/week" wording could not be confirmed from the retrieved text (search budget exhausted); the aerobic figures are confirmed.
- Specific minimum separation data (Robineau et al. 2016: 0 vs 6 vs 24 h) could not be searched; Schumann's cut-point of ">=3 h apart" is the best available.
- ACSM-specific recommendations were not retrieved separately; WHO figures are used.

## Key Question 6: Active recovery, mobility and injury prevention - what has evidence and what does not

### Takeaway
Strength training itself is the best-supported injury-prevention tool (RR ~0.32), stretching does not reduce injury rates (RR 0.96), massage is the most effective modality for soreness and perceived fatigue, foam rolling gives small flexibility and soreness benefits with negligible effects on strength, and post-lifting cold-water immersion likely blunts hypertrophy slightly and should be avoided after hypertrophy-focused sessions.

### Cited Findings
- Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen (2014), BJSM, 25 RCTs, ~26,600 participants, 3,464 injuries: strength training RR 0.315 (95% CI 0.207-0.480), "reduced sports injuries to less than 1/3 and overuse injuries could be almost halved"; proprioception training RR 0.550; multi-component programmes RR 0.655; stretching RR 0.963 (0.846-1.095), the only measure without consistently favourable results. — [Lauersen et al. 2014, BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/11/871)
- Lauersen et al. (2018), BJSM, strength-training-only follow-up: pooled RR 0.338 across six trials and 7,738 participants, no publication bias. — [NIH Brage record](https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/handle/11250/2579164?show=full)
- Dupuy et al. (2018), Frontiers in Physiology, systematic review and meta-analysis of post-exercise recovery techniques (99 studies per secondary summary; massage, CWI, compression, active recovery, stretching, cryotherapy, contrast water, electrostimulation): "massage seems to be the most effective method for reducing DOMS and perceived fatigue"; secondary summaries report CWI second and active recovery only a small effect on soreness. — [Dupuy et al. 2018, Frontiers](https://frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00403/text); [PubMed 29755363](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29755363/)
- Wiewelhove et al. (2019) foam-rolling meta-analysis, 21 studies: pre-exercise rolling improved sprint ~0.7% and flexibility ~4% with negligible effects on jump and strength; post-exercise rolling slightly attenuated losses in sprint and strength and reduced muscle pain perception; overall effects "rather minor and partly negligible," justifying use as a warm-up tool more than a recovery tool. — [Wiewelhove et al. 2019, PMC6465761](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6465761/)
- Piñero et al. (2024), Eur J Sport Sci, 8 studies, 116 participants (ages 20-26, 2 women): resistance training alone produced greater relative hypertrophy than CWI + RT, cSMD0.5 = -0.22 (95% CrI -0.47 to 0.04), described as likely greater than zero but small and uncertain. — [Piñero et al. 2024, PMC11235606](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11235606/); [Examine summary](https://examine.com/research-feed/study/0mjwe9/)

### Inferences
- Priorities for this program: keep the lifting itself balanced (it is the injury-prevention intervention), add short proprioception/balance work if lower-body injury history exists, and treat stretching as a mobility or comfort tool rather than injury prevention.
- Foam rolling 1-2 min per muscle in the warm-up is cheap and mildly useful for range of motion; as recovery, massage beats rolling but neither changes long-term adaptation.
- Avoid cold plunges within several hours after hypertrophy sessions; if used for comfort, reserve them for the lighter 3-day week or after cardio-only days.

### Gaps
- Acute effects of static stretching on strength/power during warm-up (Behm et al. 2016) and warm-up injury-prevention evidence (Fradkin et al. 2010) could not be searched.
- A dedicated meta-analysis on active recovery (light cardio between sessions) was not retrieved; only the Dupuy secondary summary addresses it.
- CWI effects on strength (as opposed to hypertrophy) were not examined.

## Key Question 7: Age considerations for lifters in their 30s-50s

### Takeaway
Direct evidence for 30-50-year-olds is thin; the best available data show that advancing age is not associated with greater exercise-induced muscle damage, and a middle-aged (~44 y) group recovered no differently from ~26-year-olds after a damaging bout, though some older (60+) groups show slower strength recovery. Protein needs rise modestly with age (older adults respond at 1.2-1.6 g/kg/day, with per-meal thresholds near 0.4-0.6 g/kg), and strength training is itself protective against injury at any age.

### Cited Findings
- A 2025 meta-analysis of 36 studies comparing older and younger adults: strength change after damaging exercise did not differ by age; younger adults actually showed more soreness and higher creatine kinase at 24 h; "advancing age is not associated with greater symptoms of EIMD." — [Cardiff Met record](https://pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk/en/publications/advancing-age-is-not-associated-with-greater-exercise-induced-mus/)
- Frontiers in Physiology (2022): 14 active men aged ~26 and 14 aged ~44 performed a 60-min downhill run; there were no statistical or meaningful differences between groups in any recovery outcome. — [Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.916924/full)
- Fernandes et al. review of aging and recovery from resistance-exercise damage: of 12 comparisons, four found worse symptoms in older adults, two found the opposite, six found no difference; authors concluded resistance training can be practiced in older populations without concern for impaired recovery. — [Newcastle repository](https://openresearch.newcastle.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Aging_and_recovery_after_resistance-exercise-induced_muscle_damage_current_evidence_and_implications_for_future_research/29013095)
- Hayes et al. (2023), Sports Medicine - Open, scoping review of recovery in adults >=65: notes that little is known and that "the time course of the exercise recovery process could be prolonged with age." — [Hayes et al. 2023, PMC10317890](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10317890/); [protocol PMC8804680](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8804680/)
- Older men (~64 y) showed slower strength recovery but less soreness than ~25-year-olds after fast eccentric elbow-flexor contractions. — [PubMed 18584196](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18584196/)
- Moore et al. (2015): MPS plateaued at ~0.40 g/kg per meal in older men vs 0.24 g/kg in young men, and some older men may need up to ~0.60 g/kg. — [summarised in Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018, PMC5828430](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430/)
- Nunes et al. (2022): LBM effect significant at 1.2-1.59 g/kg/day in adults >=65 and >=1.6 g/kg/day in younger adults doing resistance exercise. — [PMC8978023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8978023)
- Morton et al. (2018): the effect of protein supplementation on FFM gains "was reduced with increasing age." — [Nutrition-evidence record](https://nutrition-evidence.com/article/280995/a-systematic-review-meta-analysis-and-meta-regression-of-the-effect-of-protein-supplementation-on-resistance-training-induced-gains-in-muscle-mass-and-strength-in-healthy-adults)
- Lauersen et al. (2014): strength training reduces overuse injuries by almost half in the pooled RCTs. — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/11/871)

### Inferences
- A lifter in the 30s-50s should not assume he needs a longer recovery window than the 48-72 h data above; the evidence does not show greater muscle damage in middle age. Where it exists, the age effect is slower strength recovery, which argues for monitoring performance (Key Question 2) rather than blanket extra rest days.
- Protein toward the upper end (~2.0 g/kg/day, ~0.4-0.5 g/kg per meal) is a low-risk hedge against the age-related blunting of the protein response.
- Joint care is best addressed by the injury-prevention evidence (balanced strength work, proprioception) rather than stretching.

### Gaps
- No study specific to 30-50-year-old lifters' recovery time course, warm-up modifications, or joint-care practices was found; recommendations in this age band are extrapolated from young and 60+ data.
- Warm-up evidence (duration, specific vs general warm-up, effects on injury) could not be searched before the budget was exhausted.
- Masters-athlete-specific guidance from Stronger By Science / Barbell Medicine could not be retrieved.

### Corrections

1. Morán-Navarro et al. 2017, not Pareja-Blanco et al. 2017, wrote the failure vs non-failure recovery study (PubMed 28965198).
2. The 24/48/72 h study is Goulart et al. 2021, Eur J Sport Sci. It is lower body only (squat, leg press).
3. The 3 vs 6 sessions/week trial is Saric et al. 2019, J Strength Cond Res 33(7S):S122, not "Grgic et al. 2018". It measured hypertrophy directly; growth was similar except at the elbow flexors (only the 3x group grew).
4. Pelland et al. is no longer a preprint. It is Sports Med 2026;56:481-505. Strength (not hypertrophy) rose with frequency.
5. The J Hum Kinet 2020 monitoring review of 90 studies is by Helms et al., not Greig et al.
6. The "Kölling et al. 2018" sleep-as-indicator paper is Lastella et al. 2018, Front Physiol 9:436, an opinion article.
7. In the squat overreaching abstract (Fry et al. 2014, conference abstract), mood (POMS) did **not** change. The overload was 15 sessions in 7.5 days, far harsher than a 5-6-day lifting week.
8. Craven 2022 pooled 69 publications (77 participant groups), not 77 studies. Sleep loss was acute, ≤6 h.
9. Lamon 2021 tested total sleep deprivation (a night with no sleep), not a short night.
10. Tagawa et al.: published in Nutr Rev 2021, with 5,402 participants. The "~5 g/day, further above 50 g/day" finding is not in the published paper. Use the per-0.1 g/kg result instead.

### New evidence (2023-2026, peer reviewed)

- **Trommelen et al. 2023, Cell Rep Med 4:101324** (PMC10772463). 36 recreationally active young men, after whole-body resistance exercise. 100 g of milk protein produced a larger and longer (>12 h) anabolic response than 25 g, with negligible extra amino-acid oxidation. This is an acute tracer study, not a hypertrophy trial; one author is employed by FrieslandCampina. It weakens a per-meal "ceiling" of 0.4-0.55 g/kg. Per-meal spread looks like a convenience more than a requirement; total daily protein remains the main lever. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38118410/
- **Huiberts, Wüst & van der Zwaard 2024, Sports Med 54:485-503** (PMC10933151). 59 studies, 1,346 adults aged 18-50. Concurrent training blunted **lower-body strength in males**: SMD -0.43 (-0.64 to -0.22). No effect in females. Hypertrophy data were insufficient. This qualifies Schumann 2022 for a male lifter: the cost is not limited to explosive strength. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37847373/
- **Held et al. 2026, Sports Med 56:1489-1512.** Umbrella review: 17 meta-analyses, 144 studies, 1,492 participants. Concurrent vs lifting alone: strength, power and hypertrophy were comparable, and aerobic capacity was better (SMD 0.77). Sequence was not significant, but trends favoured lifting first for strength (SMD 1.69). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41762427/
- **Burke et al. 2023, Nutrients 15:2116** (creatine, H). 10 studies with direct imaging. Very small hypertrophy effect: 0.11 (95% CrI -0.02 to 0.25); muscle thickness +0.10-0.16 cm. Several authors have creatine-industry ties. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432300/
- **Wang et al. 2024, Nutrients 16:3665** (creatine, S). 23 studies, adults <50, mostly male. Upper-body strength +4.43 kg, lower-body +11.35 kg vs placebo. Significant in males, not in females. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39519498/
- **Saner et al. 2020, J Physiol 598:1523-1536** (outside the 2024-2026 window but fills the chronic-restriction gap). 24 young men; 5 nights at 4 h in bed. Myofibrillar protein synthesis fell (1.24 vs 1.53 %/day). Three HIIT sessions during the restriction kept it at control levels. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32078168/
- **Helms, Aragon & Fitschen 2014, JISSN 11:20** (older, narrative; fills the weight-loss-rate gap). Contest prep: lose 0.5-1% of bodyweight per week, protein 2.3-3.1 g/kg of lean mass, fat 15-30% of calories. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/
- **Carbohydrate gap:** Iraki 2019 recommends ≥3-5 g/kg/day for bodybuilders (narrative review). Kerksick 2017's 8-12 g/kg/day is for maximising glycogen in high-volume or endurance work, not a lifter's default.
- **Caffeine safety (secondary; EFSA 2015 opinion, EFSA Journal 13(5):4102, older).** Single doses up to about 200 mg (about 3 mg/kg) and habitual intake up to 400 mg/day raise no safety concerns for healthy non-pregnant adults. 100 mg may disturb sleep in some adults. https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102 (403 when fetched)
