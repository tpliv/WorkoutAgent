# Coaching check-ins: readiness questions, day-to-day autoregulation, and supervision

Last updated: 2026-10-21

Scope: the evidence behind running the program on a coach's cadence, meaning a weekly conversation plus a written brief before each session. Three questions: what to ask at a check-in (KQ1), how the answers should change the day's loads (KQ2), and whether being coached changes the outcome at all (KQ3). Method: web search for each claim, then the abstract, a repository record, or an open full text. Where only an abstract was read, that is flagged.

## KQ1. What to ask: subjective readiness measures

### Cited findings

- **[Evidence] Saw, Main & Gastin 2016**, *Br J Sports Med* 50(5):281-291, systematic review of 56 studies that reported subjective and objective measures of athlete well-being side by side.
  - Subjective and objective measures generally did not correlate.
  - Subjective measures reflected acute and chronic training load "with superior sensitivity and consistency" than objective measures.
  - Well-being got worse with an acute rise in load and with chronic training, and better with an acute drop in load.
  - The authors say subjective measures may stand alone or join a mixed approach.
  - Sources: [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/5/281), [Deakin repository](https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Monitoring_the_athlete_training_response_Subjective_self-reported_measures_trump_commonly_used_objective_measures_A_systematic_review/20899435). Read from the abstract and repository record.
- **[Evidence, foundational, pre-2016] Hooper & Mackinnon 1995**, *Sports Med* 20(5):321-327, a leading article recommending how to monitor overtraining.
  - The "Hooper index" that later studies use comes from this line of work. It scores four self-rated items: sleep quality, stress, fatigue, and muscle soreness.
  - Full text not read. Item wording and scale are taken from later papers that use the index ([coachsci summary](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol13/hooper2.htm), [sport-iat record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4002664?lng=de)).
  - A separate Hooper, Mackinnon et al. 1995 paper (*Med Sci Sports Exerc* 27:106-112) studied swimmers. It is not the source of the index.
- **[Evidence] This repo's existing notes.** `research/recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` KQ2 ranks red flags by evidence:
  - Reps or speed at a fixed load is the strongest flag.
  - Mood, sleep, and soreness are weaker.
  - `guide/06-recovery-nutrition-cardio.md` section 2 turns these into the readiness flags the deload rule uses.

### Inferences

- A weekly check-in should ask four 1-5 items: sleep, soreness, stress, and joint pain. It should also record bodyweight. The first three are the Hooper items. Joint pain is added because `guide/03` §4.5 lists it as a deload flag.
- Fatigue is not asked separately. In this program, logged reps and RPE at a known load already measure it, and that is the stronger signal.
- Subjective scores are for trends. One bad week is a note, not a trigger. The trigger stays the existing rule: two flags that persist for a week (`guide/03` §4.5).

## KQ2. How answers change the day's loads: RPE/RIR autoregulation

### Cited findings

- **[Evidence] Zourdos et al. 2016**, *J Strength Cond Res* 30(1):267-275. Introduced the RIR-based RPE scale (RPE 10 = 0 RIR, 9 = 1, 8 = 2). Already the scale this program uses (`templates/README.md` sources; `guide/07-tracking-and-apps.md` section 1.1).
- **[Practitioner review, peer-reviewed journal] Helms, Cronin, Storey & Zourdos 2016**, *Strength Cond J* 38(4):42-49, [doi:10.1519/SSC.0000000000000218](https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000218), [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/92cefeac-7c32-44f1-bfbc-f48f4a5dd8cf/full).
  - A how-to on putting the RIR scale into a plan.
  - Load is set or adjusted set to set against the target RPE, so the prescription follows the day's capability.
  - Abstract only.
- **[Evidence] This repo.** `research/periodization-and-progression.md` covers the RPE-based loading evidence. `templates/progression-tracks.md` sections 3-5 already cap the top set by RPE.

### Inferences

- The night-before brief gives a **planned** load and a **target RPE**. On the day, the RPE cap decides. That is the existing rule, restated in the message so it is in front of the lifter.
- Low readiness answers do not cut planned loads in advance. The RPE cap already handles a bad day. Loads change ahead of time only through the existing rules: gap clock, miss and hold, stall reset, deload.

## KQ3. Does being coached matter?

### Cited findings

- **[Evidence, foundational, pre-2016] Mazzetti et al. 2000**, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 32(6):1175-1184, [doi:10.1097/00005768-200006000-00023](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-200006000-00023), [full text PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/The%20influence%20of%20direct%20supervision%20in%20resistance%20training%20%28personal%29.pdf).
  - 20 moderately trained men were randomised; analysed n = 10 supervised and 8 unsupervised. Training was 12 weeks of periodised heavy resistance training.
  - Direct supervision by a trainer gave a greater rate and magnitude of training-load increase, and larger maximal-strength gains.
  - Small sample.
- **[Evidence] Fisher, Steele, Wolf, Androulakis Korakakis, Smith & Giessing 2022**, *Int J Strength Cond* 2(1), [doi:10.47206/ijsc.v2i1.101](https://doi.org/10.47206/ijsc.v2i1.101), [open full text](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/632777/1/Fisher%20et%20al.%20%282022%29%20supervision%20paper%20IJSC.pdf).
  - Exploratory systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 studies, 301 supervised and 276 unsupervised participants.
  - Performance/function overall: small effect favouring supervision (0.28).
  - **Strength:** moderate effect, 0.40 (95% CI 0.06 to 0.74).
  - **Body composition:** trivial, 0.07 (CI spans zero).
  - The authors call the result tentative.
- **[Practitioner] Stronger By Science** summary of the same review ([link](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/supervision-muscle-growth/)). It suggests the likely mechanisms are adherence, effort, and technique. That is the site's interpretation, not a tested finding.

### Inferences

- The supervision evidence is about **in-person** coaching. No study found tests remote, written, or AI check-ins. Any benefit here is an inference, and it rests on the mechanisms the papers suggest: planned progression gets applied, loads keep rising, and sessions get done.
- Strength outcomes have the clearer signal. Hypertrophy (body composition) showed none. Expect the cadence to help mainly by making sure the progression rules actually get applied each week.

## Conflicts and gaps

- Saw 2016 says subjective measures beat objective ones for tracking load. The repo's recovery note ranks reps at a fixed load above mood and sleep. These do not conflict: Saw compared questionnaires to physiological markers such as hormones and heart rate, not to performance at a fixed load. Both are kept. Performance decides the flag, and questionnaires show the trend.
- No study tested how often a remote check-in should happen (weekly vs per session).
- No study tested whether a written pre-session brief changes adherence.
- Hooper index scoring (1-7 in some papers, 1-5 in others) was not checked against the 1995 original. This program uses 1-5 to match the existing session readiness score (`guide/07` §1.1).
